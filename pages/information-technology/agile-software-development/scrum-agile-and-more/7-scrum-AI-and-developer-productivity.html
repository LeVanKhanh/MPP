AI, Scrum, and the Future of Product Development

AI makes execution cheaper and shifts the constraint toward defining intent, evaluating outcomes, and bearing responsibility. That may make a Scrum Team smaller, but it does not automatically make the team obsolete.

Published: August 2, 2026

From February through June 2025, a research team at METR measured the actual working time of 16 experienced developers in repositories they had worked in for an average of five years. There were 246 issues. Each issue was randomly assigned to one of two conditions: AI tools allowed or AI tools prohibited.

Before starting, the participants predicted that AI would reduce their completion time by 24%. After finishing, they still believed it had made them about 20% faster. The measurements pointed in the opposite direction: when allowed to use AI, they took 19% longer.[1]

That 19% figure remains valid within its scope: those people, that kind of work, and the tools available in early 2025. What it cannot do is represent 2026, and METR was the first to say so explicitly. In February 2026, the organization announced a change to its experimental design. Once AI had become a default tool, people no longer wanted to join a study that required them to work without it; moreover, 30% to 50% of participants avoided submitting precisely the tasks they believed AI would help with most. The subsequent run therefore did not yield a reliable current estimate: its confidence interval crossed zero.[2]

In other words, the earlier result was not proven wrong. It expired. What remains worth preserving from that experiment is a gap, not a number.

Skilled practitioners working in their own codebases still misjudged even the direction of the effect of a tool they had used for four months. If self-assessment can be that distorted at the easiest layer to measure—the time required to close an issue—then the familiar question “Does AI make the team faster?” is a question aimed at the wrong target.

The thesis of this article is that AI does not simply make a Scrum Team faster. It shifts the constraint from execution toward defining intent, supplying context, evaluating outcomes, making decisions, and bearing responsibility. AI may therefore make a Scrum Team smaller and change how Scrum operates, but it does not automatically eliminate the need for a Scrum Team.

That thesis has a safe version and a debatable one. The safe version is “AI cannot replace people.” Nobody objects, and nobody learns anything. The debatable version lies elsewhere: the capacity for judgment on which the thesis relies does not arise by itself. It has historically been a by-product of personally making the thing being evaluated. If AI removes the making, it may gradually erode the judgment as well. The final section returns to precisely this point, because this is where the argument may fail.

This article concerns product development inside an organization: there are stakeholders outside the team, someone pays for the work, and someone bears the consequences when the product is wrong. In a personal project where the author is both the user and the risk bearer, much of this argument does not apply. That is not a minor exception; it is one of the article’s two boundaries.

Four Layers Are Being Collapsed into One

The three most common questions in discussions about AI and Scrum are “Can AI replace the Scrum Master?”, “Can AI replace the Product Owner?”, and “Can AI replace Developers?” None can be answered as stated, because each compresses four different things into the word “replace.”

Layer Its real question Where AI stands as of August 2026
Capability Can this work be done, and what can do it? Advancing rapidly, far faster than the other three layers
Accountability Who is answerable for the outcome, including when it is wrong? Ultimate accountability has not shifted. A model cannot bear consequences
Authority Who has the right to decide, and who granted that right? Operational authority can be—and is being—delegated: automatic deployment and rollback, transactions approved within limits, algorithmic pricing. The source of that authority has not shifted; it still originates in the organization
Legitimacy Why do stakeholders accept living with that decision? An automated process may be accepted for use, but it does not generate the legitimacy of the underlying goals and trade-offs. This is the least discussed layer

These four layers do not stack in an order where the upper ones are harder than the lower ones. They answer four different questions, and a system can be powerful at one layer while empty at another.

The connection to Scrum lies in one sentence from the 2020 Scrum Guide: “Scrum defines three specific accountabilities within the Scrum Team: the Developers, the Product Owner, and the Scrum Master.”[3] The canonical document does not define these three by lists of tasks. It defines them by what each holder is accountable for.

The Product Owner is accountable for maximizing the value of the product resulting from the work of the Scrum Team. The Scrum Master is accountable for establishing Scrum and for the Scrum Team’s effectiveness. Developers are always accountable for four things: creating the Sprint Backlog, instilling quality by adhering to the Definition of Done, adapting their plan each day toward the Sprint Goal, and holding one another accountable as professionals.[3]

The wording matters because this article depends on that distinction. In the Scrum Guide, commitment is the term associated with an artifact: the Product Goal with the Product Backlog, the Sprint Goal with the Sprint Backlog, and the Definition of Done with the Increment. The four items above are not four commitments. They are four things for which Developers are always accountable.[3]

Read carefully, none of those four is described as the act of generating code. They concern planning, quality, adaptation, and mutual accountability. This is why a claim such as “AI can write code, therefore AI can replace Developers” misses nearly the entire definition.

The Weakness in This Distinction Itself

The four-layer distinction carries a risk that must be stated before it is used throughout the rest of the article.

It can easily become a conceptual fortress. Each time AI acquires another ability, defenders of human roles retreat behind the wall of “that is capability, but accountability is different.” Used this way, the thesis can never be false. A thesis that can never be false also says nothing.

This article therefore sets its own falsification conditions. Its argument collapses if either of two things occurs: the cost for one person to verify reliably a product they did not make falls close to zero; or organizations and the law begin to recognize an automated system as a genuine accountable subject rather than as a tool used by an accountable person. Both conditions are observable, and neither had occurred when this article was written.

AI Moves the Constraint; It Does Not Remove It

Before AI agents, most teams were constrained by execution: analysis, design, coding, testing, debugging, and documentation. When the cost of those activities drops sharply, “How much more can the team do?” becomes easy to answer and leads nowhere. A more useful question is: what does not fall with it?

The list of things that do not fall is short and stubborn:

  • The clarity of the problem being solved.
  • The quality of the Product Goal.
  • The speed of genuine user feedback, which is constrained by users’ lives rather than by team capacity.
  • Conflicting interests among stakeholder groups. This is not a shortage of computational capacity; it is a problem with multiple correct answers under different criteria.
  • Tacit knowledge of the organization, the domain, and what nobody has written down.
  • The ability to judge whether a solution is actually good.

Fred Brooks named this divide in 1986 in his essay about two kinds of difficulty in software: difficulty inherent in the problem itself, and difficulty that belongs only to the way we express that problem so a machine can understand it. He argued that even if the second kind vanished entirely, the first would remain, because design complexity is essential rather than accidental.[4]

Fairness requires acknowledging the opposing case. Brooks wrote before he could imagine a tool that turns natural-language intent directly into a working system, and his most famous claim was a quantitative prediction over ten years, made forty years ago. Saying “Brooks already answered this” uses a quotation to avoid thinking. This article borrows only a distinction from Brooks, not a verdict.

What Industry-Scale Evidence Says

The 2025 DORA report surveyed nearly 5,000 technology professionals and was published in September 2025. Ninety percent of respondents said they used AI at work, more than 80% believed it made them more productive, and 30% reported little or no trust in AI-generated code.[5]

More interesting are two relationships pointing in opposite directions. Greater AI use correlated positively with delivery throughput and negatively with delivery stability. The report explains that much of the time saved in code generation flows into testing and validation; when the volume of change rises faster than control systems can keep up, instability follows.[5]

AI does not fix a team. It amplifies what the team already has.

This was a correlational survey, not an experiment, so it cannot establish causality. It is nevertheless enough to reframe the question. If output creation accelerates while stability declines, the constraint has shifted from making something to knowing whether the thing just made is correct.

The question shifts from “Who can build this solution?” to “Who knows what should be built, why, on what evidence, and who is accountable if it is wrong?”

Scrum was designed for complex problems where the answer is not known in advance and must be learned. When generating solutions becomes cheaper but choosing the right problem and validating assumptions do not, those activities consume a growing share of the total cost.

The Three Artifacts Carry the Load Differently

Scrum has three artifacts, each with an associated commitment. As output generation accelerates, their definitions remain unchanged, but where each bears the load shifts.

Product Backlog: The Risk of Becoming a Specification Queue

The appeal of an agent that is always ready to execute encourages people to feed it a large volume of pre-specified work. The Product Backlog can then drift into a queue of detailed specifications—a fixed project plan under a different name.

AI performs many Product Backlog-adjacent tasks well: consolidating scattered feedback, analyzing usage data, detecting contradictions between requirements, drafting Product Backlog Items, clarifying acceptance criteria, and tracing relationships between a new requirement and the existing codebase.

But what the Product Backlog must preserve is not on that list. It must continue to express the problem, the goal, untested assumptions, expected value, and even what the team knows it does not know. A backlog in which every item is fully specified up front has discarded its record of uncertainty—and uncertainty is why Scrum exists.

Sprint Backlog: From Task List to Execution Context

In the Scrum Guide, the Sprint Backlog consists of the Sprint Goal, the Product Backlog items selected for the Sprint, and a plan for delivering the Increment. When agents perform most execution, the actual shape of that “plan” changes: it resembles a manually decomposed task list less and a body of context sufficient for both humans and agents to work more.

That context usually includes the Sprint Goal, the specification of the work in progress, constraints, architectural context, acceptance criteria, the test strategy, the plan the agent is executing, and its current execution state.

What does not change is ownership. An agent may continuously revise its own implementation plan, but “adapting their plan each day toward the Sprint Goal” remains something for which Developers are accountable.[3] A self-modifying plan that nobody checks against the Sprint Goal has ceased to be a Sprint Backlog, even if it remains in the same place in the tool.

Definition of Done: From Quality List to Governing System

The Definition of Done is where the role change is clearest.

Previously, it was primarily a shared commitment to quality, checked largely through human inspection and team habit. When code-generation speed exceeds human code-reading speed, it becomes a governing mechanism.

It does not stand alone. Between speed and consequences sit release authority, architectural and platform guardrails, observability, progressive delivery, feature flags and rollback capability, and the full set of security and compliance controls. DORA itself names a cluster of capabilities that must keep pace with the volume of change: strong automated testing, mature version control, and fast feedback loops.[5] What makes the Definition of Done distinctive is that it sits at the center of this cluster and is the only element Scrum requires.

Most of its standards can and should become automated checks: unit and integration tests, security scanning, architectural constraints, coding conventions, specification-to-code traceability, documentation requirements, and deployment checks. Anything that still requires human reading must be chosen deliberately, because that is precisely the expensive part.

There is a reason older than AI to do this. In 1983, Lisanne Bainbridge described a paradox of automation: when machines assume nearly all the work, the remaining human work does not become lighter; it changes into monitoring, something humans perform poorly over long periods. Worse, because operators no longer do the work directly, they gradually lose the skills needed for the rare but decisive interventions. Her conclusion was counterintuitive: the more automated the system, the more—not less— training its human operators require.[6]

That paper concerned factories and cockpits, not software, so this is an analogy rather than evidence for the present case. The limits of the analogy also matter: code reviewers are not as passive as people watching an instrument panel; they must still read actively and ask questions. Yet DORA’s finding that saved time flows into validation shows that the monitoring burden is genuinely expanding—and that is exactly what Bainbridge warned about.[5]

The Events Change Their Reason for Existing

The four Scrum events within a Sprint do not change purpose when AI agents are introduced. What changes is the kind of information inspected and the balance of time between planning and validation.

Event Where emphasis shifts when agents perform execution
Sprint Planning Less time manually decomposing tasks; more time on the goal, assumptions to test, constraints, and how the team will know what it learned during the Sprint
Daily Scrum Still the Developers’ 15-minute event for adapting toward the Sprint Goal, but inspection now also covers agent output, integration state, and where actual results are diverging from intent
Sprint Review Carries a heavier load because the volume requiring comparison with reality grows faster than the volume of real-world feedback available
Sprint Retrospective The improvement target expands to the human-and-AI system: context supplied to agents, specification practices, quality escapes, and new review bottlenecks
Sprint Its reason for existing shifts gradually from batching work toward maintaining a fixed cadence for inspection and adaptation

The last row is the most debatable, because it is where people often conclude that the Sprint has become obsolete.

AI can compress implementation time. It cannot proportionally compress the time required to observe real users, negotiate with stakeholders, or earn an organization’s trust in a result.

If a Sprint is merely a box for batching work, then it does lose its purpose as work begins to flow continuously. But if it is a cadence for stopping to ask “What have we just learned?”, then its length is determined not by coding speed but by the speed at which reality answers. Users and the market determine that speed, and they do not care what tools the team uses.

Article 6 in this series established a related point: an event is a mechanism of empiricism, not a required meeting on a calendar. When purpose disappears, the ceremony can continue on schedule while the team learns nothing. AI agents do not reduce that risk; they merely make it run faster.

Does the Agile Manifesto Conflict with Spec-Driven Development?

Both sides now invoke “working software over comprehensive documentation.” One says AI returns us to documentation-driven development. The other says the Manifesto was never anti- documentation. Both readings are incomplete.

The Manifesto ends with a sentence explaining exactly how to read its four value pairs: “That is, while there is value in the items on the right, we value the items on the left more.”[7] The right-hand side has value. It simply does not prevail when the two sides conflict.

The context to which Agile reacted in 2001 was specific: large documents written up front, used as handoff contracts between departments, built on the assumption that the future could be predicted far enough ahead, tested only when implementation was nearly complete, and difficult to change after approval.

What Spec-Driven Development Says about Itself

It is worth reading SDD’s own materials rather than guessing. GitHub Spec Kit describes its approach in direct terms: “Spec-Driven Development (SDD) inverts this power structure. Specifications don't serve code, code serves specifications.” It continues: “The specification becomes the primary artifact. Code becomes its expression in a particular language and framework.”[8]

This is not an outsider’s inference. It is the document’s own claim, and it creates direct tension with the Manifesto’s second value pair. Anyone claiming that “SDD does not conflict with Agile at all” must first address that language.

But tension is not yet contradiction. We should slow down here because two relationships are easily collapsed into one. “Which artifact generates which?” is one relationship. “Which artifact do we trust when the two disagree?” is another. Spec Kit makes a claim about the first; the Manifesto concerns the second. A specification can generate code and still yield to evidence produced when that code runs.

If we read the whole document rather than only its declaration, SDD stands closer to Agile than it first appears. It says code generation begins once the specification and implementation plan are “stable enough, but they do not have to be ‘complete’”; that “early generations might be exploratory, testing whether the specification makes sense in practice”; and that incidents and production data “don't just trigger hotfixes, they update specifications for the next regeneration.”[8]

The last sentence is the important one. It describes exactly the direction Agile requires: reality corrects the specification; the specification does not overrule reality. If SDD preserves that direction, then “code serves specifications” describes only the order of generation and does not violate the second value pair.

A Specification Is No Longer Merely Documentation

There is another reason not to read an SDD “specification” as the “documentation” discussed by the Manifesto: the specification now performs a different set of jobs.

In an AI context, a specification simultaneously serves as a medium for human communication, context for an agent, input to implementation planning, a source for generating test cases, a standard against which results are evaluated, and an audit trail explaining why the system was built as it was.

The middle of that list was not invented by AI. Executable specifications, formal methods, model-based code generation, and acceptance tests serving as specifications all predate the Manifesto. Brooks devoted an entire section to “automatic” programming in 1986 and concluded that it worked only for problems compactly described by a small number of parameters.[4] AI changes the cost, the prevalence, and—for the first time—the ability to use natural language rather than a formal representation as input.

AI does not necessarily return us to documentation-driven development. It blurs the boundary between documentation and working software because the specification now participates directly in creating and validating the software.

Read this way, SDD is compatible with Agile when the specification covers a sufficiently small scope, remains editable, is validated early through working software, results from collaboration, and is not treated as truth.

It becomes AI-powered waterfall when the entire product is specified up front, the specification is frozen, agents only execute, every change is treated as a deviation, and stakeholders see only the final result.

Those two lists look clear, but they are not a usable test because every team believes it belongs to the first. The real test appears at one precise moment.

The question has never been how much documentation is written. The question is what the team changes when the specification and evidence from running software disagree.

Where Scrum Turns Speed into Learning

The loop that a product organization actually runs extends beyond the path from idea to software. It closes only when evidence leads to a decision.

Scrum contributes to this loop in the three segments outside the middle one. The claim must be stated carefully: Scrum creates a cadence and formal opportunities for those segments to occur; it does not guarantee that they will. The Product Goal sets the direction for learning. The Sprint Goal limits the scope of an experiment. The Increment turns an assumption into something that can be compared with reality. The Sprint Review gathers evidence. The Sprint Retrospective changes the human-and-AI system that produces all these things.

The gap between “an opportunity exists” and “it actually happens” is where most teams fall. A team may run all four events in the Sprint, within their timeboxes, while spending the entire Sprint comparing output only against its own specification. Scrum turns speed into learning only when the Product Goal and Sprint Goal contain testable assumptions, when the Increment is exposed to reality rather than merely shown to the team, and when the Sprint Review returns evidence credible enough to change a decision.

The three pillars change content without changing names. Transparency now includes visibility into what agents produced and the context on which they relied. Inspection includes refusing to trust agent output by default. Adaptation includes changing how AI is used, not only changing the product.

Four Time Intervals Worth Measuring

“How much faster does AI make coding?” measures exactly one segment—the one already being compressed. Dividing the lifecycle into four intervals makes it clearer where improvement actually occurs.

Time interval The question it answers
Intent to Spec How long does it take to turn an ambiguous problem into an assumption clear enough to act on?
Spec to Increment How long does it take to produce an Increment that meets the Definition of Done?
Increment to Evidence How long does it take to receive reliable real-world feedback?
Evidence to Decision How long does it take to adapt the Product Backlog or product direction?

Validated Learning Lead Time: the time from forming an assumption to obtaining enough evidence to retain, revise, or reject it.

This metric has not been validated elsewhere. It is a proposal made by this article, not a research- backed measure, and should be treated accordingly.

A single metric can always be gamed, so it needs guardrail metrics: rework rate, production escapes, human review load, change failure rate, divergence between specifications and actual code, technical debt, and the Developers’ ability to explain what is running in their system. The last is the hardest to measure and the first to disappear.

Why Measuring the Wrong Segment Is Worse than Not Measuring

In May 2026, METR separated “uplift” into three different concepts: improvement on tasks people would previously have performed, improvement on the set of tasks they now choose to perform, and the increase in real value after accounting for time shifting among kinds of work. Their rankings differ, and the measure over the new task set is the highest of the three.[9]

The reason is task substitution: when AI makes certain work much cheaper, people shift time toward precisely that work—including low-value tasks that previously were not worth doing—simply because those tasks have become cheap enough.[9]

Translated into Scrum, this mechanism has a familiar shape. The Product Backlog begins filling with cheap items rather than valuable ones, and it still looks highly productive on every chart. This is the hardest form of waste to see because every indicator is green.

Self-assessment fares no better. A METR survey of 349 technical workers, conducted from February through April 2026, found median self-reported increases of 1.4 to 2 times in work value and three times in speed. The researchers explicitly warned that self-reported figures do not necessarily reflect reality, cited their earlier finding that people overestimated the true effect by as much as 40 percentage points, and found no corresponding trace in public output when they inspected cases claiming improvements of tenfold or more.[10]

This is why a Sprint Review that brings in feedback from outside the team is both more expensive and more valuable than any self-assessment of productivity. The Scrum Guide requires key stakeholders, not end users, to attend.[3] But for the issue under discussion, the distance between those two groups is the distance between an opinion and evidence. This is therefore my recommendation, not a Scrum requirement.

What an Agent Chain Looks Like in Operation

Many teams are experimenting with a chain like this: one agent detects a defect through tests; another traces the symptom back to a cause; another creates a change; another updates specifications and documentation; and then people review, decide, and share the outcome with the rest of the team.

What matters in this chain is not merely how much faster the defect is fixed. Four other gaps also shrink: from symptom to cause, from cause to solution, from solution to evidence that the solution is correct, and from one person’s knowledge to the whole team’s knowledge.

The last is the only one of the four that is not a technical problem—and the one the agent chain helps least.

Can AI Replace the Three Accountabilities?

We must answer separately for each accountability and distinguish two questions: what activities can AI perform, and can AI bear the corresponding accountability?

Scrum Master

The portion of Scrum Master work AI can perform is already broad: preparing and summarizing events, tracking impediments, analyzing flow metrics, finding recurring patterns across Retrospectives, keeping timeboxes visible, suggesting facilitation techniques, checking artifact transparency, and supplying Scrum knowledge at the point of need.

The remainder is far harder, but not for technical reasons. The Scrum Master is accountable for establishing Scrum and for the Scrum Team’s effectiveness.[3] This often requires recognizing an unspoken conflict, building trust, coaching without imposing, challenging existing power structures, influencing management and the wider organization, and knowing when not to follow a process literally.

Three things on that list require something AI does not have: a relationship with particular people, sustained over time, with personal risk attached to saying what is difficult to hear.

AI may eliminate Scrum Master as a full-time title in a mature team. It has not eliminated the Scrum Master accountability.

Product Owner

At the activity layer, AI can support nearly everything: consolidating stakeholder needs, analyzing usage data, proposing Product Backlog Items, drafting and clarifying requirements, comparing options, simulating impacts, supporting ordering, and tracking which assumptions have evidence and which do not.

The limit lies in the nature of maximizing value. It is not an optimization problem with a supplied objective function. It involves choosing among stakeholder groups, accepting one risk and rejecting another, deciding when evidence is incomplete, trading short-term against long-term outcomes, and bearing the consequences of a wrong choice.

A model can propose an ordering for the Product Backlog. It lacks the legitimacy to decide which customer group is served first and which must wait another quarter. This is the legitimacy layer in the opening table, and it does not reside in technical capability. It resides in whom the organization empowers.

AI can become a powerful decision-support system for a Product Owner. It does not become the subject accountable for value.

The practical consequence lies elsewhere. If AI assumes most of the analysis and drafting around a PO, what shrinks is the support layer around the PO—not the number of POs: Business Analysts, Product Operations, and roles centered on consolidating information.

Developers

This is the hardest question because AI acts directly on what Developers produce.

The list of things AI can do is long and still growing: design, coding, testing, review, debugging, refactoring, deployment, production observation, and documentation updates.

Return, however, to the four things for which the Scrum Guide says Developers are always accountable: creating the Sprint Backlog, instilling quality by adhering to the Definition of Done, adapting the plan each day toward the Sprint Goal, and holding one another accountable as professionals.[3] The fourth has no automated equivalent. The reason is not technical: it is a relationship between people, and it has meaning only when both sides bear the consequences.

Even so, we must state something that does not apply equally to the preceding two sections: the number of Developers can clearly decline. An experienced person coordinating multiple agents can cover a scope that once required several specialties. This is not a bold prediction. It is already happening.

AI replaces an increasing share of development activities. It cannot yet bear the Developers’ accountability by itself. But the number of people needed to bear that accountability is shrinking.

That final sentence leads directly to the question toward which the whole article has been moving.

Can One Person with Multiple Agents Replace a Scrum Team?

As a matter of terminology, the answer is immediate: one person working with multiple agents is not a Scrum Team, because agents are not people with accountabilities, and naming them after the three accountabilities does not give them accountability. But that is an answer by definition, and answering by definition evades the question.

The real question is whether an AI-amplified individual can replace the value a Scrum Team once created.

The Strongest Version of the “Yes” Case

Before responding, we should construct the opposing case in its strongest form, because that form is persuasive.

A multi-person Scrum Team existed because skills were both scarce and specialized; no single person could cover the entire product. Cross-functionality is a response to that scarcity, not an intrinsic good. In return, every added person brings synchronization costs, another place for misunderstanding, and another wait state. Brooks himself wrote that adding people to a late project makes it later.

This side has an even stronger move on accountability: one person owning the whole product has clearer accountability than five people sharing ownership, not weaker accountability. Responsibility divided among many people can drift away. If one person and a set of agents can build the entire product, then the team was never the goal. The team was the price.

This argument is correct across a fairly broad range, and this article does not dispute it there.

Where One Person Is Enough—and Where One Is Not

One person with multiple agents is likely enough when the product is small, that person genuinely understands the domain, there are few stakeholders, risk is low, user feedback is easy to obtain, the architecture still fits in one person’s head, and mistakes can be reversed cheaply.

It becomes much harder when stakeholder groups have conflicting interests, the domain is complex, decisions carry legal or social consequences, tacit knowledge is distributed across people, the system needs multiple independent perspectives to detect errors, mistakes are difficult to reverse, one-person control creates key-person risk, and implementation requires commitment from the whole organization rather than merely a correct decision.

These lists are not two kinds of company. They are the endpoints of a continuum, and most products fall somewhere between them, moving gradually from one end toward the other as they grow.

Ten Agents and Ten Standpoints Are Different Things

One objection often disappears from discussions of multi-agent teams, and it does not arise from model limitations.

Technically, diversity can be purchased to a degree. Agents can run on different foundation models, receive different context sources, use different tools and data, and include a dedicated critic of the others. Ten agents configured this way can produce more than one analytical approach, and that is genuine diversity.

What cannot be purchased lies elsewhere. None of those agents occupies a separate social position in the organization. None must live with the consequences of a decision differently from the coordinator. None holds the tacit knowledge available only in another department. None has independent authority to veto the person coordinating it.

Hong and Page demonstrated in a mathematical model that a group of problem solvers with diverse approaches can outperform a group composed entirely of the highest-performing individuals. The reason is counterintuitive: when the best performers are selected from a sufficiently large pool, their approaches tend to resemble one another, and their additional ability may not compensate for the lost diversity.[11] This was a formal model, not an empirical study of real teams, and it has itself drawn mathematical criticism. It is therefore grounds for doubt, not proof.

In another direction, Kleinberg and Raghavan showed that when multiple decision makers converge on the same algorithm, collective decision quality may decline even when the algorithm is more accurate for each party considered separately, and even without shocks or correlated errors.[12] Their study concerned multiple firms screening candidates, not multiple agents on a team, so applying it directly would overreach. The mechanism is similar: similarity creates shared blind spots.

Ten agents can produce ten analytical approaches. They do not automatically create ten independent standpoints in an organization—and standpoint determines who is willing and able to disagree.

Cognitive diversity is not a soft value. It is an error-detection mechanism. Diversity of method can be purchased through configuration; diversity of interests and authority cannot, because those do not reside in the technical system.

The Limits of This Argument

Everything so far rests on an untested premise: that people retain the ability to evaluate outcomes, make decisions, and bear responsibility even when they no longer directly make what they evaluate. That premise may be wrong. If it is, much of this article is wrong with it.

Bainbridge identified the mechanism of failure forty-three years ago in another industry. Operators lose skill because they no longer practice it in daily work, precisely when that skill is needed most: rare and decisive interventions.[6]

Applied here, the story takes an uncomfortable shape. The ability to evaluate a software solution is not an independent skill. It has historically been a by-product of personally building similar things and personally bearing the consequences of getting them wrong. If AI takes away the building, judgment does not remain stationary; it erodes with it.

At that point, “people still retain accountability” remains formally true and becomes substantively empty.

Madeleine Clare Elish named this condition the moral crumple zone. A vehicle’s crumple zone is designed to protect its occupants. The moral crumple zone in an automated system does the reverse: it protects the integrity of the technical system by concentrating responsibility on the nearest human, who often has only limited understanding, capacity, and control over the system’s behavior.[13]

Read the section “Can AI Replace the Three Accountabilities?” again in that light, and its conclusion gains a darker side. Saying “one person must still own the final decision” sounds like a defense of the human role. It may also describe the deliberate design of a place to assign blame.

The boundary between these readings is not a declaration but a testable question: does the accountable person have enough information, enough time, and enough authority to say no? If an Increment is generated in two hours and its reviewer has fifteen minutes, that signature is not accountability. It is procedure.

Two Gaps This Article Cannot Fill

The article has no data on the very thing it asserts. Nobody has measured whether an AI-enabled Scrum Team learns faster or slower than an AI-enabled individual. What the article has is data on individual productivity, industry-scale throughput and stability, and two theoretical models of diversity. Joining them into a conclusion about the Scrum Team is the work of argument rather than data, and the reader is entitled to part company at precisely that connection.

The second gap is me. I work in a Scrum environment and therefore have an interest in concluding that Scrum remains necessary.

Finally, every claim here about AI capability is tied to August 2026. We need not wait long to see why that date matters: the strongest quantitative evidence used by this article became obsolete within seven months.[2]

This Cannot Yet Be Closed

The most comforting conclusion for an article like this is “AI cannot replace people.” It is safe, it is true at the time of writing, and it may expire sooner than we expect.

The conclusion that survives the article’s own objections is narrower and stronger.

AI can make a Scrum Team smaller, change how the three accountabilities are fulfilled, and make a full- time Scrum Master or a large group of Developers unnecessary in many contexts. As long as product development remains a complex problem involving uncertainty, multiple perspectives, conflicting interests, and real consequences, the need for a self-managing group of people has not disappeared. How long that need survives, however, depends on whether we can preserve the capacity for judgment—and that capacity is being eroded by the very tools that make the work more convenient.

One question remains unanswered, and it is the question worth carrying forward:

If one person working with multiple AI agents can produce software, is Scrum still a framework for managing development, or has it become a framework for ensuring that a human-and-AI system continues to learn well, decide well, and remain genuinely accountable?

  1. METR (2025). Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity. A randomized controlled trial involving 16 developers and 246 issues from February through June 2025; the source for the gap between participants’ perceived productivity increase and the measured result. Published article, arXiv version.
  2. METR (February 24, 2026). We are Changing our Developer Productivity Experiment Design. The researchers explain why the design ceased to operate reliably once AI became a default tool: participants refused to work without AI, and 30% to 50% avoided submitting precisely the tasks for which AI helped most. This explains why the earlier result no longer represents the present; it is not evidence that the result was wrong. This article uses it to avoid presenting the 19% figure as a current estimate. Read the update.
  3. Schwaber, K. & Sutherland, J. (2020). The Scrum Guide. The canonical source for Scrum’s definition of three accountabilities rather than three task lists; for the accountabilities of the Product Owner and Scrum Master; for the four things Developers are always accountable for; and for commitment as a term associated with artifacts rather than Developers. It is also the source for the Sprint Review involving key stakeholders rather than requiring end users. Read the guide.
  4. Brooks, F. P. (1987). No Silver Bullet: Essence and Accidents of Software Engineering. IEEE Computer, 20(4), 10–19. The source for dividing software difficulty into what belongs to the problem’s essence and what belongs only to its expression; this article borrows that distinction while stating the limits of citing a 1986 prediction. Its precursor was UNC technical report TR86-020. Technical report.
  5. DORA / Google Cloud (September 24, 2025). 2025 DORA Report: State of AI-Assisted Software Development. A survey of nearly 5,000 technology professionals; the source for the 90% AI adoption figure, the 30% with little or no trust in AI-generated code, and the positive relationship with throughput alongside the negative relationship with stability. These are correlational data, not an experiment. Release announcement, DORA analysis.
  6. Bainbridge, L. (1983). Ironies of Automation. Automatica, 19(6), 775–779. The original source for the paradox that greater automation makes the remaining human work harder and causes operators to lose the skills needed for decisive interventions. This article uses it in two places: the Definition of Done as a governing mechanism, and the final self-critique. Article page.
  7. Beck, K. et al. (2001). Manifesto for Agile Software Development. This article quotes its closing explanation that the items on the right still have value, distinguishing the Manifesto from the common reading that Agile opposes documentation. Read the original.
  8. GitHub. Spec Kit and Spec-Driven Development. The source for SDD’s own claims that code serves the specification and that the specification is the primary artifact; also the source for three statements that resist the “SDD is waterfall” reading: generation begins when the specification is stable enough, not necessarily complete; early generations can be exploratory; and production incidents update the specification. This article uses SDD’s own materials because they are the subject under discussion. Repository, SDD document.
  9. METR (May 8, 2026). Task Substitution and Uplift. Separates three measures of uplift and shows that uplift measured over the tasks people now choose to perform is the highest because people shift time toward work AI makes cheaper, including low-value work. This supports the argument that “How much faster does AI make coding?” measures the wrong segment. Read the article.
  10. METR (May 11, 2026). Measuring the Self-Reported Impact of Early-2026 AI on Technical Worker Productivity. A survey of 349 technical workers conducted from February through April 2026. This article uses both the self-reported figures and the researchers’ own warnings about their reliability. Read the article.
  11. Hong, L. & Page, S. E. (2004). Groups of Diverse Problem Solvers Can Outperform Groups of High-Ability Problem Solvers. PNAS, 101(46), 16385–16389. A mathematical model supporting the claim that diversity of approaches can outperform greater individual ability. This article uses it as a reason to question multi-agent teams sharing the same foundation, while stating that it is a formal model rather than an empirical team study. Original paper.
  12. Kleinberg, J. & Raghavan, M. (2021). Algorithmic Monoculture and Social Welfare. PNAS, 118(22). Shows that multiple decision makers converging on one algorithm can reduce collective decision quality even when the algorithm is more accurate for each party. This article uses that mechanism analogically and states that the original research concerns applicant screening, not AI agents. PNAS version, arXiv version.
  13. Elish, M. C. (2019). Moral Crumple Zones: Cautionary Tales in Human-Robot Interaction. Engaging Science, Technology, and Society, 5, 40–60. The source of the moral crumple zone concept: responsibility is concentrated on the person nearest an automated system, often the person with the least control. This article uses it to challenge its own conclusion about accountability. Read the article.
