Landmark Arguments: East and West Classics

A horse was proven not to be a horse, a flying arrow was proven never to move, and a doctrine was turned against itself until it had to concede its own falsehood — none of it was an accident

Updated: July 25, 2026

Why Ancient Paradoxes Still Matter

The first article in this series built a framework for spotting where an argument breaks: four groups sorted by the kind of break (relevance, presumption, induction, ambiguity), and a distinction between a fallacy committed on purpose and one committed by accident. That framework was built almost entirely from modern, everyday examples. This article turns the same tools on arguments that are over two thousand years old, and the result is not the tidy confirmation one might expect.

In the centuries surrounding Aristotle's own systematic catalogue of invalid refutations — some of these thinkers earlier, some his contemporaries, some active decades after he died — thinkers on both ends of the Eurasian landmass were already constructing arguments deliberately shaped like fallacies, arguments built to look broken in a very specific, instructive way. A logician in the state of Zhao proved that a white horse is not a horse. A philosopher in Elea proved that the fastest runner in the world can never overtake a tortoise it is chasing. A teacher of rhetoric in Leontini proved, in three successive steps, that nothing exists, that nothing could be known even if it did, and that nothing could be communicated even if it were known. Read as an error, each one simply looks wrong. Read as a device — a claim this article will argue for case by case, not assume from the start — each one turns out to be doing something closer to what a proof by contradiction does in mathematics, or what a stress test does in engineering: pushing an ordinary assumption hard enough that where it quietly gives way becomes visible.

Running the four-group framework from Article 1 against arguments this old surfaces a case the framework doesn't quite have a clean slot for. Ad hominem, straw man, and begging the question describe reasoning that fails because someone made a mistake or told a lie. But what does the framework call an argument that is deliberately built to look fallacious, on purpose, precisely so the listener trips over their own hidden assumption instead of over the speaker's dishonesty? That question turns out to be the throughline connecting a Chinese debate about fish and a Greek debate about truth itself, and it's worth holding onto as the four cases below unfold.

The East: The Chinese School of Names

During the Warring States period (475–221 BCE), a loose group of thinkers later grouped together as the Mingjia, the "School of Names" (名家), built a reputation for arguments that sounded absurd on their face and were nearly impossible to refute on their terms. Confucian and Mohist contemporaries dismissed them as players of empty word games; the arguments themselves suggest something more deliberate was going on. [1]

Gongsun Long: "A White Horse Is Not a Horse"

Context

Gongsun Long (公孫龍, c. 325–250 BCE) was a philosopher and, by several accounts, a court debater retained for his skill at argument. The "White Horse Dialogue" (白馬論), preserved in the text that bears his name, stages a debate between an unnamed questioner and a defender of the thesis "a white horse is not a horse" (白馬非馬).

The Original Argument

The defender's case rests on a distinction between what a name specifies. "Horse" (馬) names a shape; "white" (白) names a color. "White horse" therefore names both a shape and a color, which is not the same thing as naming a shape alone. The argument then adds a test of extension: someone asked to bring "a horse" may bring a horse of any color, including a brown one or a black one, but someone asked to bring "a white horse" may not bring a brown horse. Since a brown horse satisfies "horse" but not "white horse," the two terms pick out different things — and if they pick out different things, "white horse" cannot simply be identical to "horse." [1]

Reading Through the Four-Group Framework

On its surface, this looks squarely like the Ambiguity group from Article 1, specifically equivocation: the word "is" (非/是) is being made to slide between two different jobs, an identity claim ("a white horse just is a horse, the same thing") and a class-membership claim ("a white horse is a member of the category horse"). The defender's proof only works against the identity reading; against the class-membership reading, which is what an ordinary listener means, it proves nothing at all. That slide is the entire trick, and it is exactly the move Aristotle catalogued among his language-dependent refutations twenty-two centuries ago on the other side of the world.

The Debate: Real Fallacy or Philosophical Insight?

Reading it as pure equivocation is not the whole story, though. Classical Chinese has no articles, no obligatory singular/plural marking, and no copula that cleanly separates "is identical to" from "is a member of" the way modern predicate logic does. Some modern scholars of Chinese logic read the White Horse Dialogue less as a trick played on a naive listener and more as one of the earliest recorded attempts, anywhere, to notice that a language's grammar can quietly blur a distinction — intension versus extension, in later logical terms — that reasoning actually depends on keeping separate. [1] Under that reading, Gongsun Long isn't committing the fallacy so much as performing it on purpose, to make the blur visible. Whether that was his intent or a modern rescue of an old joke is itself unresolved, and it's the first sign that the fallacy/insight line in this article is going to be harder to draw than it was in Article 1.

Gongsun Long: "Hard and White"

A companion piece, the "Discourse on the Hard and the White" (堅白論), pushes the same move a step further. Look at a hard, white stone. Sight tells you it is white; touch tells you it is hard. Since these two facts arrive through two entirely separate senses, and since nothing that arrives through sight can, by definition, be the same fact as something that arrives through touch, the argument concludes that "hard" and "white" cannot both be present in the stone at once as a single unified thing — they must be treated as separable. [1]

Read through the framework, this is Ambiguity again, but of a subtler kind: it treats two properties that are physically co-located in the same object (later Mohist logicians called this relationship "mutually pervasive," 相盈) as though the fact that we learn about them differently proves they exist separately. That's a quiet slide from an epistemological claim (how we come to know something) to a metaphysical one (what is actually true of the thing) — a move that, read uncharitably, is a presumption-group error dressed as an ambiguity-group one. Read charitably, it's an early, genuinely unresolved question about how a mind assembles one object out of several independent streams of sense data, a question that shows up again, essentially unchanged, in modern philosophy of mind as the "binding problem."

Hui Shi's Ten Theses

Hui Shi (惠施, c. 370–310 BCE), a friend, rival, and frequent debate partner of the Daoist philosopher Zhuangzi, left no surviving text of his own; what remains is a list of ten paradoxical propositions, the "Ten Theses" (歷物十事), preserved secondhand in the "Tianxia" (天下, "Below Heaven") chapter of the Zhuangzi. Two are worth pulling out here. [1]

  • "The ultimately great has nothing beyond it; call it the Great One. The ultimately small has nothing within it; call it the Small One." A definition of maximum and minimum by the absence of anything further, rather than by size — a move that calls to mind, loosely and from a great distance, the same questions about infinity, maximum, minimum, and divisibility that mathematics would eventually formalize with tools that have no real counterpart in Hui Shi's own vocabulary.
  • "Same on a large scale but different on a small scale; this is called the small sameness-and-difference. The ten thousand things are all, in the end, the same; all, in the end, different; this is called the great sameness-and-difference." A claim that sameness and difference are not fixed properties of things but relative to the scale at which you choose to compare them — a relativism thesis with an obvious cousin in Protagoras, discussed below.

Unlike the White Horse Dialogue, these are not built as a trap for an opponent to fall into; they read more like compressed philosophical claims stated in paradox form, closer to a Zen koan than to a debate tactic. They belong in this article less because they are fallacies in disguise and more because they establish that "using paradox on purpose to make a point" was a recognized mode of argument in this tradition, not an isolated trick.

The Debate at the Hao River Bridge

Context

Zhuangzi and Hui Shi appear together often in the text that bears the former's name, usually as sparring partners, with Hui Shi cast as the literal-minded logician and Zhuangzi as the man who wins by refusing to argue on Hui Shi's terms. Their best-known exchange is recorded in the "Autumn Floods" (秋水) chapter, set on a bridge over the Hao River. [2]

The Original Argument

Watching fish from the bridge, Zhuangzi says: "The minnows are darting about, free and easy — this is how fish are happy." Hui Shi objects: "You are not a fish. How do you know that the fish are happy?" Zhuangzi replies: "You are not I. How do you know that I do not know the fish are happy?" Hui Shi presses the point, trying to close the loophole: "I am not you, so indeed I don't know what you know. But by the same logic, you are certainly not a fish — so the case that you don't know the fish are happy is complete." Zhuangzi's final answer reframes the whole exchange: "Let's go back to the beginning. You asked me, 'How do you know the fish are happy' — which means that in asking me the question at all, you already knew that I knew it. I knew it here, on the bridge over the Hao." [2]

Reading Through the Four-Group Framework

Hui Shi's opening move, "you are not a fish, how do you know," is a Presumption-group move, structurally close to a loaded question: it smuggles in an unproven premise — "only X itself can know X's inner state" — and builds the challenge on top of it without ever putting that premise up for inspection. If that premise is simply granted, Zhuangzi has already lost before he opens his mouth again.

Zhuangzi's final reply does not attack that premise directly. Read one way, it turns Hui Shi's own question back on itself: asking "how do you know that I know" presupposes that Zhuangzi is the kind of being capable of making assertions worth interrogating in the first place. But that concession is thinner than it looks. Hui Shi's premise was never that Zhuangzi can't make assertions at all — only that he can't know a fish's specific inner experience — and granting the first doesn't obviously grant the second. Read generously, the reply still gestures at something self-referential; read strictly, the two capacities aren't the same one, and the refutation is weaker than a clean self-reference would need it to be. It's a looser cousin of the move used against Protagoras in Greece, discussed below, not a mirror image of it.

But the text itself points to something narrower and more certain than that. Zhuangzi's actual reply pivots on the Chinese word 安 (ān), which Hui Shi used to mean "how," but which can just as validly mean "from where." Zhuangzi's comeback answers the second sense — "I knew it from up above the Hao" — while appearing to answer the first, and the translation above already smooths over the seam. [3] Read this way, Zhuangzi isn't refuting Hui Shi's premise at all; he's winning by wordplay, exploiting a real ambiguity built into the Chinese question rather than exposing a hidden assumption behind it. That's a plain equivocation, the Ambiguity group from Article 1, sitting right next to whatever self-referential move might also be available in the exchange — and it means the case for "the same maneuver as Protagoras" below is weaker, and more contested, than it first looks.

The Debate: Fallacious Reasoning or a Legitimate Philosophical Question?

Hui Shi's original challenge is not obviously fallacious reasoning committed by accident; it's a serious, still-unresolved question in philosophy of mind, usually called the "problem of other minds": strictly speaking, no one has direct access to another creature's subjective experience, only to its outward behavior. Read that way, Hui Shi isn't reasoning badly, he's raising a real epistemological limit, and Zhuangzi's comeback is clever rhetoric that sidesteps the limit rather than actually resolving it — Zhuangzi wins the exchange without ever showing he really can know what a fish feels.

Two more readings compete for the rest of the answer, and the text doesn't settle between them. One possibility is that Zhuangzi's reply shows Hui Shi's own line of questioning already assumes the kind of cross-mind knowledge it claims to rule out — the self-referential reading above, which would mean neither man is reasoning badly, only using a real philosophical limit against each other in opposite directions. The other, favored by the wordplay on ān, is that Zhuangzi never actually engages the philosophical substance of the challenge at all, and wins by quietly changing what the question means partway through. What both readings agree on is that this isn't fallacious reasoning committed by accident on either side: Hui Shi is using a real philosophical limit as a debate weapon, and Zhuangzi is getting out from under it either by a genuine logical counter-move or by a well-timed pun, depending on which reading of the text one trusts. Sinologist Victor H. Mair has compared the exchange's construction to a Socratic dialogue for exactly this reason: reason is "subtly employed... in order to make an anti-rationalist point." [2] It sits closer to the paradoxes of Zeno, discussed next, than to any entry on the checklist from Article 1 — though which of its two competing readings is doing the real work is a question revisited directly in the comparison section below.

The West: Ancient Greece

Across the same centuries, Greek philosophy produced its own tradition of arguments built to look broken on purpose: the Sophists, itinerant teachers of rhetoric who were paid to argue any side of any question, and Zeno of Elea, who used impossible-looking conclusions as a weapon in defense of a single metaphysical thesis.

Protagoras and the Self-Refuting Measure

Context

Protagoras of Abdera (c. 490–420 BCE) was the most celebrated of the Sophists, a paid teacher of rhetoric and argument who opened his treatise Truth with a single sentence that became one of the most quoted lines in Greek philosophy. [4]

The Original Argument

"Of all things the measure is man: of those that are, that they are; and of those that are not, that they are not." [4] Read plainly, this says that truth is relative to each individual perceiver: if something seems a certain way to you, it is that way, for you, full stop. There is no view from nowhere to appeal to above individual judgment.

Reading Through the Four-Group Framework

As a starting premise, "truth is relative to the perceiver" is a Presumption-group move: an enormous, contestable claim asserted as a foundation rather than argued for. But the interesting failure isn't in the premise itself, it's in what happens when the premise is turned on itself. Plato, in the Theaetetus, constructs the classic rebuttal, later called the peritrope ("turning the tables"): if all individual judgments are true simply by being held, then the judgment "Protagoras's doctrine is false," held by anyone who disagrees with him, is also true — which means Protagoras's own doctrine, by its own logic, has to admit that it is false. [4]

The Debate: Real Fallacy or Philosophical Insight?

The peritrope is a genuinely devastating piece of logic, and it has stayed devastating for over two thousand years: any doctrine of total relativism has to explain why it alone is exempt from itself. But it may prove too much. Plato's own text acknowledges a defense: Protagoras could reply that his critic's judgment is only true for the critic, not in any absolute sense that would actually bind Protagoras — at which point the "refutation" only works if you've already smuggled in the very non-relative notion of truth the doctrine was built to reject. [4] Whether the peritrope is a fair kill shot or itself trades on an equivocation between "true" and "true-for-me" is still argued over in academic philosophy today, which is a strong sign this was never simply a fallacy to be diagnosed and dismissed.

Gorgias: Nothing Exists

Gorgias of Leontini (c. 483–375 BCE), a younger contemporary of Protagoras, is credited with a short treatise, On Nature, or On the Non-Existent, that mounts three cascading arguments in sequence: first, that nothing exists; second, that even if something did exist, it could not be known; third, that even if it could be known, that knowledge could not be communicated to another person. [5]

The full chain of sub-arguments survives only secondhand, through Sextus Empiricus and the pseudo-Aristotelian treatise On Melissus, Xenophanes, and Gorgias, and reconstructing all three in detail is beyond the scope of this article. But the throughline scholars point to bears a family resemblance to Gongsun Long's move above: the first argument, for instance, turns on treating "what is not" as though it must still be something in order to be talked about or denied at all, sliding between the grammatical form of a sentence and what that sentence actually commits a speaker to. Read that way, the treatise leans on the Ambiguity group, in a style that closely parodies the rigorous, step-by-step deductions the philosopher Parmenides and his student Zeno (below) had used to argue that all change and plurality are illusions. That resemblance is often read as a deliberate parody of the Eleatic tradition. An influential strand of contemporary scholarship reads Gorgias's treatise not as a sincere metaphysical claim that nothing is real, but as a rhetorical performance, built to demonstrate that Eleatic-style airtight deduction could be aimed at literally any conclusion, including an absurd one, and still look unanswerable — though the text itself doesn't settle the question, and some readers take it at face value. [5] If the performance reading is right, Gorgias is using a fallacy-shaped argument on purpose, as an advertisement for his own skill and a warning about the limits of pure deduction, which would place him closer to Zeno's own method than to an honest mistake.

Zeno's Paradoxes: Achilles, the Tortoise, and the Arrow

Context

Zeno of Elea (c. 490–430 BCE) was a student of Parmenides, who held that reality is a single, unchanging, undivided whole and that motion and plurality are illusions of the senses. According to Plato's Parmenides, Zeno wrote his famous paradoxes specifically to defend his teacher: not to prove motion is impossible for its own sake, but to show that the opposing view, that things are genuinely many and genuinely move, leads to conclusions at least as absurd as Parmenides' own. [6]

The Original Argument

Aristotle's Physics preserves the best-known versions. [7] In the Dichotomy, to walk any distance you must first cross half of it; before that, a quarter; before that, an eighth — an infinite sequence of steps to complete before taking a single one, which should make starting to move impossible. In Achilles and the Tortoise, the faster runner gives the slower one a head start; by the time Achilles reaches the tortoise's starting point, the tortoise has advanced a little further; by the time he closes that gap, it has advanced again — a gap that shrinks forever without, it seems, ever closing. In the Arrow, a flying arrow occupies, at any single instant, exactly the space equal to itself, which is what it means to be at rest; since a span of time is made of such instants, the arrow is at rest at every instant of its flight, and so, somehow, never moves at all.

Reading Through the Four-Group Framework

The Dichotomy and Achilles are Presumption-group moves at heart: each quietly assumes that summing infinitely many shrinking pieces, of distance or of time, must itself produce an infinite result. That assumption doesn't survive modern mathematics — an infinite series of shrinking distances can converge to a finite sum, exactly what a first course in calculus demonstrates using this very paradox as the worked example. The Arrow rests on a different and subtler move: it equates two things that aren't the same — an object occupying one determinate position at a durationless instant, and that object being at rest, zero velocity, at that instant. Having a fixed position at an instant says nothing on its own about velocity. Modern calculus defines instantaneous velocity as a derivative, the limit of average velocity over intervals shrinking down around that instant, which makes it a coherent, well-defined quantity, not zero by default. That resolves the paradox without needing to deny that a duration is composed of durationless instants at all; the false step was never in that composition, it was in silently reading "has a position" as "isn't moving." [8]

The Debate: Fallacy or Unresolved Philosophy?

Calling Zeno's paradoxes "solved" by calculus is the standard modern verdict, and it's correct as far as it goes: the mathematics of convergent series answers the specific question of whether Achilles catches the tortoise. But that answer is narrower than it sounds. Zeno's deeper target was never really the arithmetic of the chase, it was whether space and time are infinitely divisible in reality, not just on paper, and whether a continuum can coherently be built out of an actual infinity of point-like parts. That question still gets argued over in the philosophy of physics today. As with Gongsun Long, the "fallacy" here isn't really a fallacy at all in the sense Article 1 used the word, an accidental break in reasoning. It's a device, built on purpose, to force an assumption everyone treats as too obvious to state — that motion is real, that infinite sums behave like finite ones — out into the open where it can finally be examined.

East Meets West

What They Share

Every case above comes from the same narrow window of history, roughly the 5th through 3rd centuries BCE, on opposite ends of the Eurasian landmass, with no known evidence of direct contact between them. And in both places, the mainstream philosophical establishment of the time reacted to these arguments the same way: with open hostility. The Confucian and Mohist schools dismissed the School of Names as players of "twisted arguments" (詭辯) with no practical use. [1] Plato and Aristotle treated the Sophists as hired guns willing to make the weaker argument look stronger for a fee, a charge sharp enough that "sophistry" still means exactly that in English today, and it's the reason Article 1 borrowed the word to distinguish deliberate deception from an honest mistake. Both traditions, in other words, produced serious critics who read these arguments as bad-faith tricks rather than as serious philosophy — and in both traditions, later readers have gone back and found more going on underneath than the original critics gave credit for.

Where They Differ

The clearest difference is motive, though it's less clean-cut than it first appears. The Sophists were, by trade, professional rhetoricians: Protagoras and Gorgias taught argument as a paid skill, and a treatise like Gorgias's On Nature reads convincingly as a demonstration piece, proof that its author could make an airtight-looking case for literally anything. The Chinese side looks, on the available evidence, less commercial and more sincere: Gongsun Long and Hui Shi were court advisors and philosophers first, using paradox to make an actual point about language or reality, not to sell a technique. That distinction should not be overstated, since both Gongsun Long and Hui Shi were also known and valued for their skill at debate, and scholars still disagree about how much showmanship was mixed into their philosophy. But as a rough difference in center of gravity, it holds: the Greek paradoxes lean toward rhetorical performance, the Chinese paradoxes lean toward philosophical argument, and Zeno sits somewhere in between, using performance-grade argumentative technique in the service of a sincerely held metaphysical thesis.

The Self-Referential Counter-Move

One connection is precise enough to deserve its own subsection rather than a passing mention — with a caveat attached. Read one way, Zhuangzi's reply at the Hao River bridge and Plato's peritrope against Protagoras are structurally close: both take an opponent's challenge and, instead of contesting its content, show that making the challenge already depends on the thing it denies. The caveat, raised in the case above, is that Zhuangzi's reply may instead be doing something narrower — winning on the ambiguity of the word ān rather than on a genuine self-referential structure. What's safe to say either way: even on the more modest wordplay reading, Zhuangzi isn't stating a false premise or reasoning carelessly, he's using a real feature of the exchange's own language to get out of it. On the more ambitious reading, self-reference, the parallel to Protagoras gets clearer, though the two arguments still aren't a perfect structural match — Hui Shi's challenge and Protagoras's doctrine aren't denying quite the same capacity. The table below sets out that stronger reading, which the Greek case establishes on its own regardless of how the Chinese case is read.

The challengeThe self-referential turn
Zhuangzi vs. Hui Shi (on the self-referential reading)"You are not a fish; you cannot know what a fish knows"Asking "how do you know" already treats Zhuangzi as someone whose claims to know are worth questioning — conceding the very capacity the challenge denies
Plato vs. Protagoras"All individual judgments are true"The judgment that this doctrine is false is, by its own rule, also true — so the doctrine has to concede its own falsehood

On the self-referential reading, neither move resolves the underlying philosophical question — other minds, in one case; relativism, in the other — so much as it demonstrates that the challenger's own position cannot be stated without quietly relying on the thing under attack. That specific shape of argument, using an opponent's own premise to collapse their conclusion, doesn't have a clean home among the twelve fallacies named in Article 1, because it isn't a mistake being pointed out. And whether or not Zhuangzi himself deploys it, the fact that Greek philosophy independently produced a clear version of the same move against Protagoras — debated in its force, as the case above shows, but not in its shape — is enough on its own to establish that the maneuver is real, not an artifact of how one clever writer happened to phrase things.

A Different Function: The Argumentative Paradox

Article 1 drew a line between two things that can go wrong in an argument: a fallacy, committed on purpose to win or deceive, and fallacious reasoning, the same broken structure produced by an honest mistake. That line sorts arguments by two things bundled together — whether the structure is broken, and whether the speaker knows it. Most of this article's cases don't fit either box, but not for the same reason in each case, and lumping them together into one new box would repeat the very mistake this series exists to catch: treating several different maneuvers as one just because they share a family resemblance.

Zeno's paradoxes are not a case of Zeno believing his own reasoning is broken. A reductio ad absurdum works by holding your own logic to be entirely sound and using it to force an absurd conclusion out of an opponent's premises — Zeno never doubts the argument itself, he's using it as a weapon against an assumption he thinks is false. Gorgias's status is genuinely unsettled, as the case above lays out: the treatise might be sincere, might be parody, and the surviving evidence doesn't decide it either way. Zhuangzi's reply might be a real self-referential move or a pun on ān, and the text itself doesn't settle which. What actually unites these cases isn't a shared awareness of their own flaws — it's a shared use: each one takes an argument that looks broken and points it at the listener's assumptions instead of at the truth of its own conclusion.

QuestionWhat it asks
Is the structure broken?The same four groups from Article 1 — relevance, presumption, induction, ambiguity
Does the speaker believe it?Sincerely held, performed on purpose, or genuinely unclear from the surviving text
What is it aimed at?The listener's conclusion (to win, or by mistake), or the listener's assumptions (to test them)

This isn't a rival slot in the same two-box system so much as a different function an argument can serve. A fallacy and fallacious reasoning are typically read for what they assert — whether the conclusion holds up, and whether the speaker knew it wouldn't. An argumentative paradox does something else: instead of asserting a conclusion, it stress-tests a set of premises by following them out to the uncomfortable or absurd consequences they produce. Whether an individual case above also happens to be a fallacy in Article 1's sense is, for several of them, close to the wrong question to ask — the point of the maneuver, not the honesty of the speaker, is what marks it out.

This reframing is not free of risk. Stress-testing an opponent's premises is exactly what a manipulator does too, and "I was just testing your premises" is available to a bad-faith arguer as easily as to Zeno. What distinguishes an argumentative paradox from ordinary sophistry isn't a claim about what the speaker privately intended, which is usually unrecoverable for texts this old in any case. It's that the paradox keeps its force even after every premise is laid bare and checked, the same bóc-lập-luận exercise Article 1 walked through on the Bill Gates story. Zeno's arrow is exactly as puzzling after you've written out its premises as before, and that persistence, not any settled fact about Zeno's state of mind, is the actual mark of something worth taking seriously rather than dismissing as a trick.

Summary

Key Takeaway: Running the four-group fallacy framework from Article 1 against arguments over two thousand years old mostly works, but it also exposes the framework's edge. Gongsun Long's white horse and Gongsun Long's stone trade, in part, on real ambiguity; Hui Shi's theses and Zeno's paradoxes trade, in part, on real unresolved questions about infinity and continuity; and the Hao River debate turns out to hinge as much on a pun in classical Chinese as on any clean logical structure, which is itself the article's best evidence that these texts resist a tidy verdict. What the case for a self-referential counter-move does establish, through Plato against Protagoras, is that some of the deepest moves in argument work by stress-testing a listener's premises rather than by asserting a conclusion, and Zeno's paradoxes and Gorgias's trilemma show that same function being served by an entirely different toolkit. None of this is best filed under "fallacy" in the sense Article 1 used the word, an accidental break in reasoning. It's closer to a different function an argument can serve: built to look broken, aimed at stress-testing what a listener takes for granted rather than at winning or deceiving them, whether or not the speaker's own intent can still be recovered from a text this old. Calling something a fallacy, it turns out, is not always the end of the analysis — sometimes the argument was never trying to do what a fallacy does in the first place.
