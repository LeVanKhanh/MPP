Can Any Line of Reasoning Always Be Correct?

Logic can ensure that a conclusion follows from its premises—but what ensures that the premises are true?

Updated: July 25, 2026

After Every Fallacy Has Been Removed

After learning to identify fallacies, uncover premises, and examine evidence, we may develop a tempting expectation: once every error in reasoning has been removed, whatever remains must be the truth. If an argument contains no obvious fallacy, has a rigorous structure, and is clearly presented, where could it still go wrong?

The answer lies at its point of departure.

Every argument, when reconstructed as an inference, rests on premises, definitions, facts, or assumptions—whether explicitly stated or merely implicit. Logic can determine whether a conclusion genuinely follows from those starting points, but it cannot by itself guarantee that they accurately describe reality. Even a flawless reasoning machine can produce a false conclusion when its input is false, its concepts are poorly defined, or an important condition has been omitted.

Can there, then, be a line of reasoning that is always correct?

The question cannot be answered with a simple “yes” or “no,” because correctness takes several forms. An inference may be necessarily true within an axiomatic system, approximately true within a physical domain, supported by the best currently available scientific evidence, or reasonable within a particular system of values. Confusing these levels is the source of many disputes that appear impossible to resolve.

What Does Logic Guarantee?

Consider a simple argument:

  1. All fish can fly.
  2. A carp is a fish.
  3. Therefore, a carp can fly.

The conclusion is obviously false in reality. Yet the inferential structure is not defective: if the first two premises were true, the third statement would have to be true.

ConceptWhat it tests
ValidityWhether the conclusion necessarily follows from the premises
SoundnessWhether the argument is valid and its premises are true

The argument about the carp is valid but unsound because the premise “all fish can fly” is false. Logic has performed its task correctly: it has carried the content of the premises through to the conclusion without breaking the inferential structure. The problem lies in the raw material, not in the reasoning machinery. [1]

Logic can be imagined as a bridge. It can tell us whether the route from the premises to the conclusion is continuous, but it cannot guarantee that the ground at either end is stable.

Logic can establish that a conclusion is true if the premises are true; logic alone cannot establish that every premise accurately describes reality.

To examine a premise, we need observations, theories, or further arguments. Those arguments, however, have premises of their own. Where, then, does the chain of justification end?

Where Do Our Ultimate Premises Come From?

Suppose someone advances proposition P1. We ask why it should be believed, and P2 is offered in support. We can then ask why P2 is true and continue to demand P3, P4, and so on:

P1 ← P2 ← P3 ← P4 ← …

This chain presents three possibilities:

  • Infinite regress: every premise requires a prior premise. Yet human beings cannot complete an infinite chain of proofs before knowing anything.
  • Circular justification: P1 depends on P2, but P2 ultimately depends on P1. The system uses its own components to confirm one another.
  • A foundational stopping point: some starting points are accepted without being further proved within that same system. They may be axioms, definitions, direct experiences, observational data, or background assumptions.

This does not mean that foundational commitments are chosen arbitrarily. They can still be compared in terms of consistency, explanatory power, predictive success, and agreement with observation. Yet whether we adopt foundationalism, coherentism, or infinitism, a system cannot guarantee all its premises simply by repeating the very claims that still require justification. The regress of justification and the different responses to it form a central problem in epistemology. [2]

Different fields handle their starting points in different ways.

Mathematics: Certainty Within an Axiomatic System

Mathematics is often regarded as the domain closest to absolute truth. Once a theorem has been proved, it seems that it will remain true today, tomorrow, and thousands of years from now. That is largely correct—but it is a conditional form of certainty.

Euclidean geometry begins with a set of axioms, including the parallel postulate. One familiar formulation states that through a point outside a given line, exactly one line can be drawn parallel to the given line.

From the Euclidean axioms, we can prove that the interior angles of a triangle add up to 180 degrees. This is not a provisional result awaiting a future experiment to disprove it. If the conclusion is genuinely derived from the specified axioms by valid rules, it is necessarily true relative to that system. The value of the system as a whole also depends on its consistency and intended use.

Mathematicians nevertheless discovered that the parallel postulate could be replaced while still producing other consistent geometries:

  • In hyperbolic geometry, more than one line through an external point may fail to intersect the given line.
  • In elliptic geometry, parallel lines in the Euclidean sense do not exist.
  • The angles of a triangle in these spaces do not necessarily add up to 180 degrees.

This does not make Euclidean geometry false. It shows that Euclidean theorems are true within Euclidean geometry, not within every possible geometry. The development of Lobachevskian and Riemannian geometries demonstrated that alternative treatments of the parallel postulate yield systems in which both parallel lines and triangle-angle sums behave differently. [3]

Necessarily true within a systemDoes not mean true within every system
Mathematically consistentDoes not automatically mean physically accurate

Different mathematical systems may each be consistent. Determining which one better describes physical space is no longer a purely mathematical question; it requires physical observation and experiment.

Given the axioms and definitions, a conclusion proved through valid rules of inference must be true within that system.

Physics: True Within Which Domain?

Physics also begins with principles and assumptions, but they do not have the same status as mathematical axioms. Physical laws are judged not only by internal consistency but also by their confrontation with reality through observation and experiment.

For centuries, Newtonian mechanics described the motion of objects with remarkable success. It predicted trajectories, explained planetary motion, and became a foundation of modern engineering. Einstein’s relativity later showed that Newtonian mechanics loses accuracy when velocities approach the speed of light or gravitational fields become very strong.

Was Newton once right and then suddenly wrong?

That description is too simple. Newtonian mechanics remains highly accurate when velocities are much lower than the speed of light, gravitational fields are not too strong, and the scale is not quantum mechanical. We still use it to calculate the motion of vehicles, machines, structures, and many engineering systems. Einstein did not erase Newton; the newer theory located the older one within a more clearly defined domain. Under weak gravity, general relativity and Newtonian theory make nearly identical predictions, while relativistic corrections can be added systematically. [4]

In many cases, the history of science does not follow the pattern True → False. It is closer to Approximate model → More accurate model with a wider domain.

A scientific model may:

  • be highly accurate within one domain but fail outside it;
  • explain existing data well but not yet account for a newly observed phenomenon;
  • make useful predictions even though its assumptions simplify reality; or
  • become an approximate special case of a broader theory.

A good scientific question is therefore not merely “Is this theory true or false?” It also asks: How accurate is it? Under what conditions? Within what margin of error? Where does it fail?

Is the Earth Really Round?

“The Earth is round” is often treated as an obvious truth. More precisely, however, Earth is not a perfect sphere. It is slightly flattened at the poles, wider at the equator, and irregular across its surface. Geodesy also uses the geoid as a more accurate model of Earth’s shape. [5]

Does that make the statement “the Earth is round” false?

When it distinguishes Earth from a flat plane, it is sufficiently true. When used for precise geophysical or positioning calculations, it is insufficient.

The descriptions “Earth is round,” “Earth is nearly spherical,” “Earth is an oblate spheroid,” and “Earth’s shape approximates a geoid” do not necessarily contradict one another. They operate at different levels of precision and serve different purposes.

StatusMeaning
FalseDoes not correspond to reality
Approximately trueIgnores minor differences irrelevant to the present purpose
IncompleteDescribes part of the matter without covering all of it
True within a domainApplies only under specified conditions

A statement may be accurate enough for a classroom but not precise enough for satellite navigation. This is not a contradiction; it is a difference in the resolution of the model.

Rather than saying “what is true today may become false tomorrow,” a more careful formulation is:

What is regarded as true today may later have its domain narrowed, its degree of accuracy revised, or its place redefined within a broader model.

Philosophy: Starting Points in Our Understanding of Reality

In mathematics, axioms are usually explicit. In philosophy, foundational commitments may lie deeper, embedded in how we understand reality, consciousness, and knowledge.

Materialism and idealism are often treated as opposing starting points:

  • From a materialist perspective, matter is fundamental and consciousness is explained through material processes.
  • From an idealist perspective, consciousness, mind, or experience has a fundamental status that cannot be fully reduced to matter.

These positions should not be called “axioms” in the strict mathematical sense. They are ontological positions—different answers to the question of what exists at the most fundamental level of reality. Materialism is also commonly discussed under the broader modern label of physicalism, while idealism encompasses several distinct doctrines rather than a single unified view. [6]

Systems that begin from different conceptions of reality may interpret the same phenomenon differently. Yet having different foundational commitments does not place the choice between them entirely beyond reasons and evidence. Nor does it mean that all philosophical positions are equally correct or merely matters of preference.

A philosophical system can still be assessed by:

  • its internal consistency;
  • its ability to explain experience;
  • its compatibility with scientific knowledge;
  • its ability to address counterexamples;
  • the number of auxiliary assumptions it requires; and
  • whether it contradicts itself when applying its own principles.

The fact that philosophy is harder to test than physics does not make rational disagreement impossible. It means only that its standards of assessment are not identical to those of a laboratory experiment.

Human Nature: Benevolent or Self-Interested?

The classical claims that human beings are naturally good or naturally bad are often presented as opposing truths about human nature.

If we assume that people are naturally good, education may be understood as the preservation and cultivation of an existing moral capacity. If we assume that people are naturally self-interested or require correction, we may place greater emphasis on discipline, law, and mechanisms of control.

But are “good” and “bad” defined clearly enough? Children may show empathy while also competing for attention and resources. People may cooperate within their own group while competing against another. Behaviour also changes with the environment, education, incentives, and social institutions.

The binary question “Are human beings naturally good or naturally bad?” may oversimplify a nature in which several tendencies coexist.

More importantly, a premise about human beings can influence how society treats them, while that treatment may in turn produce behaviour that appears to confirm the original premise.

An organisation that assumes everyone is dishonest may build an extreme surveillance system. That environment of distrust can weaken autonomy and cooperation, leading to more defensive or evasive behaviour. The organisation then points to that behaviour and concludes, “See? People cannot be trusted.”

This is the possibility of a self-fulfilling prophecy: a premise does not merely describe reality but helps produce a reality resembling its prediction. Robert K. Merton systematised the concept in 1948 to explain how an initially false definition of a situation can trigger behaviour that makes the prediction come true. [7]

When considering premises about human nature, we should therefore ask at least three questions:

  1. Have the central concepts been defined clearly enough?
  2. Is the premise describing people, or imposing a particular view upon them?
  3. Does the system built on that premise manufacture the very evidence later used to confirm it?

Every Study Is Finite

If mathematics depends on axiomatic systems, physics on domains of application, and philosophy on foundational commitments, empirical research has limitations of its own.

Every study is finite in at least some respect:

  • it includes a finite number of participants;
  • it observes them for a finite period;
  • it measures a finite number of variables;
  • its instruments have margins of error;
  • its sample represents only certain populations;
  • its statistical methods rely on assumptions; and
  • no experiment can control the whole of reality.

These limitations do not directly make a result false. They limit the breadth and degree of certainty of the conclusions we are entitled to draw from it.

A study involving a few hundred participants cannot directly observe every person who has ever lived or will ever live. A two-year trial does not automatically reveal what will happen after twenty years. A result obtained from healthy adults does not necessarily apply to children, older adults, or people with underlying health conditions. In research methodology, extending findings from a sample to other populations concerns external validity, including generalisability and applicability. [8]

But “every study is finite” does not imply that “no study is trustworthy.” That would be an unjustified leap from the absence of absolute certainty to the impossibility of knowledge.

A small study may generate a hypothesis. A large controlled study may provide stronger evidence. Independent replications, a plausible mechanism, and systematic reviews or meta-analyses can raise confidence substantially.

Finite does not mean worthless. Non-absolute does not mean arbitrary.

We may lack absolute certainty and still possess enough evidence to act reasonably. In life, refusing to act until absolute certainty is available is itself a decision—and sometimes a riskier one than acting on the best available evidence.

If Every Conclusion Is Conditional, Are All Opinions Equal?

This is the most important objection.

If mathematics depends on axioms, physics on models, empirical research is always finite, and philosophy begins from different foundational commitments, does objective truth disappear? Can everyone simply choose whichever premises they prefer and conclude whatever they want?

No.

Two claims can both fall short of absolute certainty without being equally credible. A theory that has survived thousands of independent tests is not equivalent to a guess that has never been examined. A controlled study is not equivalent to one person’s anecdote. A system that states its premises and limitations openly is not equivalent to one that changes its definitions whenever it encounters a counterexample.

Arguments can still be compared using concrete standards:

  • Are the premises explicit or concealed?
  • Are the concepts used consistently throughout the argument?
  • Does the conclusion genuinely follow from the premises?
  • Is the evidence directly relevant to the question?
  • Is the sample sufficiently representative?
  • How well have confounding factors been controlled?
  • Have the results been independently replicated?
  • Does the theory make testable predictions?
  • Does the scope of the conclusion match the scope of the evidence?
  • Is the person making the argument willing to revise it in response to better evidence?

The absence of an absolute viewpoint does not mean that every viewpoint sees equally well.

Knowledge can be fallible while still being more objective, more accurate, and more reliable than an untested belief. This is the spirit of fallibilism: justification need not guarantee that a belief could never be wrong, yet we can still distinguish strong evidence from weak evidence. [9]

AttitudePosition
Reasonable scepticismI may be wrong; therefore, I should examine my belief.
Radical relativismAnyone may be wrong; therefore, there is no basis for judging one view as better than another.

These attitudes are fundamentally different. The first opens a path toward knowledge; the second eliminates the very possibility of learning from evidence.

So Can Any Line of Reasoning Always Be Correct?

The answer depends on what kind of “correctness” is being asked about.

Some inferences are necessarily correct in structure: if their premises are true and their rules of inference are valid, their conclusions must be true. Mathematical theorems can possess this kind of certainty within a specified axiomatic system.

When we reason about the real world, however, premises usually come from finite observations, approximate models, and measuring instruments with margins of error. A conclusion is then trustworthy not because it has escaped all premises, but because:

  • its premises are explicit;
  • its evidence is sufficiently strong;
  • its methods can be examined;
  • its results have been replicated;
  • its domain of application is defined; and
  • the theory remains open to revision when better evidence appears.

A good argument, therefore, need not claim that it can never be wrong. On the contrary, the ability to state what evidence would require it to change is often a sign of intellectual maturity.

The endpoint of critical thinking is neither universal doubt nor the discovery of a system permanently immune to criticism. Its value lies in making premises more transparent, conclusions more proportionate to the evidence, and errors easier to detect.

We may never be able to stand outside every system and view reality from an absolute position free from all premises. Yet we can still move closer to the truth by comparing models, testing predictions, seeking counterexamples, and revising what we once believed.

The final answer, then, is not that “nothing is really true,” but this:

Some things are necessarily true within a system; some are approximately true within a domain; and some conclusions are the most trustworthy given the available evidence. Wisdom lies not in confusing these categories, but in knowing which one we are dealing with.

Summary

Key point: Some inferences are always correct in structure when their premises are granted. But when reasoning concerns the real world, the premises usually rest on finite observations, approximate models, and assumptions with limited domains. This does not make every opinion equal: arguments still differ in consistency, evidential quality, predictive power, and resilience to counterexamples.

A good argument does not promise that we can never be wrong. It states where it begins, under what conditions it holds, what evidence supports it—and remains willing to change when those foundations no longer stand.
