Aristotle's 13 Fallacies and 24 Syllogisms in Traditional Logic

These are two important parts of the logical legacy that traces back to Aristotle. Aristotle systematized 13 fallacies, ways a refutation can look valid while actually failing; alongside that, and far less often mentioned, the logical tradition after him also systematized 24 valid syllogisms. The two lists show a shared interest in both valid inference and inference that only looks valid, but they don't form two symmetric halves: the valid syllogisms cover only a fixed class of categorical inference (two premises, three terms, one conclusion), while the fallacies in Sophistical Refutations range much more broadly across language, argumentative structure, and the conditions for a genuine refutation. Most popular fallacy literature, including Post 1 in this series, keeps only the first half and barely mentions the second.

13 Fallacies in Sophistical Refutations

Aristotle divided the 13 types into two groups: six rooted in the ambiguity of language, and seven unrelated to language, resting instead on the structure of the inference or its relation to context. [1]

Language-Dependent

Original nameShort description
EquivocationA word carrying two different meanings is used at two points in the same argument as if it had only one.
Amphiboly (syntactic ambiguity)The sentence structure allows two different readings, not because of a word but because of how the sentence is arranged.
Combination of WordsReading words together in a way that gives the sentence a different meaning than reading them apart.
Division of WordsThe reverse of the above: splitting apart words that need to be read together, distorting the meaning.
AccentChanging a sentence's meaning by stressing a different part of it, something only clear when spoken and easy to misread once written down.
Form of ExpressionThe surface form of a phrase misleads the listener about what category it actually belongs to.

Language-Independent

Original nameShort description
AccidentApplying a general rule to exactly the exceptional case the rule was never meant to cover.
Secundum QuidTaking something true only under a specific condition and stating it as if it were true absolutely, without limit.
Ignoratio ElenchiProving something genuinely true, but not the thing that actually needed to be proven or refuted.
Affirming the ConsequentFrom "if A then B" and "B is true," wrongly concluding "A is true," ignoring the possibility that B has some other cause.
Begging the QuestionSmuggling the very thing that needs proving into the premise, under different wording.
False CauseTreating something that isn't the real cause as the cause, usually because it happened before or at the same time.
Many QuestionsBundling several different questions into one, forcing a single answer to cover all of them.

Some of these are still used almost directly in modern literature, like equivocation, begging the question, and false cause. The table above shows that the origin of these names goes back more than 2,300 years, largely unchanged. Other fallacies common in Post 1, like straw man, ad hominem, or false dilemma, belong to classification systems developed or standardized later, though they can sometimes be connected to broader issues Aristotle himself explored.

24 Valid Syllogisms

Alongside analyzing the flawed refutations, the syllogistic tradition rooted in Aristotle was carried forward by Theophrastus and later ancient and medieval logicians, eventually forming the table of four figures and 24 moods presented below. [4] A valid syllogism consists of two premises and a conclusion, each statement belonging to one of four types: A (all S is P), E (no S is P), I (some S is P), and O (some S is not P). With three terms, namely subject, predicate, and middle term, arranged in four different ways called "figures," there are theoretically 256 possible combinations. [2]

By the traditional listing used here, there are 19 basic moods and 5 weakened moods (marked *), 24 moods total considered valid in traditional logic. But existential import, the assumption that the class being discussed isn't empty, doesn't affect only the 5 weakened moods. Under the modern Boolean reading, only 15 of the 19 basic moods remain unconditionally valid; the remaining 4 basic moods, Darapti and Felapton in Figure 3, Bramantip and Fesapo in Figure 4, along with all 5 weakened moods, are valid only if that existential assumption is granted. [3] The asterisk in the table below marks only the 5 weakened moods, those that draw a partial conclusion from premises strong enough for a universal one; it doesn't mark every mood that depends on existential import. The number 24 is therefore not absolutely fixed: medieval logicians variously counted 15, 19, or 24 forms depending on whether they accepted the fourth figure, accepted the weakened moods, and accepted existential import.

Before looking at the table of names, it's worth picturing exactly where the four "figures" differ. Every syllogism has three terms: the subject of the conclusion (S), the predicate of the conclusion (P), and the middle term (M), the term that appears in both premises but disappears from the conclusion, acting as the bridge between S and P. The four figures differ only in where M sits within the two premises:

FigureMajor premiseMinor premiseConclusionPosition of middle term M
Figure 1M is PS is MS is PSubject in the major premise, predicate in the minor
Figure 2P is MS is MS is PPredicate in both premises
Figure 3M is PM is SS is PSubject in both premises
Figure 4P is MM is SS is PPredicate in the major premise, subject in the minor

Within each figure, depending on which statement type (A, E, I, O) is chosen for the major premise, the minor premise, and the conclusion, some combinations produce a valid syllogism and others don't; the 24 valid combinations are exactly the 24 moods named in the table below.

FigureNames (medieval mnemonic)
Figure 1Barbara, Celarent, Darii, Ferio, Barbari*, Celaront*
Figure 2Cesare, Camestres, Festino, Baroco, Cesaro*, Camestrop*
Figure 3Darapti, Disamis, Datisi, Felapton, Bocardo, Ferison
Figure 4Bramantip, Camenes, Dimaris, Fesapo, Fresison, Camenop*

These odd-sounding names aren't arbitrary to memorize, since each vowel in the name (a, e, i, o) encodes exactly the statement type (A, E, I, O) that appears at that position in the syllogism, in the order major premise, minor premise, conclusion. The table below fully decodes all six Figure 1 names, each with a concrete example, instead of stopping at Barbara alone.

NameMoodLogicExample
BarbaraAAAAll M is PAll dogs are mammals.
All S is MAll German shepherds are dogs.
So all S is PSo all German shepherds are mammals.
CelarentEAENo M is PNo fish are mammals.
All S is MAll sharks are fish.
So no S is PSo no sharks are mammals.
DariiAIIAll M is PAll birds have a spine.
Some S is MSome pets are birds.
So some S is PSo some pets have a spine.
FerioEIONo M is PNo insects have a spine.
Some S is MSome pets are insects.
So some S is not PSo some pets do not have a spine.
Barbari*AAIAll M is PAll dogs are mammals.
All S is MAll German shepherds are dogs.
So some S is PSo some German shepherds are mammals (weaker than "all," even though it shares Barbara's premises).
Celaront*EAONo M is PNo fish are mammals.
All S is MAll sharks are fish.
So some S is not PSo some sharks are not mammals (weaker than "none at all," even though it shares Celarent's premises).

The last two entries, marked *, show exactly what "weakened" means right there on the table rows: Barbari uses exactly Barbara's two premises, Celaront uses exactly Celarent's two premises, but both draw only the partial conclusion "some," even though those very premises are strong enough to support "all" or "none at all." This weakening step is only valid if the class S is assumed to have at least one member, the same existential assumption mentioned above; without it, a universal conclusion (Barbara, Celarent) doesn't automatically carry over into the corresponding partial one.

Moving to Figure 2, the way moods are read stays the same; only the position of the middle term changes. Cesare (Figure 2, EAE) is one example:

NameMoodLogicExample
CesareEAENo P is MNo birds are cold-blooded.
All S is MAll reptiles are cold-blooded.
So no S is PSo no reptiles are birds.

Compare this with the middle-term position table above: in Barbara and Celarent (Figure 1), the middle term M is the subject in the major premise and then the predicate in the minor; in Cesare (Figure 2), the middle term "cold-blooded" is the predicate in both premises. Both reach a conclusion of the form "S is P" or "no S is P," but the path through the middle term differs by figure, and that is exactly what the table of names above is classifying.

This table isn't meant to be memorized; its purpose is to make clear something easy to forget when focused only on fallacies: alongside the 13 kinds of refutation Aristotle classified, there exists a limited, countable set of categorical syllogisms that genuinely hold up, not some vague leftover space where "everything else must be correct." But keep the limits of this comparison in mind: many other valid forms of inference, like modus ponens, disjunctive syllogism, induction, or causal reasoning, all fall outside the 24 categorical syllogisms without being fallacies at all; conversely, most of the 13 fallacies in the table above, like a red herring or a loaded question, can't be reduced to "a syllogism that doesn't belong to the 24 valid forms" either. The two lists complement each other historically without covering each other logically.

37 Reasoning Errors, Biases, and Decision-Making Mistakes

A compiled list of 37 common errors in argument, cognitive bias, and decision-making, for quick lookup whenever you need to match a name to a short description. This is a practical reference table, not a single standardized taxonomy in logic; some of these names belong more to cognitive psychology, data analysis, or everyday debate language than to standardized formal-logic terminology.

GroupFallacy / ErrorDescription
Informal1. Ad hominemAttacking the opponent's character, credentials, or motives instead of engaging with their argument.
Informal2. Red herringDeliberately introducing an unrelated topic to distract and pull the discussion away from the real issue.
Informal3. Straw manDistorting or oversimplifying an opponent's argument into a weaker version that's easier to attack and knock down.
Informal4. EquivocationUsing a vague, ambiguous word, or a pun, within the same argument to produce a misleading inference.
Informal5. Slippery slopeClaiming a small initial action will inevitably trigger a chain of extreme consequences, without reasonable evidence.
Informal6. False dilemma (Black-or-white)Forcing a choice between two extreme options when other options actually exist.
Informal7. BandwagonTreating something as true or good simply because a large number of people believe or do it.
Informal8. Appeal to ignoranceAsserting a claim is true simply because it hasn't been proven false (or the reverse).
Informal9. Circular argument (Begging the question)Using the conclusion itself as the reason supporting the argument, a logical loop.
Decision-making10. Sunk cost fallacyContinuing to commit to or invest in a bad decision simply because significant effort, time, or money has already been spent, colloquially "throwing good money after bad."
Informal11. Appeal to pity (Ad misericordiam)Exploiting emotion or pity to persuade instead of using reason and objective evidence.
Informal12. False compromise (Middle ground)Assuming the solution lying exactly between two extreme positions is always the correct one.
Tactic13. Moving the goalpostsRepeatedly changing the standard or setting a harder new requirement once the opponent has already met the original one.
Informal14. Nirvana fallacy (Perfect solution fallacy)Rejecting a workable solution simply because it doesn't reach an ideal, perfect standard.
Tactic15. Proof by assertionRepeating a claim over and over without providing evidence, pressuring the listener into accepting it as true.
Informal16. Appeal to accomplishment (Appeal to ego)Using one's achievements, status, or social standing to assert being right without offering a logical argument.
Informal17. Special pleadingExempting oneself, or one's own argument, from general rules or standards without a legitimate reason.
Informal18. False cause (Correlation proves causation / Post hoc)Asserting a cause-and-effect relationship between two events simply because they happen at the same time or one right after the other.
Informal19. Fallacy of divisionAssuming a property of the whole automatically holds for each individual part within it.
Informal20. Fallacy of compositionAssuming a property of each individual part automatically holds for the whole.
Informal21. Appeal to authority (Ad verecundiam)Treating an authority figure's assertion as decisive proof, even when their fame, power, or even genuine expertise doesn't itself guarantee the conclusion; citing a suitably qualified expert in a field with real consensus is not automatically a fallacy.
Informal22. Hasty generalizationDrawing a general conclusion about a large population from a sample or observation that's too small or unrepresentative.
Informal23. No true ScotsmanIntroducing arbitrary conditions to exclude counterexamples in order to protect an overly broad general claim.
Informal24. Genetic fallacyJudging whether an argument is right or wrong based on its origin or history rather than its actual content.
Tactic25. Mind reading fallacyAssuming with certainty that you know what the other person is thinking, or guessing at their hidden motives, to rebut them without evidence.
Informal26. False analogyUsing a resemblance between two things in one narrow respect to conclude they're also alike in another respect that isn't actually relevant.
Informal27. Appeal to force (Ad baculum)Using threats, pressure, or violence to force someone to accept an argument instead of using reason.
Informal28. Appeal to fearExploiting fear or panic in a crowd to steer them toward accepting a particular claim.
Bias29. Confirmation biasFocusing only on seeking and noting information that supports one's own view while ignoring evidence against it; classified as a cognitive bias rather than a fallacy, since the error lies in how evidence is gathered before an argument is even made, not in the structure of the argument itself.
Tactic30. Moral superiority (Ethical fallacy)Positioning oneself on the moral high ground to dismiss someone else's view without actually analyzing whether it's logically right or wrong.
Informal31. Appeal to probabilityAssuming that because something could possibly happen, it therefore will happen.
Decision-making32. Opportunity cost fallacyIgnoring or misjudging the real opportunity cost when making a decision or an argument.
Informal33. Loaded question (Complex question)Asking a question that already contains an unproven assumption, so that any direct answer, "yes" or "no," ends up conceding that assumption.
Informal34. Tu quoque (Appeal to hypocrisy)Pointing out that the opponent has committed a similar wrong, to neutralize their criticism instead of addressing the actual issue.
Informal35. Argument from silenceConcluding a claim is true or false simply because of a lack of public information or evidence, or because the other party stays silent instead of objecting.
Decision-making36. Data fallacyUsing, extracting, or manipulating flawed or context-stripped data to create a false impression that supports an argument.
Informal37. Two wrongs make a rightTreating one's own wrongdoing as acceptable simply because someone else did something wrong too.

The "Informal" group covers relevance, presumption, language, and induction/statistics errors, following the classification in Post 1; "Tactic" covers debate maneuvers made in bad faith rather than pure structural errors in an argument; "Bias" is a cognitive error that happens before an argument is even voiced; "Decision-making" is a distortion in how cost, benefit, or data is weighed when choosing a course of action.

Economic Fallacies According to Thomas Sowell

In Economic Facts and Fallacies, economist Thomas Sowell compiles five foundational fallacy patterns, then applies them in turn to six specific areas of economic and social debate. [5] What follows presents Sowell's own framework for reference, not to judge whether any particular argument is right or wrong; this is one author's own point of view, not a scholarly consensus.

A. Five Foundational Fallacies

NameDefinitionScope of application
A1. Chess-pieces fallacy Treating people as mindless chess pieces to be rearranged at will by policymakers according to some master plan, ignoring that people have their own will, motives, and reactions to policy. Arguments favoring centralized planning and top-down policy, such as rent control, urban planning, or planned labor allocation.
A2. Open-ended fallacy Assuming that if a small amount of something is good, more must always be better, with no limit, ignoring diminishing marginal returns and cost-benefit trade-offs. Debates over minimum wage, workplace safety regulation, public spending, and environmental regulation.
A3. Fallacy of composition Assuming what's true for an individual or a small part of a system is also true for the system as a whole, ignoring second-order effects once that behavior is scaled up. Macroeconomic arguments, such as the paradox of thrift, or assuming what holds for a single firm still holds once every firm does the same thing.
A4. Zero-sum fallacy Assuming every economic transaction is a zero-sum game, meaning one side's gain must come at the other side's proportional loss, ignoring the possibility that both sides benefit through voluntary exchange. Debates over international trade, corporate profit, and the wealth gap.
A5. False cause fallacy (post hoc) Assuming that because B happened after A, A must be the cause of B, ignoring the possibility of coincidence or a third factor driving both. Evaluating the effectiveness of economic policy and social intervention programs, where a change observed right after a policy takes effect gets credited entirely to that policy.

B. Six Areas of Application

The six areas below simply list the arguments Sowell considers fallacious in each field, without analysis or evaluation; see the book itself to follow the author's full reasoning and evidence.

B1. Urban Fallacies

  • Rent control helps low-income people find affordable housing over the long run.
  • "Open space" and anti-sprawl policies always benefit the community.
  • Traffic congestion is mainly caused by poor planning, not by the absence of peak-hour pricing mechanisms.

B2. Male-Female Fallacies

  • The gender pay gap directly reflects the degree of discrimination.
  • Men and women would have identical career outcomes if not for social barriers.
  • Hours worked, choice of occupation, and career interruptions don't meaningfully affect income.

B3. Academic Fallacies

  • Preferential admissions (affirmative action) always benefit the group being favored.
  • Higher education spending always produces better learning outcomes.
  • A university's prestige directly reflects the quality of its teaching.

B4. Income Fallacies

  • "The rich keep getting richer, the poor keep getting poorer" rests on comparing fixed income brackets over time, ignoring that individuals move between brackets.
  • The ratio of CEO pay to worker pay directly reflects the degree of injustice.
  • Rising income inequality means poor people's lives are necessarily getting worse.

B5. Racial Fallacies

  • Income and wealth gaps between racial groups can only be explained by discrimination.
  • Minimum wage laws affect all racial groups equally.
  • Differences in economic outcomes between groups always reflect differences in opportunity, not other factors like average age or family structure.

B6. Third World Fallacies

  • Poverty in developing countries is mainly caused by colonial legacy.
  • Foreign aid always drives economic growth.
  • Abundant natural resources guarantee economic prosperity.
