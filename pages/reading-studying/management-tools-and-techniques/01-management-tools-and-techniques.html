Management Tools and Techniques

No tool is neutral: each one makes one thing visible, and everything else fades into the background

Updated: October 10, 2026

Definition

In this series, "management tools and techniques" is an umbrella term for four things that look nothing alike: sequences of steps like 5S or PDCA, ways of drawing a situation so it can be seen like a Gantt chart or a flowchart, analytical frames like SWOT or PESTEL, and measures like KPI or ROI. What they share is packaging rather than shape: someone compressed the handling of a recurring situation into something repeatable, gave it a name, and from that point on it could travel to an organisation completely unlike the one that produced it. Whether it shows up as a table, a diagram, a metric or a sequence of steps, at bottom each one is a portable procedure. They exist because an organisation has to decide faster than it can afford to think everything through from scratch: a workshop of three hundred people cannot spend every morning debating where the spanner belongs, and a project with twenty work items cannot rebuild from zero each week the question of who goes before whom. The name matters more than the shape, because only a name travels, gets taught, and gets sold.

The thing most easily confused with a management tool is management theory. The two are not separable, most tools rest on some theory left unstated and can fail because that underlying theory is wrong, but what each one is checked against differs. Theory answers why people in an organisation behave the way they do; a tool answers what to do tomorrow morning. Maslow's hierarchy of needs leans hard toward the first, it does not tell you which box to fill in. 5S leans hard toward the second, it tells you what to throw out first. A wrong theory is wrong because it misdescribes people, and that kind of wrong can still be argued over. A broken tool usually does not announce itself that way: it keeps running smoothly, keeps producing full tables, it is just that what it measures has stopped having anything to do with what you actually need to know. That failure mode is harder to catch, because the tool never throws an error.

At this point the reader will reach for the obvious escape: just pick the right tool for the right job. That sentence is true, and thinner than it looks, because the hard part is that fit depends on assumptions the tool rarely states out loud: it silently assumes the work can be broken into pieces, or that it cannot; that what deserves measuring is time, or error rate, or on-time delivery count; that people will report honestly, or will report in whatever way makes the numbers look better. Those assumptions travel with the tool into the new organisation and nobody opens them up to read. Fit can be guessed at in advance, from the nature of the work, the level of uncertainty, the cost of getting it wrong, or a narrow pilot run. What cannot be done is confirming fit with certainty before rollout; that part has to be checked and adjusted while the tool is already in use.

The point of this series is therefore not to add one more toolkit. For each tool, it tries to answer three questions: what does this tool make visible, what does it push out of sight, and when does it fail. Those three questions matter more than the step-by-step instructions, because instructions are one lookup away, while the sales material for a tool rarely dwells on those three. This series is also not a list of tools you should use, and not a list of tools you should avoid. Most of what is in here has a place where it works well and a place where it does harm, and telling those two places apart is the reader's job, not the writer's.

Classic Examples

The three stories below do not belong to any single tool in the list above. They are about the trade of making management tools itself, at three different scales: a country banning a technique by statute, a movement sweeping through the entire corporate world and then correcting itself, and a single company running the same tool in two places and getting two opposite results.

The US Congress Bans the Stopwatch

In August 1911, moulders at the Watertown Arsenal in Massachusetts walked off the job, refusing to be timed with a stopwatch. The system being installed there was Frederick Taylor's scientific management, and the strike led directly to a special House committee set up to investigate the Taylor system and other shop management systems. Taylor testified on 25 January 1912, and that testimony was later reprinted as a book. [1] By 1915, a clause in the Army Appropriations Act banned the use of stopwatches and the payment of premiums or bonuses in government arsenals. [2]

The ban matters less than the date on it. Scientific management was less than a decade old at that point, and already strong enough to change how work was done in the largest workshops in the country, and provocative enough that a legislature had to step in. A management technique is not a neutral thing sitting there waiting to be chosen. It touches who is accountable for what, who gets measured and who holds the measuring stick, so it is political from day one, even when in itself it is nothing more than a few steps and a table.

The $4.7 Billion Industry and Its Own Founder's Retraction

In 1993, Michael Hammer and James Champy published Reengineering the Corporation, and business process reengineering became the biggest management movement of the decade. By 1996, the Wall Street Journal was calling it a $4.7 billion industry. That same article carried Hammer's own admission, that he and the people leading the movement had neglected people. He said he was not smart enough about that dimension, that he was reflecting his engineering background and had not given proper weight to the human piece. [3]

Thomas Davenport, one of the people who helped originate the movement, later wrote that unfounded optimism was a major reason reengineering rose fast and fell just as fast, and that the movement's rhetoric, the kind that talked about shooting the stragglers, both frightened employees and inflated management's expectations past what could be delivered. [3] The limits of this example need stating plainly: a founder's retraction does not prove the tool was worthless, and nobody has measured how much of that $4.7 billion was money wasted. What it shows is something else, smaller but more solid: how far a management tool spreads and how solid the evidence behind it is are two quantities that barely track each other.

The Same Six Sigma, Two Opposite Results in One Company

James McNerney took over as CEO of 3M after being announced on 5 December 2000, the first outsider to lead the century-old company. He cut eight thousand jobs, about 11% of the workforce, and rolled out Six Sigma, a quality programme built at Motorola in 1986 to drive variation and defects out of a process, into every corner of the company, including product development and research. Financially it worked: operating margin rose from 17% in 2001 to 23% in 2005. [4]

In the lab, it went the other way. 3M had long prided itself on at least a third of revenue coming from products launched in the past five years; by 2007 that share had fallen to a quarter. On the Boston Consulting Group's ranking of the most innovative companies, 3M sat first in 2004, second in 2005, third in 2006, then seventh in 2007. George Buckley, McNerney's successor, dropped the requirement that 3M's research scientists chase Six Sigma targets. Larry Wendling, the vice president in charge of research, described what had happened in a very concrete sentence: some of it made sense in the lab, some of it was people dreaming up green-belt projects just to fill their quota of green-belt projects for that period. [4]

This is the most vivid of the three examples, but it has to be said plainly that it is not a natural experiment, and it does not isolate the effect of Six Sigma. Over that same stretch 3M changed CEOs, cut eight thousand people, cut capital spending 22% from $980 million down to $763 million and then to a low of $677 million in 2003, and kept the research budget essentially flat from 2001 to 2005. [4] The lag built into any product pipeline alone is enough to make the 2007 numbers reflect decisions made years earlier. On top of that, an innovation ranking is a measure of reputation, and this piece will later warn that reputation can come apart from real results.

What this example offers is a mechanism that sounds plausible and needs broader evidence to check. The mechanism runs like this: Six Sigma reduces variation. On a production line, reducing variation is exactly what is needed. Art Fry, the 3M scientist behind the Post-it note, reckoned it takes five to six thousand raw ideas to reach one successful business. [4] In a place running on those odds, reducing variation cuts into the raw material the work needs. Checking that mechanism is the job of the next section.

Scientific Research

The effectiveness of management methods has been studied for a long time. What arrived late was a different strand: one that treats a tool's own popularity, and the cost of managing by procedure, as something to be measured rather than a solution to be spread. That strand only took shape in the 1990s and 2000s. The three studies below are not evidence covering all thirty tools in the list; they mostly touch Taylorism, reengineering, Six Sigma and total quality management. They were chosen because they make clear three mechanisms that generalise: tools redistribute control, managing by procedure can tilt the balance between exploitation (working ground the company already knows) and exploration (going outside it), and attaching your name to a fashionable tool can buy reputation that comes apart from business results.

How Do You Even Measure a Management Tool

The difficulty is that nobody rolls out a management tool in a sealed room. It always arrives alongside a restructuring, a new leadership team, an economic cycle. So this line of research rarely asks the tool's effectiveness directly, and instead works around it three ways. The first is a usage survey, asking hundreds to thousands of managers which tools they use and how satisfied they are. The second is tracking an objective external indicator across many years and many companies, then watching how it moves around the time a tool gets adopted. The third is comparing adopting companies against non-adopting ones, on several measures at once, including measures that are not business outcomes at all.

The first approach is the one that looks most direct and delivers least. Claude Besner and Brian Hobbs put a list of seventy tools and techniques in front of more than 750 practising project managers and asked how far each one was actually used. [5] A survey like that maps what a field reaches for, which is worth knowing on its own. What it cannot do is say whether any of it works, and the whole distance between those two questions is what the rest of this section is about.

The second approach has produced the sharpest results so far. Mary Benner and Michael Tushman tracked patenting activity along with ISO 9000 certification across the paint and photography industries over twenty years. They split patents into two kinds: those building on knowledge the company already had, and those reaching beyond what was already known. The more process management activity, the more the first kind grew, both in raw count and as a share of total patents. [6] In other words, process management does not stop a company from innovating; it pushes innovation toward the safe end, and that shift is measurable. The 3M story pointed at this shape; here the shape is measured, across two industries and twenty years, instead of told through one company.

The third approach delivers the most uncomfortable finding in the whole field. Barry Staw and Lisa Epstein looked at companies that attached their name to fashionable techniques, specifically total quality management, teamwork and empowerment. The result: those companies did not have higher economic performance. But they were more admired, rated as more innovative, scored higher on management quality, and their CEOs were paid more. [7]

That is a sentence worth reading slowly, and worth reading at the right scope. This study is observational, not an intervention, so what it has is correlations in the data: attaching your name to a fashionable technique does not come with higher economic performance, but it does come with higher reputation and higher CEO pay. Reading that as a reward-distribution mechanism is a step this piece is taking, not one the two authors took. But if that correlation really does hold, then the incentive for a company to adopt a fashionable tool survives even when the tool does nothing for operations, and that is a mechanism that could help explain why management fads keep arriving in waves without waiting for evidence.

Where the field has had to correct itself. The research just described is itself a retraction from management studies, a field that for decades mostly played the role of tool salesman. But it has weaknesses too, and they need stating. These studies are almost all observational, not controlled experiments, so the direction of causation stays open: a company doing well might have spare cash and spare attention to run more programmes, rather than the programmes making it do well. Measuring adoption relies on companies self-reporting and on how the press labels them, and both are skewed. Among the studies cited here, what is solid is evidence about the shape of the effect, the kind of thing Benner and Tushman found, not about its size; none of these studies answers the question of how much you gain by adopting tool X.

That does not mean management tools in general are a con, and it does not license the conclusion that anything popular is suspect. 3M's margin gain was real, not illusory. What these studies reject is a much narrower claim: that a tool's popularity is a signal that it works. Popularity measures something else, and Staw and Epstein showed what that something else is.

Further Reading

  • Hugh G. J. Aitken, Scientific Management in Action: Taylorism at Watertown Arsenal, 1908-1915. The fullest historical account of the first time a management technique was hauled before a legislative committee. Worth reading to see what applying a tool to a real workplace looks like, told week by week. Publisher's page.
  • The full BusinessWeek article on 3M. Four pages, fifteen minutes to read, and it carries voices both for and against from inside the same company. Few documents show as clearly how a tool can be right in one place and wrong in another. Read the PDF.
  • Frederick Winslow Taylor, The Principles of Scientific Management (1911). A founding text of modern scientific management, short, and free to read in full because its copyright has expired. Worth reading for two opposite reasons: to see how much of today's management tools still stand on the same set of assumptions, and to see how bluntly Taylor stated them himself, without the hedging later tool-selling literature learned to add. Read the full text on Project Gutenberg.

Summary

Key Point: A management tool is a portable procedure, and each one carries an unwritten set of assumptions: about whether the work can be broken into pieces, about what deserves measuring, about how honestly people will report. So the question worth asking of a tool is not whether it works, but what it makes visible, what it pushes out of sight, and when it fails. The three examples in this piece show three faces of the same thing: a management technique strong enough to trigger an act of Congress, a major movement that could spread before the evidence arrived and then had to correct itself, and at 3M, the same programme running alongside a company-wide margin increase while indicators and research findings suggest the innovation reaching beyond known territory may have been squeezed out. The most uncomfortable part comes from the research: in Staw and Epstein's data, companies that attached their name to a fashionable technique did not have higher economic performance, but were more admired and paid their CEOs more. That does not turn management tools into a con; it only cuts the thread most people still assume is there, the one connecting a tool's popularity to what it actually does.
