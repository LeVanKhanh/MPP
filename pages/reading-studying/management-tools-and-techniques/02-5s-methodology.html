5S

Everyone remembers it as a spring clean, but what it builds is a workplace where anything wrong shows itself

Updated: October 9, 2026

Definition

What Is 5S

5S is a five-step method for organising a workplace, named after five Japanese words that all begin with S: seiri, seiton, seiso, seiketsu, shitsuke. In English they are usually rendered as sort, set in order, shine, standardise and sustain. Put plainly: throw out what you do not use, give everything that remains a fixed home, clean, lock that state in as the standard, then keep the standard from drifting. In a repair shop that has done 5S, the 17mm spanner is not "somewhere in the toolbox"; it sits in a cut-out shaped like itself, which means an empty outline on the board is an alarm all by itself.

Hiroyuki Hirano set out the five steps in 5 Pillars of the Visual Workplace, published in English by Productivity Press in 1995. [1] Who first devised 5S has no tidy answer, and the section on limits below explains why. The book's title says the destination out loud: a visual workplace, one where looking is enough to know what is wrong.

Why We Need It

A small workshop, and a machine that stops at nine in the morning. The fitter knows exactly what needs replacing and how; that part takes fifteen minutes. But the spare bearing is in one of four cabinets, the star-head screwdriver was borrowed yesterday and never came back, and somebody is fairly sure the strip-down instructions were printed out, just not where they ended up. The machine runs again at nine forty. In the month-end report the incident is logged as forty minutes of downtime from a bearing failure. No line records that twenty-five of those minutes were spent looking for things, because nobody measures that, and because looking for things has never counted as an incident.

5S goes after exactly that unrecorded time, and the way it does so is the mechanism of the whole method: build a standard state clear enough that deviations announce themselves. When every item has a home, a missing item is visible without anyone taking inventory, like the empty outline on the tool board. Cleanliness belongs to it as an instrument: when the floor under a machine is wiped every shift, a fresh oil stain becomes information instead of one smear among many, which makes the wipe-down an inspection.

An operational result of 5S has been measured at least once with a tight design. In a trial across sixteen hospitals in northern Tanzania, allocated to groups by lot, patients' waiting time fell significantly at the medical records counter and in the consultation rooms. [2] The trial measured waiting, and could not separate how many of those minutes came from finding records faster, so it shows a measurable result, not yet the mechanism.

What Are Its Limits

5S rearranges the place where work happens. It can win back time lost to searching, walking and waiting for things, but it cannot fix a constraint that lives in the sequence of the work. If the constraint lies in the rules of a three-day approval step, or in two departments that do not talk to each other, a tidy shop floor will hardly shave a single day off it.

The fifth S has more than one reading, and even its name has not settled. In Hirano's 1995 book, the chapter on the fifth S is titled Discipline. [1] A year later, a shortened edition for shop-floor operators, same author, same publisher, renamed that chapter Sustain. [3] The Lean Enterprise Institute keeps both: it uses the word Sustain, and defines shitsuke as the discipline to perform the first four S's. [4] The change of name does not tell us which reading is right, since discipline can itself be built through training, clear ownership and steady practice. What breaks is the implementation: when the fifth S shrinks to an appeal for people to be more conscientious, it stops being anyone's job. To make it stand, someone has to own it, people have to be trained for it, and there has to be a concrete cadence of checks.

Toyota shows the fifth S can sit outside the five steps altogether. According to the Lean Enterprise Institute and Art Smalley, Toyota's traditional presentation has only four S's. [4] [5] The LEI gives the reason: shitsuke becomes redundant under Toyota's daily, weekly and monthly audits of standardised work, meaning the upkeep already lived inside how the place was managed. [4] Why the fifth S was added once the method travelled outside Toyota has not been settled, since Smalley himself notes that the history of 4S and 5S is documented less cleanly than many other Toyota practices. [5]

Examples

The first example is a familiar kind of 5S drive, all five steps done on paper and nothing thrown away. The other two are places that did 5S and then counted: a laboratory counting its non-conformities, and four Senegalese health centres asking nearly two thousand patients, with the biggest gain turning up somewhere few would guess.

An Audit Score of Ninety-Two, and Nothing Left the Store

An illustrative scenario: a spare-parts store launches "5S week". The team stops production for an afternoon, wipes the shelving, paints yellow lines at the foot of the racks, prints a label for every bin, and posts before-and-after photos to the company chat. At the end of the week the quality department brings down a fifty-item scoresheet and awards 92 points. The "after" photo is printed large and hung by the store door.

Nothing leaves the store. Three crates of parts for a machine line scrapped two years ago are still there, now freshly labelled, because throwing them out needs a signature and nobody wants to be the one who signed if they turn out to be needed next month. The labels were made for whatever was already on the shelves, so the shelves are as full as before, just straighter. Three months later, newly delivered parts sit in the aisle because no bin is free, and the next audit is postponed because it is peak order season. All five steps were "done", but the first was done as tidying, and the fifth was never given to anyone.

Three Months of 5S in a Laboratory Working Towards ISO 15189

In the clinical laboratory of a university hospital in Izmir, Doğan and colleagues used 5S to deal with the working-conditions and safety part of ISO 15189, the quality standard for medical laboratories. They trained both management and staff in 5S, formed a 5S team, then ran the five steps over three months. The laboratory's non-conformity score for its working environment, a score it had tracked over previous years, fell by 69.7% after the rollout; the abstract does not say how the score was calculated. Of 114 staff given the questionnaire, 63 replied, and they reported that 5S made the job easier and the environment safer. [6]

This is a before-and-after measurement at a single site, with no comparison group. The laboratory was the first public clinical laboratory in Turkey accredited to ISO 15189, and 5S was used as part of building its safety system for that standard; other work done for the ISO standard ran through the same system, and the paper does not separate 5S from it. The number shows how one laboratory tied 5S to a measurable goal; it does not isolate the part that belongs to 5S alone.

Five Days in Four Senegalese Health Centres, and the Clearest Gain Was in How Staff Spoke to Patients

In the Tambacounda and Kédougou regions of eastern Senegal, a JICA technical cooperation project brought 5S into health centres through a five-day package: a first day of lectures, two days of practice on site, and two days of evaluation and wrap-up. Every member of staff took part, clinical, administrative and support alike. The facilitation team included "apprentice mentors", staff from the centres themselves who learned by doing so they could lead later. Kanamori and colleagues compared four centres that had done 5S with three that had not, surveying 1,928 patients and caretakers with ten questions on a five-point scale. Six to eight months after the intervention, the observed mean score of the 5S group went from 3.98 to 4.20, and that of the comparison group from 4.01 to 4.07. After adjusting for respondents' characteristics, the 5S group gained 0.13 points more than the comparison group. None of the ten questions asked about cleanliness; the clearest extra gain was on the two questions about how staff spoke to patients, how they communicated and how they explained the illness, 0.23 points each. [7]

The centres were not allocated by lot: the comparison group was made up of facilities about to move into new buildings, where 5S was postponed until after the move. Staff from both groups still met at regional meetings. The authors themselves say they could not tell whether staff attitudes shifted because the workplace was tidier or because staff had taken part in 5S together.

Some Research on 5S

Within the search behind this piece, the most tightly designed study of 5S is a trial in African hospitals. It measures one concrete thing, the minutes patients spend waiting, and sets the hospitals that did 5S beside those that did not.

The study: In 2016, Ishijima, Eliakimu and Mshana published a cluster randomised trial in The TQM Journal: sixteen hospitals in northern Tanzania were split into a 5S group and a control group by block randomisation. Waiting time in the outpatient departments was measured at all sixteen hospitals before the intervention and again after one year, and the groups were compared by difference-in-differences, taking the fall in the 5S group minus the fall in the control group. [2] A second paper with Ishijima among its authors, by Kamiya and colleagues, evaluated sixteen district hospitals in the Kilimanjaro and Manyara regions from the patient's side, surveying 3,292 people as they left, over twelve months of 5S. [8]

Findings: In the 5S group, waiting time fell significantly at the medical records counter (p=0.002) and in the consultation rooms (p=0.020). The difference-in-differences estimates point the same way: 15.66 minutes less at the records counter and 41.90 minutes less in the consultation rooms. [2] On the patient side, 5S raised the cleanliness of the department, perceived waiting time and overall satisfaction, but made negligible difference to patients' experience of how staff treated them. [8]

What it shows: The trial shows that, in these hospitals, 5S shortened a measurable wait at the records counter and in the consultation rooms. The abstract does not say what mechanism cut the consultation-room wait by more than the records-counter wait. It says nothing about whether the effect lasted beyond the first year, what it cost, or whether it would repeat on a factory floor or in an office. The authors list their own limitations: data were collected over only three days at baseline and three days at end-line, the areas piloted differed from hospital to hospital, and the measurement of waiting time may be biased. The two papers are best read as two consistent views, not two independent checks: one shared author, the same number of hospitals, the same northern region. Both, along with the Senegal study, are tied to JICA, the agency supporting 5S in health care in both countries: one of Kamiya's co-authors is from JICA, and in Senegal JICA funded the study. That does not by itself make the results less trustworthy, but it is a reason to hope an independent team repeats the work.

The wider picture is hard to read. A 2024 systematic review of 5S in healthcare, searching PubMed, Google Scholar and Cochrane up to March 2024, gathered only six qualifying studies according to its abstract (its methods section says seven, and its table lists seven), different enough that the numbers could not be pooled. That list contains neither the Tanzania trial nor the 2016 Senegal study, although both Kamiya's paper and the Senegal paper are on PubMed. [9] This says first of all something about the limits of that review: six is the count from one search, not an inventory of the field. Still, for a method taught worldwide for thirty years, the fact that the best evidence has to be found outside a systematic review is worth noticing.

The counter-case: The case for 5S rests on three good arguments. First, 5S is almost never deployed on its own, so isolating its contribution demands designs that run longer and cost far more than the way 5S actually gets rolled out. Second, its direct cost is far below most transformation programmes: no licence, no expensive equipment, no obligatory consultant, so the evidence threshold for trying it across one small area can reasonably be lower. Third, the real output of 5S may not be the metrics at all but a team's capacity to notice deviation, something every improvement method that comes afterwards quietly assumes is already there.

This piece accepts the second argument, with a caveat: a low direct cost does not mean a failed attempt costs nothing, because a going-through-the-motions 5S programme still eats the team's time, still burns money on labels, shadow boards and re-layout sessions, and burns the most expensive thing of all, the feeling that improvement has already happened. The Tanzania trial answers the first argument in part: hard to measure does not mean impossible to measure, since a comparison with a control group and allocation by lot can be organised without anyone deliberately leaving a place in a mess. The rest of that argument stands: sixteen clusters do not by themselves guarantee enough statistical power for every result. No study in this piece measures the third argument directly. What rose in Senegal was how patients felt staff spoke to them, a different variable from staff's ability to spot deviation, so this argument remains a reasonable hypothesis that needs measuring in its own right. It is also precisely the sentence that lets a dying programme defend itself indefinitely: whenever the numbers refuse to move, one can always say the thing being built is capacity, not results yet. What separates the two cases is not the audit score but in two questions: has that team lately caught a problem it used to miss, and how long does it take from an abnormality appearing to its being dealt with.

Why this is useful: It changes the question you should ask when somebody shows off 5S results. A before-and-after photograph only shows the area looked different on two occasions; it says nothing about what the change produced or how long it lasted, and every workplace looks tidier on precisely the day someone photographs it. Three questions are worth asking instead: apart from the 5S score, which metric moved, such as minutes waited at a records counter; when was it measured again after rollout; and who decided to keep, move or discard each group of items, against what criteria. The third is the hardest to answer, because if nobody can say why each group of items needs to stay in the area, the sorting step may have stopped at tidying.

How to Apply It

5S works by making the abnormal visible, so the implementations that hold up are the ones that keep that looking inside the working day, and keep only the checks that lead to an abnormality being dealt with.

  • Agree the criteria for keeping things, and who may sign, before sorting: seiri is easily dodged because throwing something out means somebody carries the blame if it turns out to be needed later; after the 5S round, the test is whether anyone can explain why each group of items is still there, and counting what was thrown away cannot replace that test, because an area that was already sensible may need nothing removed
  • Prefer building the check into a rhythm that already exists: Toyota held the standard while using only four S's because its daily, weekly and monthly audits of standardised work were already part of how the place was managed; a 5S audit on its own calendar can still help when each round leads to an abnormality being dealt with, but if it only produces a number it tends to be what gets postponed first when an order gets urgent
  • Measure a baseline before changing anything: reuse numbers that already exist, such as downtime or picking errors; whatever nobody records, like the time spent searching in the repair scene above, has to start being measured before the clear-out, because a 5S score only measures compliance with the 5S scoresheet
  • Do not use 5S to fix a constraint that lives in the sequence of work: before choosing 5S, ask whether the lost time really sits in searching, walking and waiting for things; if work is slow because of approval rules or two departments that do not talk, a tidy workplace also manufactures the sense that improvement has happened and stops people looking for the actual constraint

Applying It to a Scenario

The accounts team at a trading company has five people sharing one bank of document cabinets and one shelf of files. Whenever auditors, the bank or the board need an original set of documents, the request goes to the team's shared inbox. September brought 26 such requests. Response time, counted in working hours from the email arriving to the documents going out, averaged 47 minutes, and 5 requests were not dealt with until the next working day. That figure includes waiting for someone to be free, checking and copying the documents, and waiting for approval, so it does not show the search time on its own, the part that rearranging a shelf targets most directly. The figures in this scenario are illustrative.

  1. Choose the measures before tidying anything: the shared inbox already logs when each request arrived and when it was answered, so response time is already there. Nobody records search time, so throughout September, for every request, the person handling it also noted the minutes from starting to search to having the set in hand: 24 minutes on average. Both figures were recorded on the same 26 requests, so they can be set side by side. The team leader is given authority to sign off every decision to move or discard a file, so the next step does not stall on who carries the blame
  2. Sort: the shelf holds 41 lever-arch files. The team agrees what stays on the shelf: documents for the current and previous year, plus any set with an open request. 23 files fail that test, most of them unopened in the past twelve months. Documents still within their retention period are not destroyed; they are boxed, indexed and moved to the archive store, because sorting means removing from the workplace, not necessarily throwing away. Three boxes of dead pens, broken calculators beyond repair and old training handouts go in the bin
  3. Set in order so things go back to the right place and a gap shows: the remaining 18 files are arranged by year and month, each in a fixed place. The team runs a diagonal stripe of coloured tape across the spines of the whole row, so a file taken out or put back in the wrong slot leaves a break in the stripe, visible from the doorway. Anyone who takes a file out puts a borrowing card with their name and the date in the empty slot
  4. Clean as an inspection: on Friday afternoon the person on duty spends ten minutes wiping the shelf and cabinet tops, and in those ten minutes looks at the stripe and the borrowing cards. Any card more than three days old gets a reminder to the borrower there and then
  5. Standardise: a photo of the shelf in its standard state is taped inside the cabinet door, with two rules: where a new month's file goes, and when a file must move to the archive store
  6. Hand the upkeep to a routine that already exists: looking over the shelf is added to the end-of-week closing checklist the team already works through, with a rotating person on duty, instead of a separate 5S audit calendar
  7. Measure again with the same two measures, then check whether anything else changed: November brought 24 requests, close to September's 26, and the kinds of documents asked for were similar, mostly purchase invoices and contracts. Across those same 24 requests, average search time fell to 5 minutes and response time to 30 minutes, and 2 requests ran into the next working day. Over those two months the team changed no software and no staff, and requests still came from the same parties. One of the two slow requests needed documents that had been moved to the archive store, so the team puts a copy of the archive index right beside the shelf

Search time fell from 24 to 5 minutes, while response time fell only from 47 to 30. Time outside searching, counted on the same requests, went from 23 to 25 minutes, so it barely changed; it covers waiting for someone to be free, checking and copying documents, and waiting for approval, which the team has not recorded separately and which rearranging a shelf does not target. The price that comes with it is visible too: anything moved to the archive now takes longer to fetch. With two months of figures this is a signal of improvement, not proof of cause, and it rests on three conditions: criteria for keeping things plus one person with authority to sign, so that unneeded material actually left the shelf; looking at the shelf attached to something the team already does every week; and both measures in place before the clear-out, so the two readings can be compared. If the main cause of delay had been waiting for the board to approve requests, the same clear-out would have produced a nicer shelf and left response time almost untouched.

Summary

Key Point: 5S establishes a standard state so that deviations and gaps surface on their own; cleanliness is an inspection instrument within it. The most counter-intuitive part is the fifth S: according to the LEI and Art Smalley, Toyota's traditional presentation has only four S's, because upkeep already lived inside its audits of standardised work. Within the search behind this piece, the tightest evidence is the randomised trial in Tanzanian hospitals, reported in two papers, where waiting time fell; the two other field studies presented here, in Turkey and Senegal, were not allocated by lot, and all three come from healthcare. The implementation worth trusting agrees criteria for what stays, builds the check into a rhythm that already exists, and measures a baseline before changing anything.
