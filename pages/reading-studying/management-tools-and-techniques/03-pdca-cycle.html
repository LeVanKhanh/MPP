PDCA Cycle

A cycle that never says beforehand what it expects is not a learning cycle, just a meeting cut into four parts

Published: August 10, 2026

Definition

The PDCA cycle is a four-step improvement loop: plan, do, check, then act on what the check turned up, and back to the start. It usually gets introduced under the heading of continuous improvement, and that heading is accurate while leaving out the hard part. Picture a support team that keeps having to escalate too many tickets to second line. They meet and decide to build a triage checklist for incoming work; that is Plan. They use the checklist for a month; that is Do. At the end of the month they sit down and confirm that everyone used it; that is Check. They write the checklist into the standard process; that is Act. All four steps done, nobody skipped anything, and a month later that team still knows nothing more about why tickets get escalated.

The gap is in step three, and it is there because step one already had one. The real mechanism of PDCA is that it forces a change to take the shape of a prediction that can be wrong. A Plan step done properly does not stop at writing down what will be done and by whom; it writes down the result you expect together with the reason you believe it, as a number wherever a number can be had. Only then does step three have work to do: set what happened beside what was predicted. Where those two meet is where the whole cycle produces understanding, and it produces it in both directions. Diverge, and the team knows one of its assumptions was wrong, and knows which one to go and check. Match, and the theory used to make the prediction has survived one more test, enough to be worth carrying somewhere else, though not enough to say the change caused it: a trend already running, another change happening at the same time, or plain measurement error will each produce a matching figure. Neither direction happens without something said in advance. In 1991 Ronald Moen, Thomas Nolan and Lloyd Provost added an explicit requirement for prediction to the planning step, and stated that step three is where observed data is set against the prediction to serve as the basis for learning. Their argument reaches past the single test: knowing that a change worked once will not do, because what has to be built is the ability to predict whether it will work under the different conditions you will meet later. [1]

The cycle was not born with those four letters. In 1939 Walter Shewhart published Statistical Method from the Viewpoint of Quality Control, setting out a three-step process in a straight line: specification, production, inspection. [2] Shewhart himself wrote that the three had to run in a circle instead of a straight line, and that read as steps of the scientific method, specification, production and inspection correspond to hypothesising, carrying out an experiment, and testing the hypothesis. [1] In 1950 Deming took a revised version of the cycle to Japan and presented it at an eight-day seminar run by the Japanese Union of Scientists and Engineers. His version had four steps, design, production, sales and research, and became known as the Deming wheel. According to Masaaki Imai, it was Japanese executives who recast that wheel into PDCA in 1951. Imai does not say who did it or how; to this day nobody has claimed the revision and nobody has disputed his account. [1]

Deming never accepted the PDCA cycle as his own, and he said so more sharply as the years went on. He warned audiences that the PDCA version is frequently inaccurate because the English word check means to hold back. In a letter to Ronald Moen dated 17 November 1990 he put it in one line: be sure to call it PDSA, not the corruption PDCA. The following year, sent a chart labelled plan-do-check-act, he replied that what he had been shown was not the Deming cycle, that he did not know the source of it, and that how PDCA ever came into existence he knew not. Earlier, at a roundtable held at the US Government Accounting Office, asked how the PDCA cycle and the Deming circle related to each other, he answered flatly that the two bear no relation. In 1993 he settled his own version in The New Economics and called it the Shewhart cycle for learning and improvement, that is, PDSA. [1] Study in place of Check is not a word chosen because it sounds better, it is a plain statement that the job of step three is to learn rather than to sign off.

Examples

The cycle shows up at three different levels: a situation you can picture, a study that sat down and counted how people actually run it, and a national programme whose results have to be read with real care.

Two Clinics Run the Same Cycle, One of Them Learns Something

Picture two clinics in the same system, both seeing patients wait too long, both deciding to try booked appointment slots instead of walk-in order.

The first clinic writes into its plan: roll out appointment slots from the start of next month, run by the senior nurse. A month later they meet, see that average waiting time has come down, and write the new arrangement into the standard process. The second clinic writes a different sentence into its plan: we expect average waiting time to fall from forty minutes to under twenty-five, because most of the current wait comes from patients bunching up early in the morning. They run a two-week test in a single clinic session. The real figure comes back at thirty-eight minutes. That is barely a move, and because they had written down why they believed the prediction, they knew straight away what to go and check: patients were not bunching early at all, they were bunching after their test results came back, and the bottleneck sat in the lab rather than in the appointment book.

The first clinic ran all four steps and recorded a small drop in waiting time, with nothing yet to show whether the drop came from the appointment book or from something else entirely. The second ran all four steps, improved nothing in those two weeks, and walked away with something the first did not have: knowing where the bottleneck is. Running all four steps does not produce understanding on its own; what opens the way to it is the thing said in advance at step one.

Four Hundred and Twenty-One Cycles, Seven That Met the Standard

In 2019 Christopher McNicholas and colleagues published a retrospective study in BMJ Quality & Safety, reviewing all 421 PDSA cycles run by 39 front-line improvement teams across three annual rounds of projects, six teams in 2009, sixteen in 2010 and seventeen in 2011. They scored every cycle against six predefined measures of fidelity. The result: 2% (7/421) of cycles met all six, 12% (49/421) met five or more, and 29% (121/421) met four or more. [3]

On the one measure that maps directly onto the mechanism above, an explicit prediction written down, the figures across the three rounds ran like this: 0% in round one, 3.3% in round two, 17.5% in round three, and 12.2% overall. [3] Two things are worth taking from that at once. In the first round, not a single one of those cycles wrote down a prediction. And the figure did climb once the support team changed how it trained people, how it selected projects, and how it coached them on the ground, which makes this something that can be fixed rather than a permanent defect in working people.

The limits are clear and the authors state them: this is one improvement programme, and the interview work reached only the three quality improvement staff who spanned all three rounds, so the account of why the figure climbed is much thinner than the counting behind it.

Four Hospitals in the Programme, Eighteen Outside It, and Both Groups Improved Alike

The Safer Patients Initiative was a patient safety programme funded by the Health Foundation, bringing quality improvement methods into British hospitals along the lines of the Institute for Healthcare Improvement, an American organisation devoted to improving healthcare quality, with the test cycle as its central frame. In 2011 Amirta Benning and colleagues published an independent evaluation of the first phase in the BMJ, comparing four participating hospitals, one in each country of the United Kingdom, against eighteen control hospitals. [4]

Quality of care did improve over the eighteen months of the study. The trouble is that it improved almost identically in both groups. The programme made a difference on one of eleven dimensions of the staff questionnaire, organisational climate, by 0.08 points on a five-point scale, and on one type of clinical process, the recording of respiratory rate, which rose from 37% to 78% in the participating hospitals against 40% to 69% in the controls. Prescribing errors and the quality of medical histories showed no additional improvement. Mortality leaned in favour of the participating hospitals without reaching statistical significance. [4]

The limits of this example have to be spelled out, because it is very easy to read too much into. The Safer Patients Initiative was a large multi-component intervention, not an experiment on PDCA, and there is no way to separate the contribution of the test cycle from everything else in the package. This result does not prove PDCA is useless. What it does prove is narrower and firmer: a well-funded programme, methodically run and shipped with an independent evaluation, can still fail to move ahead of the trend already under way among those who never joined. It also has to be said that the eighteen control hospitals were not eighteen places sitting still; they simply did not receive SPI1, while remaining hospitals running improvement work of their own, which is exactly why they improved too. That is what makes "did it improve" far too easy a question to ask of an improvement programme. The question that carries weight is heavier: did it improve beyond what the group outside the programme managed anyway.

Some Research on the PDCA Cycle

The study below does not ask whether PDCA works. It asks a question that gets skipped more often and is harder to deflect: when people say they are running PDCA, what are they actually doing. All three empirical sources in this piece come from healthcare, so they show one pattern of drift in application clearly while saying nothing about how common that drift is in manufacturing, software or anywhere else.

The study: In 2014 Michael Taylor and colleagues at Imperial College London published a systematic review in BMJ Quality & Safety on how the PDSA method is applied in healthcare. They built a theoretical framework first, five key features of the method drawn from the descriptions of the people who originated it: iterative cycles, prediction-based testing of change, initial small-scale testing, use of data over time, and documentation. They then scored every piece of peer-reviewed literature they could find against that framework. From 942 records they went to 409 after removing duplicates, and 73 articles met the inclusion criteria. [5]

Findings: Fewer than 20% (14/73) of articles fully documented a sequence of linked iterative cycles. Only 15% (7/47) used quantitative data at monthly or more frequent intervals to decide what the next cycle should be. On the central mechanism itself, only 9% (4/47) of articles recorded an explicit prediction of the outcome, which led the authors to conclude that PDSA cycles were largely not being used as learning cycles to test and revise a theory-based prediction. Across all five principles together, exactly 2 of the 73 articles complied. The most expensive figure sits elsewhere: among articles using the term "PDCA", 13.6% (3/22) described iterative cycles; among those using "PDSA", the figure was 44% (11/25). [5]

What it shows: This is a review of documentation and application, not of effectiveness. The authors write plainly that the study did not compare quality of application against reported outcomes, so it cannot conclude that better application produces better results; it only draws on the principles of the method to explain why that would be expected. The scope is healthcare, and peer-reviewed English-language literature up to September 2012. The authors raise publication bias themselves, running in two directions: research using PDSA that did not yield good results is less likely to be published, and journals are more accustomed to before-and-after studies than to work presenting data over time. What the review does show, within exactly that scope: the distance between the method as described and the method as practised is very large, and it is widest at the prediction step.

The counter-case: Before settling this, the defence deserves a fair hearing, because it has at least three arguments that stand up. First, the review counts what got written into the article, and scientific papers are squeezed for length and shaped by journal habit; a team could perfectly well have discussed a prediction at the whiteboard and left it out of the manuscript. Second, the real strength of PDCA is that it is cheap and lowers the barrier: it lets a senior nurse try a change on a Tuesday without standing up a formal research design, without a statistician, without a budget, though how much approval is needed still depends on the level of risk and on local rules. Demanding a quantitative prediction and time-series data from every cycle drags it toward formal research, and doing that may kill the very speed that makes it useful. Third, even a threadbare cycle produces something that doing nothing does not: a habit of trying things, and a team used to changing the way it works.

The first argument is the one worth arguing with, and it is also the easiest to overread. If predictions do exist and simply go unwritten, it is hard to explain why the rate of documented iterative cycles differs by more than three times between the group calling the method PDCA and the group calling it PDSA: a journal word limit does not distinguish between two names. But it needs saying how far that gap proves anything. It is an association observed in data, and Taylor and colleagues themselves write only that the difference in terminology may help to explain the level of compliance. The direction of causation stays open, and the reverse reading is no less plausible: people trained in the Deming line already understood step three as learning, and they are also the people who choose the word PDSA. If that is right, the name does not create the understanding, it is only a trace of an understanding already there. This piece has no data to choose between the two readings, and does not need to: both lead to the same thing to do at step one.

The other two arguments are weaker. Writing down what you expect before a test asks for no statistician at all, it costs one extra sentence in the minutes, so treating a prediction as equivalent to formal research sets up a false choice. The argument about building a habit is sound, and it is also the sentence that lets a programme going nowhere excuse itself indefinitely: as long as the numbers refuse to move, one can always say the thing being built is a culture of improvement. The distinction sits in one concrete question: over the past six months, has that team dropped or scaled back any change because it failed to produce what was predicted. A team that has never dropped or revised anything after measuring has either predicted correctly every time, which is rare enough to be suspicious in itself, or has never really checked.

Why this is useful: It hands you a question to carry into the next improvement meeting, and the question is not "which steps have we finished". Ask instead: before we started, what did we predict would happen, and how far is today's figure from that. If nobody can answer the first half, the meeting under way is a sign-off, whatever the calendar invitation calls it, and everything the team concludes today is reasoning backwards from the result. The habit to build is small: each time you set out to try a change, add one line to the minutes recording what you expect to happen and why you believe it. That one line is where a repeating loop starts to differ from a learning one.

How to Apply It

PDCA works by forcing a change to take the shape of a prediction that can be wrong, so the ways of running it that hold up are the ones that keep that prediction written down beforehand and brought back out afterwards, instead of letting step three drift into a session confirming that the plan was followed.

  • Write down the result you expect before you start, with the reason you believe it: As a number where one can be measured, and otherwise a directional prediction is still testable, along the lines of error type A will drop while type B holds steady, or the bottleneck will shift from intake to the lab; the reason is the part that carries the value, because when the result diverges it is the reason that tells you which assumption just failed, whereas a bare prediction with no reasoning behind it tells you only that you missed
  • Keep the first cycle small enough that being wrong is cheap: One clinic session, one production shift, five patients, three orders; small scale here buys the freedom to be wrong several times in a short stretch, and a cycle run across a whole department for six months buys you exactly one lesson at the price of six months
  • Separate "have we done it" from "did it turn out as predicted": These two get folded into the same meeting and the first always swallows the second, because it is easier to answer and because answering it makes everyone feel lighter; if they have to share a meeting, ask the second one before anybody gets a chance to report progress
  • Know in advance what it cannot do: PDCA tests a change for you, it does not find you a change worth testing, so running the cycle on a bad idea still yields nothing more than a carefully measured bad idea; nor does it reach anything outside the authority of the team running it, and a team ordered to improve continuously within a boundary where it can change nothing will learn very quickly how to write good-looking minutes

Summary

Key Point: Turning through all four steps does not by itself make PDCA produce understanding. Understanding appears only when a change is forced to take the shape of a prediction that can be wrong, so that step three has something to compare against and the place where the predicted figure meets the real one becomes the place where learning happens, whether the two diverge or match. The most counterintuitive part sits in the name itself: Deming, the man whose name is attached to this cycle, repeatedly rejected the term PDCA, warning that the English word check means to hold back, and settled his own version as PDSA with Study at step three. What looks like a quarrel over wording turns out to travel with measurable differences in practice: in Taylor's review, the share of articles describing linked iterative cycles was more than three times higher among those using the word PDSA than among those using PDCA. And the evidence on how the method is actually practised, within the healthcare scope this piece was able to check, falls well short of its reputation: fewer than one in ten articles recorded an explicit prediction. To find out whether a team is really running PDCA, do not read their minutes. Ask one question: what change have they recently dropped or revised because it failed to produce what was predicted.
