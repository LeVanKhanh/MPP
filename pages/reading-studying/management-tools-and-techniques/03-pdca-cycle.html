PDCA Cycle

A cycle that never says beforehand what it expects is not a learning cycle, just a meeting cut into four parts

Updated: October 9, 2026

Definition

What Is the PDCA Cycle

The PDCA cycle is a four-step improvement loop: plan, do, check, then act on what the check turned up, and back to the start. Each turn tests one change, usually a small one, and what this turn shows decides what the next one tries. A company canteen that wants to throw away less rice can run a full turn in a week: write down that cooking to the number of meals booked by ten in the morning, instead of to yesterday's headcount, will roughly halve the rice thrown out, because most of the waste falls on days when many staff are away on business; try it at lunch, weigh the leftover rice every day, then set the weighed figure beside the predicted one to decide whether to keep, adjust or drop the booking rule.

The cycle was not born with those four letters. In 1939 Walter Shewhart published Statistical Method from the Viewpoint of Quality Control, setting out a three-step process in a straight line: specification, production, inspection. [1] Shewhart himself wrote that the three had to run in a circle instead of a straight line, and that read as steps of the scientific method, specification, production and inspection correspond to hypothesising, carrying out an experiment, and testing the hypothesis. [2] In 1950 Deming took a revised version of the cycle to Japan and presented it at an eight-day seminar run by the Japanese Union of Scientists and Engineers. His version had four steps, design, production, sales and research, and became known as the Deming wheel. According to Masaaki Imai, it was Japanese executives who recast that wheel into PDCA in 1951. Imai does not say who did it or how, and according to Moen and Norman, as of 2010 nobody had claimed the revision and nobody had disputed his account. [2]

This piece uses the name PDCA because it is the more familiar one, while the mechanism rests on Deming's PDSA and the people who carried it forward, where the role of prediction is spelled out most clearly; most of the empirical evidence below is also recorded under the PDSA name. What the piece criticises is how the loop tends to be run in practice.

Why We Need It

A support team keeps having to escalate too many tickets to second line. They meet and decide to build a triage checklist for incoming work; that is Plan. They use the checklist for a month; that is Do. At the end of the month they sit down and confirm that everyone used it; that is Check. They write the checklist into the standard process; that is Act. All four steps done, nobody skipped anything, and a month later that team still knows nothing more about why tickets get escalated.

PDCA usually gets introduced under the heading of continuous improvement, and that heading is accurate while leaving out the hard part. The gap is in step three, and it is there because step one already had one. The real mechanism of PDCA is that it forces a change to take the shape of a prediction that can be wrong. A Plan step done properly does not stop at writing down what will be done and by whom; it writes down the result you expect together with the reason you believe it, as a number wherever a number can be had. Only then does step three have work to do: set what happened beside what was predicted. Where those two meet is where the loop produces understanding of the team's own assumptions, and it produces it in both directions. Diverge, and something has slipped: an assumption, the way the change was carried out, the measurement, or the context; because the reasoning was written down in advance, the team knows which of these to check first. Match, and the theory used to make the prediction has survived one more test, enough to be worth carrying somewhere else, though not enough to say the change caused it: a trend already running, another change happening at the same time, or plain measurement error will each produce a matching figure. Neither direction happens without something said in advance.

In 1991 Ronald Moen, Thomas Nolan and Lloyd Provost added an explicit requirement for prediction to the planning step, and stated that step three is where observed data is set against the prediction to serve as the basis for learning. Their argument reaches past the single test: knowing that a change worked once will not do, because what has to be built is the ability to predict whether it will work under the different conditions you will meet later. [2] This is also a part that often goes missing when cycles are run for real: in a review of PDSA projects in healthcare, only 4 of the 47 articles that could be assessed on this criterion recorded an explicit prediction. [3]

What Are Its Limits

PDCA tests a change for you, it does not find you a change worth testing, so running the cycle on a bad idea still yields nothing more than a carefully measured bad idea. Nor does it reach anything outside the authority of the team running it, and a team ordered to improve continuously within a boundary where it can change nothing will learn very quickly how to write good-looking minutes.

The next weakness sits in the name itself, and it was Deming who pointed it out. He never accepted the PDCA cycle as his own, and he said so more sharply as the years went on. He warned audiences that the PDCA version is frequently misread, since to check something is also to hold it back. In a letter to Ronald Moen dated 17 November 1990 he put it in one line: be sure to call it PDSA, not the corruption PDCA. The following year, sent a chart labelled plan-do-check-act, he replied that what he had been shown was not the Deming cycle, that he did not know the source of it, and that how PDCA ever came into existence he knew not. Earlier, at a roundtable held at the US Government Accounting Office, asked how the PDCA cycle and the Deming circle related to each other, he answered flatly that the two bear no relation. In 1993 he settled his own version in The New Economics and called it the Shewhart cycle for learning and improvement, that is, PDSA. [2] Study in place of Check is not a word chosen because it sounds better, it is a plain statement that the job of step three is to learn rather than to sign off. That does not mean the Check step in descriptions of PDCA is only a sign-off; it means the word Check leaves that reading open, and the word Study closes it.

Examples

The first example sets two ways of running the same cycle side by side. The other two are healthcare programmes that outsiders went back and counted: one that scored every PDSA cycle run by front-line teams over three years, and a patient safety programme in four hospitals set beside eighteen hospitals that stayed outside it.

Two Clinics Run the Same Cycle, One of Them Learns Something

An illustrative scenario: two clinics in the same system, both seeing patients wait too long, both deciding to try booked appointment slots instead of walk-in order.

The first clinic writes into its plan: roll out appointment slots from the start of next month, run by the senior nurse. A month later they meet, see that average waiting time has come down, and write the new arrangement into the standard process. The second clinic writes a different sentence into its plan: we expect average waiting time to fall from forty minutes to under twenty-five, because most of the current wait comes from patients bunching up early in the morning. They run a two-week test in a single clinic session. The real figure comes back at thirty-eight minutes. That is barely a move, and because they had written down why they believed the prediction, they knew straight away what to go and check: patients were not bunching early at all, they were bunching after their test results came back, and the bottleneck sat in the lab rather than in the appointment book.

The first clinic ran all four steps and recorded a small drop in waiting time, with nothing yet to show whether the drop came from the appointment book or from something else entirely. The second ran all four steps, improved nothing in those two weeks, and walked away with something the first did not have: knowing where the bottleneck is.

Four Hundred and Twenty-One Cycles, Seven That Met the Standard

In 2019 Christopher McNicholas and colleagues published a retrospective study in BMJ Quality & Safety, reviewing all 421 PDSA cycles run by 39 front-line improvement teams across three annual rounds of projects, six teams in 2009, sixteen in 2010 and seventeen in 2011. They scored every cycle against six predefined measures of fidelity. The result: only 2% (7/421) of cycles met all six. [4]

On the one measure that maps directly onto the mechanism above, an explicit prediction written down, the figures across the three rounds ran like this: 0% in round one, 3.3% in round two, 17.5% in round three, and 12.2% overall. [4] Two things are worth taking from that at once. In the first round, not a single one of those cycles wrote down a prediction. And the figure climbed over exactly the years in which the support team changed how it trained people, how it selected projects, and how it coached them on the ground. That is not enough to say which form of support worked, but it is enough to see that skipping the prediction is a habit that can shift, and not necessarily a permanent defect in working people.

The limits are clear and the authors state them: this is one improvement programme, and the interview work reached only the three quality improvement staff who spanned all three rounds, so the account of why the figure climbed is much thinner than the counting behind it.

Four Hospitals in the Programme, Eighteen Outside It, and Only Two Measures Pulled Apart

The Safer Patients Initiative was a patient safety programme funded by the Health Foundation, bringing quality improvement methods into British hospitals under the mentorship of the Institute for Healthcare Improvement, an American organisation devoted to improving healthcare quality. The way the programme chose to bring safe practice onto the wards was the PDSA cycle: test a change on a small scale, refine it, and only then widen it, with charts over time tracking each measure. In 2011 Amirta Benning and colleagues published an independent evaluation of the first phase in the BMJ, comparing four participating hospitals, one in each country of the United Kingdom, against eighteen control hospitals in England. [5]

Quality of care did improve over the eighteen months of the study. The trouble is that most measures improved almost equally in both groups. The programme made a difference on one of eleven dimensions of the staff questionnaire, organisational climate, by 0.08 points on a five-point scale, and on one type of clinical process, the recording of respiratory rate, which rose from 37% to 78% in the participating hospitals against 40% to 69% in the controls. Prescribing errors and the quality of medical histories showed no additional improvement. Mortality leaned in favour of the participating hospitals without reaching statistical significance. [5]

PDSA cycles were only one component of a large intervention, so this result cannot isolate the contribution of the cycle, and it does not prove PDCA is useless. What it shows is narrower: a well-funded programme, methodically run and shipped with an independent evaluation, can still move little beyond the group that stayed outside. Nor did that group stand still. The evaluators considered that the improvement in vital-sign monitoring across both groups fitted a general improvement across the NHS at the time, and noted that half the control hospitals were due to join the programme's second phase, so may already have been performing above average. That makes "did it improve" far too easy a question to ask of an improvement programme; the one worth asking is whether it improved beyond what the group outside managed anyway.

Some Research on the PDCA Cycle

The value of PDCA is usually told through projects reporting on themselves, and in one review of 120 published projects, almost every one said it had achieved an improvement. The two reviews below set that self-report beside the question of how those projects actually ran the cycle. All four empirical sources in this piece come from healthcare, so they show one pattern of drift in application clearly while saying nothing about how common that drift is in manufacturing, software or anywhere else.

The study: In 2019 Søren Valgreen Knudsen and colleagues published a systematic review in BMC Health Services Research of PDSA-based quality improvement projects published in peer-reviewed journals in 2015 and 2016: 120 projects from 18 countries, most of them from the United States and the United Kingdom. They asked two questions: what results did the projects report, and did they carry out the four core features of the method, namely iterative cycles, continuous data collection, small-scale testing, and a rationale for why the change was expected to work. [6] Five years earlier, Michael Taylor and colleagues at Imperial College London had published a systematic review in BMJ Quality & Safety on how the PDSA method is applied in healthcare. They built a theoretical framework first, five key features of the method drawn from the descriptions of the people who originated it: iterative cycles, prediction-based testing of change, initial small-scale testing, use of data over time, and documentation. They then scored every piece of peer-reviewed literature they could find against that framework. From 942 records they went to 409 after removing duplicates, and 73 articles met the inclusion criteria. [3]

Findings: Of Knudsen's 120 projects, 118 (98%) reported an improvement. Yet only 32 (27%) had set a specific quantitative aim beforehand and reached it; 68 (57%) set no quantitative aim at all and still recorded an improvement, and 18 (15%) set an aim and missed it. Of the 72 projects documented well enough to assess, only 3 (4%) had all four features. Small-scale testing was the feature most often missing, present in 10 projects (14%); a rationale for why the change was expected to work appeared in 26 (36%). [6] In Taylor's review, fewer than 20% (14/73) of articles fully documented a sequence of linked iterative cycles, and of those 14 only 2 started with small tests and scaled up from there. Only 15% (7/47) used quantitative data at monthly or more frequent intervals to decide what the next cycle should be. On the central mechanism itself, only 9% (4/47) of articles recorded an explicit prediction of the outcome, which led the authors to conclude that PDSA cycles were largely not being used as learning cycles to test and revise a theory-based prediction. Across all five principles together, exactly 2 of the 73 articles complied. The most expensive figure sits elsewhere: among articles using the term "PDCA", 13.6% (3/22) described iterative cycles; among those using "PDSA", the figure was 44% (11/25). [3]

What it shows: Both reviews measure application and documentation; neither measures effectiveness. The 98% is improvement as reported by the projects themselves, and Knudsen and colleagues write that the fact that most projects reported good results may itself signal bias, so the claim that PDSA leads to improvement should be read with caution. [6] Taylor and colleagues write plainly that their study did not compare quality of application against reported outcomes, so it cannot conclude that better application produces better results; it only draws on the principles of the method to explain why that would be expected. Their scope is healthcare, and peer-reviewed English-language literature up to September 2012. They also raise publication bias themselves, running in two directions: research using PDSA that did not yield good results is less likely to be published, and journals are more accustomed to before-and-after studies than to work presenting data over time. [3] The two reviews come from different author groups, but Knudsen's criteria draw partly on the same body of literature that includes Taylor's paper, so they are better read as two consistent findings than as two fully independent checks. What they show, within exactly that scope: the distance between the method as described and the method as practised is very large, and two of the largest gaps sit at the prediction step and at testing small before scaling up.

The counter-case: Before settling this, the defence deserves a fair hearing, because it has at least three arguments that stand up. First, the review counts what got written into the article, and scientific papers are squeezed for length and shaped by journal habit; a team could perfectly well have discussed a prediction at the whiteboard and left it out of the manuscript. Second, the real strength of PDCA is that it is cheap and lowers the barrier: it lets a senior nurse try a change on a Tuesday without standing up a formal research design, without a statistician, without a budget, though how much approval is needed still depends on the level of risk and on local rules. Demanding a quantitative prediction and time-series data from every cycle drags it toward formal research, and doing that may kill the very speed that makes it useful. Third, even a threadbare cycle produces something that doing nothing does not: a habit of trying things, and a team used to changing the way it works.

The first argument is the one worth arguing with, and it is also the easiest to overread. At first glance, the more-than-threefold gap in documented iterative cycles between the group calling the method PDCA and the group calling it PDSA seems to cut against it, since a journal word limit does not distinguish between two names. But the two groups of articles differ in other ways too. Of the 31 articles using the term PDCA, 20 paired it with the FOCUS framework, a sequence of steps for selecting and analysing a process that comes before the cycle; and over time PDCA grew rarer while PDSA grew more common. [3] So the two groups may differ in period, in reporting standards and in type of project alike, and the gap does not rule out teams that predicted without writing it down. It is also only an association observed in data, and Taylor and colleagues themselves write only that the difference in terminology may help to explain the level of compliance. The direction of causation stays open, and the reverse reading is no less plausible: people trained in the Deming line already understood step three as learning, and they are also the people who choose the word PDSA. If that is right, the name does not create the understanding, it is only a trace of an understanding already there. This piece accepts the core of this argument: the 9% figure measures whether a prediction was written into the article, not whether the team ever thought of one. It has no data to choose between the readings, and does not need to: every one of them leads to the same thing to do at step one.

The second argument is half right. Writing down what you expect before a test asks for no statistician at all, it costs one extra sentence in the minutes, and the prediction need not be a number. Measurement is different: data over time takes real effort, and the investment in measuring should match the decision it has to support; a small change that is easy to reverse may need no more than a few counts before and after. The defence has a point about measurement, but treating a written prediction as equivalent to formal research still sets up a false choice. The argument about building a habit is sound, and it is also the sentence that lets a programme going nowhere excuse itself indefinitely: as long as the numbers refuse to move, one can always say the thing being built is a culture of improvement. One concrete question helps tell the difference, though it is not enough to grade a whole programme: over the past six months, has that team dropped or scaled back any change because it failed to produce what was predicted. A team that has never dropped or revised anything after measuring has either predicted correctly every time, which is rare enough to be suspicious in itself, or has only ever tried safe bets, or has never really checked; the third possibility is the reason the question is worth asking.

Why this is useful: It hands you a question to carry into the next improvement meeting, in place of "which steps have we finished". Ask instead: before we started, what did we predict would happen, and how far is today's figure from that. If nobody can answer the first half, the meeting under way is closer to a sign-off, whatever the calendar invitation calls it. The team can still spot an obstacle in implementation or a faulty measurement, but any explanation of causes it offers today is built after seeing the result, and is hard to separate from what it already wanted to believe. The habit to build is small: each time you set out to try a change, add one line to the minutes recording what you expect to happen and why you believe it. That one line is where a repeating loop starts to differ from a learning one.

How to Apply It

PDCA works by forcing a change to take the shape of a prediction that can be wrong, so the ways of running it that hold up are the ones that keep that prediction written down beforehand and brought back out afterwards, instead of letting step three drift into a session confirming that the plan was followed.

  • Write down the result you expect before you start, with the reason you believe it: As a number where one can be measured, and otherwise a directional prediction is still testable, along the lines of error type A will drop while type B holds steady, or the bottleneck will shift from intake to the lab; the reason is the part that carries the value, because when the result diverges it is the reason that points to which assumption to check first, whereas a bare prediction with no reasoning behind it tells you only that you missed
  • Keep the first cycle small enough that being wrong is cheap: One clinic session, one production shift, five patients, three orders; small scale here buys the freedom to be wrong several times in a short stretch, and a cycle run across a whole department for six months buys you exactly one lesson at the price of six months; this is also the principle most often dropped in Knudsen's review, practised by only 10 of 72 projects [6]
  • Separate "have we done it" from "did it turn out as predicted": These two get folded into the same meeting and the first tends to swallow the second, because it is easier to answer and because answering it makes everyone feel lighter; if they have to share a meeting, ask the second one before anybody gets a chance to report progress
  • Give the Act step the power to drop a change, not just to adopt it: If the change under test has already been decided on, the prediction at Plan becomes a formality, because being wrong changes nothing; this breaks down precisely in cycles opened to legitimise a decision already made, and the easiest sign to spot is a team that has run many cycles without ever dropping or scaling back a single change

Applying It to a Scenario

The accounts payable team at a company of around 150 people approves claims for business travel expenses. Any claim with missing or incorrect supporting documents goes back to the claimant, and every return adds a few more days before the traveller gets their money back. In September there were 120 claims, and 37 (31%) were returned at least once. According to the return notes, 24 returns were over supporting documents, 9 over the wrong cost code, and 4 for other reasons. The sales department, which travels most, had 46 claims, of which 11 (24%) were returned over documents and 3 (7%) over the wrong cost code. The figures in this scenario are illustrative.

  1. Ask before choosing a change: The chief accountant has a quick word with five people in sales whose claims had just been returned. Four say they do not know what kind of invoice a taxi or a hotel requires. The team picks a cheap change to test: attach to the claim form a one-page list of the documents required for each type of expense
  2. Write the prediction and the reason into the minutes before running it: In sales, the share of claims returned over documents will roughly halve, from 24% to around 12%, because most returns happen when claimants do not know what to submit. The share returned over the wrong cost code will stay around 7%, because the list says nothing about codes. The second half is a directional prediction, and it can be tested just like the first
  3. Run it small: Apply it only in sales, for the first half of October. Other departments keep the old form. The accountants record every return under its specific reason, including reasons nobody predicted
  4. Set the real figures beside the predicted ones before asking who did what: Sales had 24 claims in the two weeks: 4 (17%) were returned over documents, down but not as far as predicted, and 2 (8%) over the wrong code, almost unchanged, just as predicted. Over the same two weeks the other departments had 38 claims, 7 (18%) returned over documents, level with their September figure of 13 out of 74, so there is no sign of the whole company improving in that period; but with a few dozen claims on each side, and departments that travel in different ways, that is not enough to credit the drop to the list. That 22 of the 24 claims came with the list ticked shows it was used, not that it worked, so the team does not treat it as a result
  5. Ask why it diverged, starting from the reason written down: Before concluding that their understanding was wrong, the team rules out two other possibilities: was the list really used, and it was, since 22 of 24 claims included it; did the accountants record return reasons the same way as in September, and they did, since the same two approvers used the same note format. All 4 document returns did have an invoice, but the invoice carried the wrong company name or tax code, mostly issued at hotels and restaurants. Rereading the September notes, 7 of the 11 sales returns were of the same kind. The reason behind the prediction held only for the smaller part: people did know they needed an invoice, but at the counter they misread or could not remember the company's details. Genuinely missing documents, the problem the list was aimed at, went from 4 out of 46 claims to 0 out of 24
  6. Act on the revised theory: Keep the list and roll it out company-wide, because it is cheap, carries almost no risk, and the early results fit the effect expected on missing documents. Open a second cycle aimed at invoices with wrong details: print a small card with the company's name, tax code, address and invoicing email for staff who travel. The second-cycle prediction, written in advance: in sales, the share of claims returned over invoices with wrong details falls from about 15% to under 5% over the following two weeks. From now on the return notes record "missing document" and "document with wrong details" separately

After one two-week cycle, the share of sales claims returned over documents fell from 24% to 17%, less than predicted. What the cycle was worth sits in that gap: it showed that more than half the returns came from invoices with the wrong company details, something a list of required documents never touches. It also has to be said plainly that seven of those eleven cases were already sitting in the September notes, and had the team sorted the return reasons carefully at the very first step, they would have found them without running a test. What the prediction contributed was to send the team back to those notes, because the real figure did not fit the reason they had written down. With 24 claims in two weeks, a difference of one or two claims is enough to move the rate by several percentage points, so this is a signal, not yet evidence, and only the second cycle will show whether the direction holds. The cycle learned something because of four conditions: the prediction and its reason were written before the test, the return notes were specific enough to reread, there was a group in the same period that did not get the change, and the review meeting asked how far the real figure was off before asking who had done what. Had the minutes said only "add a document checklist, chief accountant responsible", the 17% would have been read as a success, and the invoices with wrong details would have stayed exactly where they were.

Summary

Key Point: Turning through all four steps is not enough for PDCA to tell a team which of its assumptions hold. For that, a change has to take the shape of a prediction that can be wrong, so that step three has something to set beside the real figure, whether the two diverge or match. The most counterintuitive part sits in the name itself: Deming, the man whose name is attached to this cycle, repeatedly rejected the term PDCA and settled his own version as PDSA, with Study at step three. On practice, within the healthcare scope this piece was able to check, Knudsen's review shows almost every project reporting an improvement. Taylor's review shows fewer than one in ten articles recording an explicit prediction. To get a quick read on whether a team is really running PDCA, do not start with their minutes. Ask one question: what change have they recently dropped or revised because it failed to produce what was predicted.
