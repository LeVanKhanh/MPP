A3 Problem Solving

Anyone can copy the template, and the template is the one part of A3 that needs no teaching

Updated: October 9, 2026

Definition

What Is A3 Problem Solving

A3 problem solving sets out the whole handling of a problem on one side of a sheet of A3 paper. The number of boxes and their names vary by organisation, some versions run to seven steps and some to ten, but the frame usually covers background, current condition, target, root cause analysis, countermeasures, implementation plan and follow-up. The name comes straight from the paper size and stands for nothing. An A3 about late deliveries from a warehouse reads left to right in a few minutes: the left half says what the problem is, how bad it is and why it happens; the right half says what will be done, by whom, and when it will be checked, and every line on the right traces back to a line on the left.

The history of the tool is more specific than most people using A3 today realise. Art Smalley, in the A3 entry of the encyclopaedia of the Toyota system, writes plainly that A3 as practised today did not grow up from the shop floor but was deliberately built at Toyota headquarters in the late 1970s as a tool for developing managers. [1] The Lean Enterprise Institute's account, written with Isao Yoshino who was there, gets more specific: in 1978 Masao Nemoto set up the Kanri Nouryoku programme, Kan-Pro for short, the Management Capabilities Programme. Nemoto set it up because he saw management capability slipping among office managers. It ran for two years and covered roughly two thousand managers. There were two presentation sessions a year, in June and December, and every manager had to present using an A3. The detail most worth keeping is the question period: senior officers attended to ask about the problem the presenter faced and about how he had arrived at his answer, rather than to establish who was at fault. [2] Both sources are people inside the lean movement rather than historians standing outside it, and they are not independent of each other: the Art of Lean entry cites the Lean Enterprise Institute piece directly. Their dates and figures are specific; how they read the meaning of those dates should be taken as the word of a party with a stake in the story.

There is another detail repeated everywhere: that A3 was chosen because it is the largest sheet that will feed through a fax machine. That detail has a secondary source: the PLoS One study cited later says exactly that when it introduces A3, though without a citation of its own for the claim. [3] This piece has not found a primary document confirming it. Even if it is true, the fax only answers why that paper size, not why Toyota turned that sheet into a way of developing people; Kan-Pro answers the second question, and the two answers do not exclude each other. The thing to watch for is the moment the fax story gets told as though it explained the whole tool, because then it shrinks a decision about training people into an accident of office equipment. The Lean Enterprise Institute itself records that A3 sheets had been used by many Japanese companies since the 1960s to tell a PDCA improvement story, which means the sheet predates Nemoto's programme by a good margin. [2]

Why We Need It

A department head brings thirty slides to the weekly operations meeting about rising late deliveries: charts by week, by region, by driver, photos of the warehouse, a list of everything already tried. The meeting runs an hour, everyone comes away feeling the problem has been examined closely, and the decision is to add a delivery shift. Nobody notices that among those thirty slides a great many show where orders are late and not one says why. Thirty slides leave plenty of room for a gap in the reasoning to sit unnoticed between the charts.

Compression does the opposite. One of the mechanisms the people who built A3 talk about, and the one this piece takes as its spine, is forcing whoever owns the problem to shrink their entire thinking onto one side of one sheet, so that the limit on area makes the places where that thinking still has holes easier to see. An A3 has far less room to hide in than thirty slides. When the analysis box holds three lines at most, those three lines either carry you across to the countermeasures box or they do not, and a reader who looks has a chance of seeing which. But one side of a sheet still has room for a wrong argument that happens to be tidy, so compression only works when somebody reads the sheet and asks back. The real output of an A3 is not the sheet, it is the sequence of revisions those questions force on the author, each one pushing the author back down to where the problem is happening to fetch something they did not have. The sheet is only a pretext concrete enough to get two people to sit down together.

Compression is not the only mechanism attributed to A3 either, and this piece takes it as the spine because it explains the hardest part: why two organisations using the same template produce completely different results. The PLoS One study cited later names three more. A3 is built on the PDCA cycle, with the left half of the sheet handling the planning and the right half the doing and checking. It runs through nemawashi, the practice of going round each person involved to gather agreement before anything is settled, which the authors themselves call the key to a good A3. And it is the support for dialogue between a mentor and a learner. [3] Those three do not compete with compression, they are other things the same sheet does. Those are the claimed mechanisms, and how far this piece can test them has a short answer, not far enough: the best result on A3 itself that it found, a 78% fall in door movements reported by the authors for one Dutch operating theatre and followed for twelve months, comes from a study with no control group. [4]

What Are Its Limits

A3 does not produce people who know how to ask, it only gives such people somewhere to ask. An organisation with nobody who understands the work well enough to push back on the analysis box will simply acquire one more kind of form. For problems whose causes lie outside the authority of the person holding the pen, A3 can still make the problem clear and carry it to someone with the power to act, but it cannot put the countermeasure in place by itself, and a very sharp piece of analysis can still end in a very small countermeasure. Compression has its own price too: what gets cut to fit one side of a sheet is usually the conditions, the exceptions and how certain each figure is, so the detailed data still has to be kept somewhere else for a reader to check when needed.

The expensive part of the tool is also the part most easily lost. The cheap part of A3, the template, always arrives first and always stays; the expensive part, someone who understands the work reading and asking back over several rounds, can be cut at any moment and nobody notices straight away, because however a sheet was produced, the finished version looks much the same.

Examples

The first example sets two ways of producing the same A3 side by side. The other two are places that used A3 for real and then counted: a laboratory animal facility where every author had a coach of their own, and a hospital that trained more than a hundred and fifty staff and then went through eighteen A3s to see which had followed every step.

An A3 Signed Off in Forty Minutes

An illustrative scenario: a workshop where the rework rate has been climbing all quarter, and a supervisor asked to produce an A3 about it. He opens the template the company hands out. Background: the project name. Current condition: a chart cut from the monthly report. Root cause analysis: one line, operators are not following the procedure. Countermeasures: run refresher training. Follow-up: continue to monitor. Seven boxes filled, the sheet signed, the sheet pinned to the board, and next quarter the rework rate sits exactly where it was. He took forty minutes to fill it in, because every box could be answered from what he already knew before he opened the file. Not one box made him leave his desk.

The other version starts from the same sheet. His manager reads it, stops at the analysis box, and asks one question: how do you know it is the operators not following the procedure. He cannot answer, because he has not been down to look. He goes down, stands through three shifts, and finds that two machines are calibrated to two different parameter sets, because one of them had a sensor replaced and nobody updated the parameter sheet. The analysis box is rewritten. The countermeasures box drops the refresher training entirely and puts in its place a step tying the parameter sheet into the sensor replacement procedure. The current condition box has to be corrected too, because the old figure lumped both machines together.

The second A3 took three weeks and four revisions. The two sheets look alike in layout and form, same template, same number of boxes, same type size, and a careful reader can of course tell the contents apart. What the final version does not show is the road to it: one was finished in forty minutes out of what the author already knew, the other is the product of three weeks of watching and four rounds of being asked again.

From Zero Per Cent to Ninety, in a Laboratory Animal Facility

In 2013 James Bassuk and Ida Washington published a study in PLoS One on the use of A3 at the laboratory animal facility of the Seattle Children's Research Institute. They ran A3 in a ten-step version, and the first problem they went after was veterinarians not being available in time when an animal fell ill during working hours. [3]

Before the work, the share of sick-animal cases receiving veterinary input within two hours was 0%. In the first audit, from 15 February to 15 March 2012, it was 82.4%, or 98 of 119 cases. In the second audit, from 15 May to 15 June, it reached 90.6%, or 77 of 85. The rise from baseline to the first audit was statistically significant at p below 0.0001; the further rise in the second audit was only a trend, at p of 0.11. [3]

The most valuable detail in the study is not one of those numbers. Nine A3s were completed in the 2012 financial year, and all nine ran inside a teaching and coaching system that had been set up for them: a course of four classroom sessions of one to two hours each, spread over two to three months, and every A3 author was assigned a coach of their own, drawn from the internal improvement consultant pool or from colleagues who had finished the course earlier. The coach helped scope the problem and guided the process of observing where the work happens and gathering the data. The paper also describes the revision loops properly: where a countermeasure fell short of expectations, the author revised it and tested again. [3] This is the closest this piece gets to evidence for the mechanism set out at the start, and it is worth saying exactly how close that is: the study describes a good result occurring inside a coaching system, it does not compare a coached group against an uncoached one, so it cannot isolate what the coaching contributed.

The remaining limits are equally clear: a single unit, no comparison group, and the people who ran the A3s are the people who wrote the paper. One more detail is worth keeping, a piece of self-criticism inside the paper itself: the authors record that the A3 form they used does not leave enough white space to write down the outcomes they observed. [3] The template, the part that gets copied fastest, turns out to be the part that made recording the results harder.

Eighteen A3s in One Hospital, and the Ones That Skipped Steps

At a hospital in the Rocky Mountain region of the United States, Durward Sobek, a professor of industrial engineering at Montana State University, worked with a colleague, an experienced trauma care nurse, to adapt Toyota's A3 into a nine-step routine: observe the current process, draw the current state, find the root cause by asking "why" five times, develop countermeasures, draw the target process agreed with those affected, plan the implementation, discuss all of the above with those affected, implement, then collect follow-up data to compare against the target that had been set. They tried it in the cardiology department, built a seven-week introductory course, tested it again in the pharmacy, and over a year and a half trained more than 150 staff in person. Manimay Ghosh, who had spent six months at the hospital and facilitated many problem-solving efforts there, then came back and selected 18 cases that had used A3 across many departments, from the laboratory, intensive care, registration and transport to finance, read each A3 alongside the related emails and minutes, interviewed the authors, and scored which steps had been completed, partly completed or skipped. [5]

Ten cases completed all nine steps, and their improvement ranged from 77% to 100%. The cases that skipped at least one step improved markedly less: the text of the paper gives 17% to 60%, while the results table lists two further cases at zero. The authors conclude that following every step went with greater improvement, and that skipping even one step markedly reduced effectiveness. [5]

That figure needs careful reading. The working paper does not say which measure the improvement percentages were calculated on, reports no statistical test behind the word "significant", and the person who selected and scored the cases had also led and taught the method at that same hospital. The direction of cause is open as well, since an easy problem is also easier to see through every step. What the case shows is the shape of the failure when A3 spreads across a whole organisation: the template reaches everyone, while the steps get followed in full on some sheets and skipped on others, and the working paper does not say why.

Some Research on A3 Problem Solving

Within the search this piece was able to run, no systematic review focused specifically on the effectiveness of A3 turned up. There are individual studies on A3 itself, and there is a review of the whole family of methods A3 belongs to. Those two give answers that pull in different directions, so both are worth reading.

The study: In 2014 Frederique Simons and colleagues published a study in the International Journal for Quality in Health Care using A3 to reduce door movements in an operating theatre, a factor the literature has long treated as capable of raising the risk of surgical site infection. This study did not measure infection rates; it measured door movements. The baseline survey covered 8,009 operations across 26 specialisms over eight months at a university medical centre in the Netherlands, but the A3 intervention ran in exactly one orthopaedic operating theatre, not across that baseline sample. A multidisciplinary team ran an A3 built on the PDCA cycle, and door movements were counted by a digital counter rather than by hand. [4]

One level up, in 2016 John Moraros, Mark Lemstra and Chijioke Nwankwo at the University of Saskatchewan published a systematic review in the same journal that puts the question straight into its title: do lean interventions in healthcare actually work. They filtered on methodological quality and kept 22 papers, of which 4 measured health outcomes only, 3 measured both health and process, and 15 measured process only. The Simons study is one of those 22. [6]

Findings: In the Simons baseline survey, door movements per operation ranged from 0 to 555, averaging 24 per hour across all 26 specialisms. In the orthopaedic theatre that received the intervention, the authors report a 78% decrease, to an average of 4 per hour, call that decrease sustainable, and record that the effect of the changes was monitored over the course of twelve months. [4] Those two averages do not reproduce the 78%: going straight from 24 to 4 is a fall of about 83%, and the 24 is the figure for all 26 specialisms rather than for this theatre alone. The abstract gives no separate baseline for the orthopaedic theatre, so this piece keeps the authors' figure and records the discrepancy. Across Moraros's 22 papers, Lean had no statistically significant association with patient satisfaction, nor with health outcomes; one study of six million patients found no effect on thirty-day post-discharge mortality. On process outcomes such as patient flow and safety there were benefits, but inconsistent ones. On cost the authors are blunt: they could not identify a single paper with real quantitative data on savings. [6]

The review also analysed the Lean programme of the province of Saskatchewan separately, from publicly available data rather than from the peer-reviewed literature. With their condition attached, if the numbers reported are accurate and true, the authors work out that the province spent $1,511 on Lean for every dollar it saved: the numerator is thirty-five million paid to outside consultants plus roughly seventeen million a year for internal kaizen offices, the denominator is the reported saving of $56,934.26. A telephone survey of 1,500 nurses brought back all 15 indicators, from supplies and workload to patient safety, negative and statistically significant on the authors' analysis; but it is a survey of perceptions, commissioned by the Saskatchewan Union of Nurses, the union of the very people surveyed. [6]

What it shows: Simons is a study of A3 itself rather than of Lean in general, and it has three things the laboratory animal facility example does not have at once: a measure taken by machine, a large reduction, and a follow-up long enough to say something about whether the gain held. The limits keep their familiar shape: one theatre, one hospital, no control group, so it cannot separate what A3 contributed from what an entire multidisciplinary team paying attention to one number for a year contributed.

The scope of Moraros is Lean in healthcare, not A3, and certainly not A3 outside healthcare. The authors name three limits themselves: most of the pooled studies had no control group, the outcomes were too varied to permit a meta-analysis, and the effect of Lean could not be separated from changes running alongside it. Their closing line carries no hedge: the evidence to date simply does not support the belief that lean interventions improve healthcare quality. Read at its proper scope, this is a statement about the state of the evidence on lean interventions in healthcare, and it says nothing about what one A3 with a coach beside it can do. It is also worth remembering that Moraros is not the last word, and that the Simons study sits inside their own 22: a review that concludes negatively can still contain individual cases that came out well, and there is no contradiction in that.

The counter-case: The strongest argument for the defence is that the review measures the wrong level. What was evaluated in Saskatchewan and in most of those 22 studies is an implementation programme: consulting contracts, training courses, quotas of improvement events, reporting machinery. What the people who built A3 were talking about is a relationship between two human beings around a sheet of paper, repeated long enough for one of them to learn a way of thinking. Measuring the first and concluding about the second is common, and it is unfair in both directions: it never gives A3 a chance, and it lets every failed programme excuse itself by saying it never did the original properly.

That defence holds up to a point, and the point has a clear edge. This piece accepts that Moraros says nothing about an A3 with a coach. But if a method only works where there are already managers who know which question to ask and have time to ask it, then that is a real condition, and it has to sit in the budget rather than off the books. The hospital case in the Examples section shows that, even at the scale of one hospital and after more than a hundred and fifty people had been trained, following every step was uneven: eight of the eighteen A3s selected for comparison skipped steps. The working paper does not say why, so this is a sign of uneven adherence, not evidence of missing coaches. The worst news for the defence lies elsewhere: nearly fifty years after Kan-Pro, within the search this piece was able to run, no large-sample study has isolated the effect of the coaching.

Why this is useful: It changes what you look at when somebody shows off a folder of A3s. Do not count the sheets and do not read the countermeasures box first. Ask two questions about any one of them: how many revisions has this sheet been through, and which revision made the author leave their desk to go and get more data. If the answers are one and none, especially when the whole folder looks like that and the problems are far from simple, what that organisation has propagated is most likely a form. If you need a set of criteria for reading the content of the sheet itself, Myers and colleagues built a 23-item tool for scoring A3 proposals for healthcare improvement projects, and 12 raters scoring six sheets reached very high overall agreement, an intraclass correlation of 0.89. The tool shows that different readers can score the same A3 alike; it does not yet show that a higher-scoring sheet leads to more improvement. [7] And if you are the one reading other people's A3s, the test lands on you: the last time you read an A3, did you ask a question the author could not answer on the spot.

How to Apply It

Of the mechanisms attributed to A3, this piece takes compression as its spine: A3 works by squeezing the thinking of whoever owns the problem onto one side of a sheet so that the remaining holes are easier to see for somebody else, so the ways of running it that hold up are the ones that protect the questioning, the most expensive part and the easiest to cut, rather than protecting the template.

  • Give the A3 to the person living with the problem, not to whoever has spare capacity: Compression only helps when the person compressing is the one who will bear the consequences of a wrong analysis; hand it to an improvement specialist sitting in another room and the sheet still comes out looking fine, except that every hard question lands on someone with no way of going to find the answer
  • Watch the number of revisions rather than the number of finished A3s, but do not turn it into a target: An organisation reporting two hundred sheets in a quarter at an average of one revision each is describing a paperwork machine; twenty sheets at four revisions each is worth asking about. The catch is that the moment it becomes a target, one revision splits neatly into four versions, so the real question lies elsewhere: of those revisions, how many changed the hypothesis about the cause, how many forced someone to go and gather more data, how many led to a countermeasure being dropped. Hard problems naturally need more rounds than easy ones, so this number is a signal to go and investigate rather than a score to award
  • Make the current condition box answer the question actually being asked: This is the cheapest box to cheat on and the one that determines every box after it. The problem is not whether the data already existed or was gathered fresh: in software or digital operations, transaction logs are often far more trustworthy than an afternoon of watching. The problem is that a chart cut from the monthly report has been aggregated and smoothed to suit the purposes of whoever built that report. So the test is two questions: has the author been to where the work happens to check that the figure matches reality, and does the data in hand have enough detail to isolate the thing actually under suspicion
  • Let the reader ask how the author reached the answer before asking who is responsible: At Kan-Pro, senior officers attended the question period to ask about the problem and about how the presenter had arrived at the answer; when the reader of an A3 is also the person who rates the author's performance or approves their budget, the author will write to get through rather than to expose the holes, and compression fails right there

Applying It to a Scenario

The HR department of a software company of around 200 people takes on a few new starters every month. In the third quarter 14 people joined, and 9 of them reached the end of their first day still without a working laptop or without being able to log in to the systems they work in; the slowest waited until day four. A person counts as ready when, by the end of their first working day, they have a working laptop and can log in, and that definition stays fixed for every later measurement. The head of HR is asked to write an A3 about it, with the operations director as its reader. The figures in this scenario are illustrative.

  1. Write the background box in terms of what the problem costs: Every day of waiting is a day's salary paid to someone who cannot yet work, plus a session in which the person meant to induct them sits waiting too. The nine late starters lost 17 working days between them in the quarter, which is why this is worth the operations director's time to read
  2. Take the current condition from where the work happens: The first draft pastes in the HR report figure, "9 of 14 not ready". The operations director asks: not ready at which step. The head of HR traces each case through the IT request system and redraws the path of a laptop request: HR raises the ticket, IT receives it, procurement approves if stock has run out, IT sets the machine up, the line manager confirms access rights
  3. Compare the late group with the on-time group before naming a cause: The second draft gives the cause as "IT overloaded". The reader's next question: did the five who were ready go through the same IT team. They did. Set side by side, all 5 on-time starters had their ticket raised at least seven working days before their start date, while 8 of the 9 late starters had theirs raised within the last three days, because tickets were only raised once the contract had been signed. The ninth was late because stock had run out and the purchase request was waiting for approval. That is not enough to strike out "IT overloaded": an overloaded team would also manage early tickets and miss late ones. So the head of HR opens the ticket log as well: from receipt to handover, IT takes about five working days on a laptop ticket, in the on-time group and the late group alike, and IT's backlog in the quarter was no higher than the quarter before. The cause is rewritten: laptop tickets are raised later than the time IT needs to process them. "IT overloaded" is not the main explanation at the current hiring rate, but it will need checking again if hiring rises sharply
  4. Take the countermeasure round to each party before settling it: The third draft proposes raising the laptop ticket as soon as the candidate accepts the offer by email, and keeping two spare laptops in stock. Before presenting it, the head of HR meets IT, procurement and legal separately. Legal is concerned about creating accounts for someone who has not yet signed a contract, so the parties agree that the laptop is prepared early while the account is only activated once the contract is signed, which IT does on the morning of day one. The fourth draft comes out of those meetings, before any formal meeting has taken place
  5. Give the follow-up box a date, a name and a number: The target for the fourth quarter is at least 90% of new starters ready, under exactly the definition used in the third quarter. IT records the handover time for each person, and the head of HR checks at the end of every month. Alongside it goes a process measure: the number of working days between the ticket being raised and the start date, because that is where the countermeasure acts directly; this measure says whether the countermeasure is being carried out, while the readiness rate says whether it works
  6. Count again, then check what else changed at the same time: In the fourth quarter 13 people joined, and 12 were ready on their first day, about 92%, meeting the target. Tickets were raised on average 9 working days before the start date. The remaining person was late because they accepted the offer only two days before starting, exactly the kind of case the countermeasure cannot reach. During the quarter IT kept the same staff, but one fewer person joined than in the previous quarter, so IT's load was slightly lighter
  7. Make the countermeasure the standard, and record the kind of case it does not reach: Raising the laptop ticket as soon as the candidate accepts goes into the recruitment process, owned by the recruiter handling the file. Late acceptances are logged in the follow-up box as a case type of their own; with only one such case so far, the A3 opens no countermeasure for it yet, and will if the same kind of case recurs next quarter

This A3 went through four drafts. Two revisions came from the reader's questions, and one from legal while the countermeasure was being taken round. The share of new starters ready on their first day, measured under the same definition in both quarters, went from 5 of 14 to 12 of 13. With one quarter and a few dozen people, that is a signal rather than proof, and it rests on three conditions: the author was the person living with the problem and traced each case personally, the reader asked how the cause had been reached rather than who was to blame, and the countermeasure was taken round to each party before it was settled. Had the sheet stopped at the second draft, the countermeasure would most likely have been to ask for more IT staff, and most of the late cases would still have been late, because the tickets would still have arrived late.

Summary

Key Point: Of the mechanisms attributed to A3, this piece takes compression as its spine: squeezing the thinking onto one side of a sheet makes the holes easier to see, but only when someone reads it and asks back, so what has value is the sequence of revisions rather than the final sheet. The most counterintuitive part sits in the history: the A3 sheet dates from the 1960s, while what Toyota built in 1978 was A3 as a management development programme, where the question period aimed at how the presenter reached the answer rather than at who was to blame. The evidence on A3 itself consists of individual cases without control groups; Moraros's review of Lean in healthcare, the level of method A3 sits within, found no improvement in outcomes. Between those two levels lies the part this piece has not found anyone measuring, and it happens to coincide exactly with the expensive part, so the dependable way to run A3 is to protect that part: the author lives with the problem, the reader asks how the answer was reached, and the number of revisions is read as a signal to go and ask further rather than as a target.
