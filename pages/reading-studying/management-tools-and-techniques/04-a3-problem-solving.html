A3 Problem Solving

Anyone can copy the template, and the template is the one part of A3 that needs no teaching

Published: August 11, 2026

Definition

A3 problem solving sets out the whole handling of a problem on one side of a sheet of A3 paper. The number of boxes and their names vary by organisation, some versions run to seven steps and some to ten, but the frame usually covers background, current condition, target, root cause analysis, countermeasures, implementation plan and follow-up. The name comes straight from the paper size and stands for nothing. Picture a workshop where the rework rate has been climbing all quarter, and a supervisor asked to produce an A3 about it. He opens the template the company hands out. Background: the project name. Current condition: a chart cut from the monthly report. Root cause analysis: one line, operators are not following the procedure. Countermeasures: run refresher training. Follow-up: continue to monitor. Seven boxes filled, the sheet signed, the sheet pinned to the board, and next quarter the rework rate sits exactly where it was.

Nothing is wrong with any of those seven boxes. What is wrong is that the sheet was never questioned by anyone. One of the mechanisms the people who built A3 talk about, and the one this piece takes as its spine, is compression: it forces whoever owns the problem to shrink their entire thinking onto one side of one sheet, and the limit on area is what exposes the places where that thinking still has holes. An A3 has nowhere to hide. When the analysis box holds three lines at most, those three lines either carry you across to the countermeasures box or they do not, and a reader sees which immediately. But compression only works when somebody reads the sheet and asks back. The real output of an A3 is not the sheet, it is the sequence of revisions those questions force on the author, each one pushing him back down to where the problem is happening to fetch something he did not have. The sheet is only a pretext concrete enough to get two people to sit down together. That is the claimed mechanism, and how far this piece can test it belongs in the research section; the short answer is not far enough.

The history of the tool sides with that reading, and it is more specific than most people using A3 today realise. Art Smalley, in the A3 entry of the encyclopaedia of the Toyota system, writes plainly that A3 as practised today did not grow up from the shop floor but was deliberately built at Toyota headquarters in the late 1970s as a tool for developing managers. [1] The Lean Enterprise Institute's account, written with Isao Yoshino who was there, gets more specific: in 1978 Masao Nemoto set up the Kanri Nouryoku programme, Kan-Pro for short, the Management Capabilities Programme. Nemoto set it up because he saw management capability slipping among office managers. It ran for two years and covered roughly two thousand managers. There were two presentation sessions a year, in June and December, and every manager had to present using an A3. The detail worth pausing on is the question period: senior officers attended to ask about the problem the presenter faced and about how he had arrived at his answer, rather than to establish who was at fault. [2] One thing has to be said about both of those sources: they are people inside the lean movement, not historians standing outside it. Smalley worked in the Toyota system and now consults; the Lean Enterprise Institute is the movement's own institute, and Yoshino is a Toyota man recounting Toyota. The two accounts are consistent with each other, but consistent is not the same as cross-checked: the Art of Lean entry cites the Lean Enterprise Institute piece directly in its own reference list, so this is one source drawing on the other rather than two independent tests landing on the same result. Their dates and figures are specific; how they read the meaning of those dates should be taken as the word of a party with a stake in the story.

There is another detail repeated everywhere: that A3 was chosen because it is the largest sheet that will feed through a fax machine. That detail does have a source, and the source sits in this piece's own reference list. The PLoS One study cited later says exactly that when it introduces A3, though without a citation of its own for the claim. [3] What this piece could not find is a primary document confirming that the fax machine really was the historical reason for the paper size. More important than whether it is true is that two questions keep getting folded into one: why that paper size, and why Toyota turned that sheet into a way of developing people. The fax, if true, only answers the first. Kan-Pro answers the second, and the two answers do not exclude each other. The thing to watch for is the moment the fax story gets told as though it explained the whole tool, because then it shrinks a decision about training people into an accident of office equipment. The Lean Enterprise Institute itself records that A3 sheets had been used by many Japanese companies since the 1960s to tell a PDCA improvement story, which means the sheet predates Nemoto's programme by a good margin. [2]

Compression is not the only mechanism attributed to A3, and this piece takes it as the spine because it explains the hardest part: why two organisations using the same template produce completely different results. The study cited later names three more. A3 is built on the PDCA cycle, with the left half of the sheet handling the planning and the right half the doing and checking. It runs through nemawashi, the practice of going round each person involved to gather agreement before anything is settled, which the authors themselves call the key to a good A3. And it is the support for dialogue between a mentor and a learner. [3] Those three do not compete with compression, they are other things the same sheet does. The limit on area exposes the holes, PDCA organises the testing, and nemawashi and dialogue turn one person's analysis into a decision several people own.

Examples

The three examples below run in a slightly odd sequence: a situation you can picture showing how A3 fails, then a measured study showing it working when someone is coaching, then a province-wide programme showing what it costs to copy a whole method at scale.

An A3 Signed Off in Forty Minutes

Carry on picturing the supervisor from the opening. He takes forty minutes to fill in all seven boxes, because every one of them can be answered from what he already knew before he opened the file. Not one box makes him leave his desk.

Now picture the other version. His manager reads the sheet, stops at the analysis box, and asks one question: how do you know it is the operators not following the procedure. He cannot answer, because he has not been down to look. He goes down, stands through three shifts, and finds that two machines are calibrated to two different parameter sets, because one of them had a sensor replaced and nobody updated the parameter sheet. The analysis box is rewritten. The countermeasures box drops the refresher training entirely and puts in its place a step tying the parameter sheet into the sensor replacement procedure. The current condition box has to be corrected too, because the old figure lumped both machines together.

The second A3 took three weeks and four revisions. The two sheets look alike in layout and form, same template, same number of boxes, same type size, and a careful reader can of course tell the contents apart. What the final version does not show is the road to it: one was finished in forty minutes out of what the author already knew, the other is the product of three weeks of watching and four rounds of being asked again.

From Zero Per Cent to Ninety, in a Laboratory Animal Facility

In 2013 James Bassuk and Ida Washington published a study in PLoS One on the use of A3 at the laboratory animal facility of the Seattle Children's Research Institute. They ran A3 in a ten-step version, and the first problem they went after was veterinarians not being available in time when an animal fell ill during working hours. [3]

Before the work, the share of sick-animal cases receiving veterinary input within two hours was 0%. In the first audit, from 15 February to 15 March 2012, it was 82.4%, or 98 of 119 cases. In the second audit, from 15 May to 15 June, it reached 90.6%, or 77 of 85. The rise from baseline to the first audit was statistically significant at p below 0.0001; the further rise in the second audit was only a trend, at p of 0.11. [3]

The most valuable detail in the study is not one of those numbers. Nine A3s were completed in the 2012 financial year, and all nine ran inside a teaching and coaching system that had been set up for them: a course of four classroom sessions of one to two hours each, spread over two to three months, and every A3 author was assigned a coach of their own, drawn from the internal improvement consultant pool or from colleagues who had finished the course earlier. The coach helped scope the problem and guided the process of observing where the work happens and gathering the data. The paper also describes the revision loops properly: where a countermeasure fell short of expectations, the author revised it and tested again. [3] This is the closest this piece gets to evidence for the mechanism set out at the start, and it is worth saying exactly how close that is: the study describes a good result occurring inside a coaching system, it does not compare a coached group against an uncoached one, so it cannot isolate what the coaching contributed.

This is one of the few examples this piece found that uses A3 properly, has before and after figures, and states its significance levels. The limits are equally clear: a single unit, no comparison group, and the people who ran the A3s are the people who wrote the paper. The detail most worth noticing is a piece of self-criticism inside the paper itself: the authors record that the A3 form they used does not leave enough white space to write down the outcomes they observed. [3] The template, the part that gets copied fastest, turns out to be the part that made recording the results harder.

A Province Buys the Whole Method for Forty Million Dollars

In 2012 the government of Saskatchewan in Canada signed a forty million dollar contract with John Black and Associates, an American consultancy, to roll out Lean, meaning the whole family of lean production methods of which A3 is one piece, across the province's health system. By late 2014 the province gave ninety days' notice and ended the contract early on 31 March 2015, by which point its value had been trimmed to about thirty-five million. Health Minister Dustin Duncan explained that the province had reached the point of being able to carry on by itself and would cut costs by doing so; ending early saved roughly three million more. [4]

One thing has to be said straight away: that report does not mention A3 once, and it describes no specific lean tool the programme taught. The only detail it gives about content is that more than two hundred health system staff had received "Lean leader certification" and would go on to train others. [4] So this piece does not claim Saskatchewan taught A3; if A3 was in the package, the available data still does not let anyone separate out its contribution. This example does not say A3 is useless, and it does not say the forty million was money thrown away, because no measurement here licenses that. What it offers is a hypothesis worth testing rather than a conclusion. At Seattle, nine A3s ran with one coach per author. In Saskatchewan, forty million bought access to a method and a team to teach it to the health system of an entire province. Both were paying for training capacity and implementation support, so the difference does not lie in whether such things can be bought. What the available source does not tell us is how deep Saskatchewan's coaching went, and that gap is precisely why what follows is only a hypothesis. The hypothesis runs like this: as scope grows, the depth of coaching falls away faster than the template propagates, because handing a template file to a whole province costs almost nothing extra, while the ratio of coaches to authors grows expensive in exact proportion to scale. But two cases are not enough to settle that this is the difference that mattered: they also differ in kind of organisation, kind of problem, objective, political context and how results were measured, and no measurement here separates the coaching ratio from any of those.

Some Research on A3 Problem Solving

Within the search this piece was able to run, no systematic review focused specifically on the effectiveness of A3 turned up. There are individual studies on A3 itself, and there is a review of the whole family of methods A3 belongs to. Those two give answers that pull in different directions, so both are worth reading.

One Operating Theatre, Door Movements Down Seventy-Eight Per Cent

The study: In 2014 Frederique Simons and colleagues published a study in the International Journal for Quality in Health Care using A3 to reduce door movements in an operating theatre, a factor the literature has long treated as capable of raising the risk of surgical site infection. It has to be said at once that this study did not measure infection rates; it measured door movements. The baseline survey covered 8,009 operations across 26 specialisms over eight months at a university medical centre in the Netherlands, but the A3 intervention ran in exactly one orthopaedic operating theatre, not across that baseline sample. A multidisciplinary team ran an A3 built on the PDCA cycle, and door movements were counted by a digital counter rather than by hand. [5]

Findings: Door movements per operation ranged from 0 to 555, averaging 24 per hour at baseline. In the theatre that received the intervention, the figure fell 78%, to 4 per hour. The authors call that decrease sustainable, and record that the effect of the changes was monitored over the course of twelve months. [5]

What it shows: This is a study of A3 itself rather than of Lean in general, and it has three things the earlier example does not have at once: a measure taken by machine, a large reduction, and a follow-up long enough to say something about whether the gain held. The limits keep their familiar shape: one theatre, one hospital, no control group, so it cannot separate what A3 contributed from what an entire multidisciplinary team paying attention to one number for a year contributed.

Twenty-Two Studies, and One Very Ugly Division

The study: In 2016 John Moraros, Mark Lemstra and Chijioke Nwankwo at the University of Saskatchewan published a systematic review in the International Journal for Quality in Health Care that puts the question straight into its title: do lean interventions in healthcare actually work. They filtered on methodological quality and kept 22 papers, of which 4 measured health outcomes only, 3 measured both health and process, and 15 measured process only. The Simons study just cited is one of those 22. [6]

Findings: Across the 22 peer-reviewed papers, Lean had no statistically significant association with patient satisfaction, nor with health outcomes; one study of six million patients found no effect on thirty-day post-discharge mortality. On process outcomes such as patient flow and safety there were benefits, but inconsistent ones. On cost the authors are blunt: they could not identify a single paper with real quantitative data on savings. [6]

The Saskatchewan material sits in a separate block, and the authors state plainly that they analysed and reported it separately, from publicly available data rather than from the peer-reviewed literature. There they run a division of their own, with the condition they set attached: if the numbers reported are accurate and true, the province spent $1,511 on Lean for every one dollar it saved. The numerator is thirty-five million paid to outside consultants plus roughly seventeen million a year for internal kaizen offices, fifty-one million over three years; the denominator is the reported saving, $56,934.26. It is a denominator that small that sends the quotient through the roof. The same block holds a telephone survey of 1,500 nurses, commissioned by the Saskatchewan Union of Nurses from an outside polling firm: all 15 indicators came back negative and statistically significant on the authors' analysis. Those 15 indicators cover far more than engagement, taking in the quality and availability of supplies, time available for direct patient care, workload and stress, patient safety, the ability to meet professional nursing standards, time for training, and staff morale and engagement. Two things to hold in mind while reading that figure: this is a survey of perceptions rather than an experimental design, and the body that commissioned it is the union of the very people surveyed. [6]

What it shows: The scope of this review is Lean in healthcare, not A3, and certainly not A3 outside healthcare. The authors name three limits themselves: most of the pooled studies had no control group, the outcomes were too varied to permit a meta-analysis, and the effect of Lean could not be separated from changes running alongside it. Their closing line carries no hedge: the evidence to date simply does not support the belief that lean interventions improve healthcare quality. Read at its proper scope, this is a statement about the state of the evidence on lean interventions in healthcare, and it says nothing about what one A3 with a coach beside it can do. It is also worth remembering that Moraros is not the last word, and that the Simons study above sits inside their own 22: a review that concludes negatively can still contain individual cases that came out well, and there is no contradiction in that.

The counter-case: The strongest argument for the defence does not come from attacking the review's method, it comes from the charge that the review measures the wrong level. What was evaluated in Saskatchewan and in most of those 22 studies is an implementation programme: consulting contracts, training courses, quotas of improvement events, reporting machinery. What the people who built A3 were talking about is a relationship between two human beings around a sheet of paper, repeated long enough for one of them to learn a way of thinking. Measuring the first and concluding about the second is common, and it is unfair in both directions: it never gives A3 a chance, and it lets every failed programme excuse itself by saying it never did the original properly.

That defence holds up to a point, and the point has a clear edge. If a method only works where there are already managers who know which question to ask and have time to ask it, then that is a real condition, and it has to sit in the budget rather than off the books. The cheap part of A3, the template, always arrives first and always stays; the expensive part can be cut at any moment and nobody notices straight away. The worst news for the defence lies elsewhere: nearly fifty years after Kan-Pro, within the search this piece was able to run, no large-sample study has isolated the effect of the coaching.

Why this is useful: It changes what you look at when somebody shows off a folder of A3s. Do not count the sheets and do not read the countermeasures box first. Ask two questions about any one of them: how many revisions has this sheet been through, and which revision made the author leave his desk to go and get more data. If the answers are one and none, what that organisation has propagated is a form. And if you are the one reading other people's A3s, the test lands on you: the last time you read an A3, did you ask a question the author could not answer on the spot.

How to Apply It

Of the mechanisms attributed to A3, the one this piece takes as its spine is compression: squeezing the thinking of whoever owns the problem onto one side of a sheet so that the remaining holes show up in front of somebody else. If that is right, the ways of running it that hold up are the ones that protect the questioning, the most expensive part and the easiest to cut, rather than protecting the template.

  • Give the A3 to the person living with the problem, not to whoever has spare capacity: Compression only helps when the person compressing is the one who will bear the consequences of a wrong analysis; hand it to an improvement specialist sitting in another room and the sheet still comes out looking fine, except that every hard question lands on someone with no way of going to find the answer
  • Watch the number of revisions rather than the number of finished A3s, but do not turn it into a target: An organisation reporting two hundred sheets in a quarter at an average of one revision each is describing a paperwork machine; twenty sheets at four revisions each is worth asking about. The catch is that the moment it becomes a target, one revision splits neatly into four versions, so the real question lies elsewhere: of those revisions, how many changed the hypothesis about the cause, how many forced someone to go and gather more data, how many led to a countermeasure being dropped. Hard problems naturally need more rounds than easy ones, so this number is a signal to go and investigate rather than a score to award
  • Make the current condition box answer the question actually being asked: This is the cheapest box to cheat on and the one that determines every box after it. The problem is not whether the data already existed or was gathered fresh: in software or digital operations, transaction logs are often far more trustworthy than an afternoon of watching. The problem is that a chart cut from the monthly report has been aggregated and smoothed to suit the purposes of whoever built that report. So the test is two questions: has the author been to where the work happens to check that the figure matches reality, and does the data in hand have enough detail to isolate the thing actually under suspicion
  • Know in advance what it cannot do: A3 does not produce people who know how to ask, it only gives such people somewhere to ask; an organisation with nobody who understands the work well enough to push back on the analysis box will simply acquire one more kind of form, and it also cannot reach problems whose causes lie outside the authority of the person holding the pen, which is where a very sharp piece of analysis still ends in a very small countermeasure

Summary

Key Point: Of the mechanisms attributed to A3, the one this piece takes as its spine is compression: one side of a sheet leaves no room for thinking that still has holes, so the holes show, and every time they show the author is forced back down to where the work happens to fetch something he did not have. But that compression only works when somebody reads and asks back, so what has lasting value is the sequence of revisions, and the final sheet is only its trace. The most counterintuitive part sits in the history, and it splits in two. The A3 sheet came first: Japanese companies were using it to tell improvement stories from the 1960s. What was built at Toyota headquarters in 1978 was A3 as a management process, inside a programme training two thousand managers, where the question period aimed at how the presenter reached his answer rather than at who was to blame. As for the story that the A3 size was chosen because of fax machines, it has a secondary source but no primary document, and even if it is true it only answers why that paper size, not why that sheet became a way of developing people. The evidence tilts hard at both ends. At the level of individual cases, A3 works and holds: a laboratory animal facility took a response rate from zero to over ninety per cent, and a Dutch operating theatre cut door movements by seventy-eight per cent with the effect monitored over twelve months. At the level of the whole family of methods, the review of Lean in healthcare found no improvement in outcomes and found nurses' ratings falling, even though that same operating theatre study sits among the twenty-two papers in that very review. Between those two levels lies the part nobody has measured, and it happens to coincide exactly with the expensive part.
