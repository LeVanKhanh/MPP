FMEA

The list of failure modes and the actions that follow are the real output; the number multiplied out in the last column does not guarantee that the list is complete or right

Published: October 10, 2026

Definition

What Is FMEA

Two weeks before a supermarket chain launches two-hour delivery, the project team walks through every step of the new process, from the moment a customer taps "order" to the moment the bag reaches the door, and at each step asks the same run of questions: how can this step fail, what happens to the customer if it does, why would it fail, and if it fails, who notices before the customer does. At the step where the driver collects the bags, one line goes on the sheet: picking up the wrong bag from two orders packed side by side, so that two customers both get the wrong shopping, one of whom may have ordered formula for a newborn. That exercise is called FMEA, short for Failure Mode and Effects Analysis. What it produces is a table in which each row is one way one step can fail, together with its effect, its cause, and three scores for that failure mode.

FMEA differs from a fishbone diagram in the direction it looks: a fishbone diagram usually starts from a problem that has already happened and searches for possible causes, while FMEA starts from a process that has not yet failed and searches for the ways it could. It also differs from a risk matrix, a tool for scoring risks that have already been named on two axes, likelihood and consequence: FMEA generates its own list from the steps of the process, and adds a third axis, detection.

The tool was born in the US military. Military procedure MIL-P-1629, Procedures for Performing a Failure Modes, Effects and Criticality Analysis, dated 9 November 1949, used it as a reliability evaluation technique, to determine the effects of system and equipment failures. In the 1960s the aerospace industry and NASA used it on the Apollo programme; many other industries took it up later, most notably the automotive industry. [1] In healthcare, the toolkit published by the Institute for Healthcare Improvement (IHI) uses a nine-column table: process step, failure mode, causes, effects, three columns scored from 1 to 10 for likelihood of occurrence, likelihood of detection and severity, an RPN column holding the product of the three scores, and a column for actions. The detection column runs against intuition: 10 means very unlikely to be detected. [2]

The mechanism of FMEA has two halves, and they deserve very different degrees of trust. The first half is an orderly enumeration. Instead of asking in general terms "what are the risks on this project?", it makes the team ask "where does it break?" at each step in turn, so failures that sit in the hand-off between two departments, where nobody feels they own the problem, get a chance to be named. The second half is the scoring: each failure mode is scored separately on three axes, severity (S), occurrence (O) and detection (D), and in the traditional method the three scores are then multiplied into a risk priority number (RPN) used for ranking. The first half produces the tool's real output: the list of failure modes, and from it, the actions. The second turns three judgements into a number that looks like a measurement.

Why We Need It

If nobody sits down beforehand and asks "how does this break?", a new process gets its test run on its own customers. Where it fails, people find out from the first incident, and root cause tools only start working once the damage is done. For processes where a single failure is already too expensive, such as giving medicines to patients or delivering radiotherapy, waiting for the first incident to learn means letting patients pay for the lesson. FMEA reverses the order: the team has to imagine the failure modes first, while fixing them is still cheap, and IHI states that the tool is particularly useful for evaluating a new process before it is implemented, or a proposed change to an existing one. [2] The amount of work this drags onto the table is not small. A review of FMEA in paediatric hospital care brought together 18 studies that, between them, identified 220 high-risk failure modes and proposed 443 improvement actions, mostly in prescribing, dispensing and administering medicines. [3] The cost is also within reach of a small team: at one radiotherapy centre, a streamlined FMEA was finished in four one-hour meetings and about 75 hours of staff time. [4]

What Are Its Limits

FMEA can only find the failure modes the people in the room think of. Two teams analysing the same process independently can produce two lists that overlap far less than they differ, [5] and the type of incident reported most often in practice may not have a single row in the table. [6] A table with every column filled in is therefore easy to read as an inventory of risk, when it is only what one particular team managed to think of.

The scoring is weaker still than the listing. The three scores are three ordinal judgements: a severity of 10 says the consequence is worse than a 5, not that it is twice as bad, so multiplying the three together is a calculation these scales do not allow. Shebl and colleagues relied on that argument, together with Bowles's 2004 analysis, to conclude that the RPN is invalid. [6] The consequence can be counted directly: three scales from 1 to 10 give 1,000 combinations, but their products take only 120 distinct values, and 12 quite different combinations all give exactly 100, from a failure mode of the highest severity that is rare (S 10, O 2, D 5) to a minor one that happens all the time (S 2, O 10, D 5). The joint FMEA handbook from AIAG and VDA, the American and German automotive industry associations, published in 2019, has also stopped ranking by multiplication: it replaces the RPN with an Action Priority (AP) table. [7] The table looks up all three scores together and assigns each failure mode a high, medium or low need for action, and the handbook still lets an organisation set its own values for the table instead of using the suggested one. [8] Dropping the multiplication does not make the three scores any more accurate. It only stops treating their product as a measurement.

The worked example in IHI's toolkit, an eight-row FMEA of a medication dispensing process, shows how the multiplication reshuffles the order. These are illustrative scores from a teaching document, not data from a hospital, and the detection score is 5 on all eight rows, so the ranking is in effect decided by the other two columns alone. The last row deserves a pause: its effect reads that the patient may receive the wrong medication, the wrong dose or a dose by the wrong route, yet its severity score is 1. The document does not say why that row was given a 1, and the scale itself is described in two ways: the instructions ask how likely the failure mode is to cause severe harm if it occurs, while the key under the template says 10 is the most severe effect. [2] On a scale that a scorer can read either as a probability or as a consequence, two people scoring the same row may be answering two different questions.

Examples

The two examples below sit at the two ends of the same table: a client conference where an RPN cut-off removed the most serious row, and a radiotherapy centre where more than half of the failure modes came from forms that staff took home and filled in on their own.

The Row That Scored 80 and Fell Below the Line

An illustrative scenario: an events company is preparing a 400-guest client conference for an insurer. Three weeks before the day, the project lead brings the logistics team, the registration team, the technical team and the banqueting manager together to run an FMEA on the whole day, from the moment guests reach the gate to the end of the evening dinner. The team records 14 failure modes, scores each row on the three axes, and agrees on a familiar rule: any row with an RPN of 100 or more must have an action. Three rows clear the line: the registration queue running past fifteen minutes (S 4, O 8, D 4, RPN 128), the hall wifi collapsing during the livestream (S 5, O 6, D 4, RPN 120), and the keynote speaker missing a flight (S 7, O 3, D 5, RPN 105). The row "a guest with an allergy eats a dish containing the allergen" is scored S 10, O 2, D 4, comes out at 80, and falls below the line. The detection score of 4 rests on two things everybody believed: the registration form has a box for allergies, and the serving staff will ask.

On the day, the registration queue runs smoothly thanks to two extra desks. But the allergy list from the registration form was only exported to the registration team to print name badges; nobody passed it on to the kitchen, and at a standing buffet no member of staff stands there asking each guest. A guest who had declared a shrimp allergy eats a canapé with dried shrimp and has to be taken to A&E. The row was in the table, carrying the highest severity score on it. The table failed in two other places. The RPN cut-off let a row with S 10 drop beneath three rows with far milder consequences, because the multiplication let a low occurrence score drag the whole row down. And the detection score was based on a control that nobody had checked: the allergy box was real; the path from that box to the kitchen was not.

Thirty of Fifty-Two Failure Modes Came From Forms Taken Home

In 2014, Eric Ford and colleagues described in Medical Physics how they ran a streamlined FMEA of the external beam radiotherapy process, in which radiation is delivered from a machine outside the body, at an affiliated radiation oncology centre treating about 60 patients a day. The core team had seven people, led by a facilitator chosen in advance, with defined roles and goals for each phase. The whole analysis was finished in one month with four one-hour meetings, taking 55 hours of staff time plus 20 hours from the facilitator. The team identified 52 failure modes: 22 raised in the meetings, and 30 from worksheets that members took home and filled in on their own. More than half the list, in other words, was not born in the meeting room. The four top-ranked failure modes, all with an RPN above 150, received safety interventions: a delay in the film check, a missing pacemaker protocol or consent, critical structures not contoured on the planning images, and a pregnant patient being put through simulation, the scan used to plan the treatment, without the treatment team knowing about the pregnancy. [4]

Five years earlier, a team led by the same first author had run a full FMEA of the entire external beam radiotherapy service: a process map with 269 nodes, 127 failure modes, RPNs ranging from 2 to 160 on a scale that can reach 1,000, and 15 failure modes with an RPN of 75 or more taken forward for improvement and then built into practice. [9] The two analyses used two cut-offs, 75 and 150. The cut-off here is each team's own choice, sized to the amount of work the team can take on, not a constant of the method.

Both papers describe how the work was done and the list it produced; neither measures what happened afterwards. The abstract of the 2014 paper reports the effort and the failure modes, does not report whether incidents fell, and suggests using the reduction in RPN as a measure for future study. [4]

Some Research on FMEA

The evidence on FMEA leans to one side. There are a great many reports of a team using it and what they found; there are very few studies of whether anyone came to less harm afterwards. A recent review shows that imbalance clearly. A research group in England did something rarer: it tested the tool itself, by having two teams do the same job and comparing their results with each other, and with the incident records.

The study: In 2025, Färlin-Helin, Suominen and Tuominen published in the Journal of Patient Safety a scoping review, a type of review that maps what has been studied, of FMEA in paediatric and adolescent hospital care, searching six databases up to March 2024. It included 18 studies published between 2004 and 2022, assessing 21 processes, 11 of them in prescribing, dispensing or administering medicines. [3] Nada Shebl, Bryony Dean Franklin and Nick Barber tested the tool itself. In their 2009 paper, two multidisciplinary teams at two hospitals in the same NHS trust each ran an independent FMEA of the same process: the use of the antibiotics vancomycin and gentamicin. [5] A second paper, in 2012, tested the validity of the results in four ways, including a comparison of the failure modes with incidents involving these two drugs in the trust's incident database from January 2006 to January 2009. [6] In 2010, Laura Ashley and Gerry Armitage had a multidisciplinary team run an FMEA of chemotherapy administration, then scored the same failure modes in two ways: each member scoring alone with the scores averaged, and the whole team discussing until it agreed. [10]

Findings: Across the 18 paediatric studies, FMEA identified 220 high-risk failure modes and led to 443 improvement actions. Ten studies evaluated the impact of the improvements, mostly through audits, surveys or incident reports; four rescored the RPN after the changes, and one of these reported no failure mode above 100 afterwards, where previously some had been above 225. The review's authors themselves note that improvement actions had not been systematically reported. [3] In England, the two teams drew almost identical five-step process maps, but each identified 50 failure modes and only 17 appeared on both lists; the two teams' severity, detection and RPN scores differed markedly, so the failure modes were prioritised in two different orders. [5] The comparison with the incident records found 22 incidents that met the criteria, 13 of which matched failure modes the teams had named. Of the remaining 9, 7 were omitted doses, the type of incident reported most often in the trust's database. The teams also imagined consequences more severe than those the incident records show: the database incidents were all rated no harm or minor harm, while most severity scores in the FMEA fell between 5 and 7. The two figures measure different things, and the authors themselves point this out: the incident records capture harm that actually happened, the FMEA scores the potential consequences of failures that have not yet happened, and at times the teams assumed the worst outcome. [6] In the chemotherapy process, almost three-fifths of the 30 failure modes were scored differently by the two procedures, just over a third by a substantial margin, and under the cut-off used by HFMEA, the healthcare variant of FMEA, consensus scoring prioritised almost twice as many failure modes as averaging did. [10]

What it shows: FMEA is most trustworthy in the part where the process is mapped: in the English study, the steps both teams drew matched the steps the researcher observed on the wards. [6] The list of failure modes depends on who is in the room, and can miss precisely the type of incident reported most often. The scores depend on both who scores and how. On value, the studies cited here show that FMEA generates many failure modes and many actions, and some of them measured the impact after improvement. But when the measures are mostly audits, surveys, incident reports or rescored RPNs, and reporting is unsystematic, they are not enough to estimate with confidence how much FMEA reduces harm to patients. The scope of these studies is narrow: all are in healthcare, the English study covers only two teams in one trust, and the paediatric review does not appraise the quality of the studies it gathers. They say nothing about FMEA in the automotive industry, where AIAG and VDA have standardised the tool in a joint handbook.

The counter-case: Defenders of FMEA have two strong arguments. First, the value of FMEA lies more in the process than in the table: a team with many kinds of expertise that walks through a process together will understand it better, and Shebl and colleagues themselves call FMEA a useful tool for mapping and understanding a care process. [6] Ashley and Armitage go further and recommend consensus scoring precisely because it brings out where members score differently and why, so the team can talk it through. [10] Second, the weakness of the RPN lies in a calculation that sits behind the method, and a calculation can be replaced: the AIAG and VDA handbook has replaced it with a lookup table. [7] This article accepts the first part of the first argument: understanding the process is something FMEA does, and it is the only part the English study confirmed without qualification. But understanding the process does not guarantee a complete list: two teams that both mapped the process correctly still produced lists that differed for the most part, which means the list depends on who is in the room, and omitted doses stayed off both of them. That does not make the two lists of little value. An incomplete list is still worth making if it catches the serious failure modes and leads to action; it just must not be read as a complete one. Consensus scoring brings disagreement into the open, but Ashley and Armitage's study shows only that two scoring procedures give two results, not which one is closer to reality. The second argument the article accepts in full, for what it says: the Action Priority table removes the error of multiplying ordinal scales. It does not touch the two problems that come before the multiplication: the list depends on who writes it, and the scores depend on who gives them.

Why this is useful: It changes how you read a finished FMEA. A table with every column filled in, three scores and an RPN on each row, looks like a set of measurements; in fact it is what one particular team thought of and judged in a few particular meetings. Read as the best draft available, it is very useful. Read as an inventory of risk, it is dangerous, because anything not in the table will be taken as nothing to worry about. Before you sign off an FMEA, ask yourself two questions: which type of incident appeared most often in last year's incident log, and is it in the table; and which row has the highest severity score, and is it being dealt with or sitting below the line?

How to Apply It

FMEA works by making a team list in advance, step by step, the ways a process can fail, and then score each one on three axes, so using it well means putting the effort into making the list complete, and reading the three axes as three separate answers rather than folding them into one measurement. It fits best before a new process or a major change goes live, in places where a single failure is already expensive. For a problem that has already happened, root cause tools are a better fit.

  • Have each person write failure modes alone before the meeting, and invite the people who actually do each step: At the radiotherapy centre above, 30 of the 52 failure modes came from forms filled in individually. IHI advises including even people who touch only one stage, such as the couriers who carry medicines from the pharmacy to the wards, because they know steps that pharmacists and nurses never see. The meeting is for merging and challenging the list, not the only place to come up with it
  • Check the list against real incident data before scoring: Incident logs, complaint forms, error logs from the process itself or from one like it. At the English hospitals in Shebl's study, the type of incident reported most often matched no row in the table, and one pass through the incident records was enough to expose it. A brand-new process has no records of its own yet, so borrow data from somewhere that already runs a similar process
  • Read the severity column on its own, before looking at the RPN: Agree up front on a severity level, say 9 or 10, at or above which every row must have an action, whatever its RPN. The obvious fix is to lower the RPN cut-off to be safe, but that does not work: the two rows scoring 250 in IHI's example have severities of 10 and 5, so no cut-off can separate them. The AIAG and VDA Action Priority table avoids this because it looks up all three scores together
  • Measure effectiveness with real incidents, not rescored RPNs: An RPN rescored after a fix is the opinion of the team that just made the fix about its own action; it tells you how far the team believes in the action, not how the process has changed. IHI suggests adding up the RPNs for the whole process and setting a goal of halving the total; adding up products of ordinal scales only takes the number further from a measurement. Choose an outcome measure in advance, such as the number of incidents or an error rate per unit of activity, and measure before and after using the same definition

Applying It to a Scenario

A language centre with about 600 students, most of them children, is about to switch from collecting fees in cash at the front desk to bank transfers by QR code, starting next term. The management sets two goals for the first term: no student locked out of their learning account while their parents have already paid, and fewer than 2% of transactions needing manual reconciliation. The figures in this scenario are illustrative.

  1. Fix the scope and split it into steps: From sending the fee notice to opening the learning account, in five steps: send the notice with a QR code, parents make the transfer, the bank confirms receipt, the system matches the transaction to a student using the transfer reference, open the learning account and send a receipt. Whether the fees are reasonable is out of scope
  2. Build the team around the steps, including people outside the office: The accountant, the receptionist, the person who runs the system, a form teacher, and two parents, because the second step is done by parents and no member of staff can see it from their side
  3. Everyone writes failure modes alone before the meeting: That gives 31 failure modes, 19 after merging duplicates. Three were raised only by the parents: one transfer covering two children, grandparents paying from an account in another name, and writing the reference out of habit, like "hoc phi be Na" (fees for little Na), instead of the student code
  4. Check against data from somewhere already running a similar process: The centre has no bank transfers yet, but a sister branch on the same system has been taking QR payments for three months: 1,150 transactions, 64 of them needing manual matching, about 5.6%. Of those 64, 41 were due to a missing or wrong code in the reference, 15 to a single transfer for several students, and 8 to underpayment. Underpayment was not among the 19 rows; the team adds it as row 20
  5. Anchor each scale, score the three axes by discussion with a reason for every score, then read the severity column on its own: Severity 9 and 10 are reserved for failure modes in which parents lose money they cannot get back; 8 is when a student is shut out of class even though the family has paid in full; below 5 is a nuisance that costs no more than an extra phone call or resending something. Occurrence is scored from the branch's data, with 8 meaning it happens every week: a wrong code in the reference gets an 8 because it is the most common reason for manual matching, 41 of 64 transactions, about three a week. A detection score has to say who, specifically, will spot the failure, at which step, and whether before or after it does harm. The table below shows the six rows the team took forward for action. By RPN, only the locked-account row is above 100. By severity, the row for transfers into the old account stands level with it, even though its RPN is only 64. The team agrees: every row with a severity of 8 or more must have an action, plus any row with a cheap fix
  6. Choose actions aimed at the axis that makes each row dangerous: The locked-account row is dangerous because the failure is found late, so the actions aim at finding it early; this row is set out in full in the second table below. The old-account row has a severe consequence, so the QR code carries the new account number, the notice lists only one account, and the bank sets up an alert for any money paid into the old account. The two frequent rows, a wrong code in the reference and one transfer for several children, are stopped at the source: the QR code pre-fills the reference, and each family gets a single shared payment code. For underpayment, the system texts the outstanding amount the same day. The misaddressed-notice row is left under observation
  7. Measure with real outcomes, using the same definition as the branch: The manual-matching rate is the number of transactions needing manual matching divided by the total number of transactions, exactly as the branch calculates it. The number of students locked out after paying is counted from parents' complaints and checked against bank statements. The first term has 640 transactions, 11 of which need manual matching, about 1.7%, below the 2% target and below the branch's 5.6%. No student is locked out after paying; the three transactions that could not be matched in the first five days are all cleared by the accountant from the morning list. Two parents still pay into the old account, and the bank's alert catches both on the same day. A failure mode that was not on the list also surfaces: on the night the bank runs system maintenance, five transfers are confirmed six hours late, just as the system sends its automatic morning fee reminders, so five parents who have already paid still get a reminder. The team adds it as row 21 for next term. The comparison with the branch is not a controlled trial, since the two sites differ in their students and in who collects the money, so the gap cannot be put down entirely to the FMEA. What made the result possible was a list built from many people writing alone and checked against real data, serious rows dealt with regardless of their RPN, and results measured with real transactions rather than rescored points.

Summary

Key Point: FMEA makes a team walk through every step of a process and list in advance the ways it can fail, then score each one on three axes; its value lies in the list and the actions that come with it, while the RPN is only the product of three ordinal judgements and guarantees neither a complete list nor a correct order. The most counter-intuitive point is that both parts are less solid than a fully filled-in spreadsheet makes them look: in Shebl's study in England, two teams analysing the same process shared only 17 failure modes between two lists of 50, while the multiplication can rank a minor failure mode that happens all the time level with one of the highest severity. The trustworthy way to use it is therefore to have many people write the list separately and check it against real incident data, to read severity before reading the RPN, and to measure the results with real incidents instead of rescored points.
