Standard Operating Procedure (SOP)

A procedure that no longer matches the real work does not make people work the same way; it teaches them that procedures are only for reference

Published: October 10, 2026

Definition

What Is a Standard Operating Procedure

A standard operating procedure, usually shortened to SOP, is a document that records an agreed way of doing a repeated task, clear enough that someone else can follow it and get the same result. The US Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) defines SOPs as procedures that have been written down and tested, and are applied uniformly and consistently across the whole operation. [1] A laminated sheet hanging beside the autoclave in a university laboratory is an SOP: seven lines, in a fixed order, from checking the water level, loading the instruments so they do not touch the chamber wall, and choosing the 121°C programme, to waiting for the pressure needle to fall back to zero before opening the lid. A first-year student and a researcher with ten years in the lab, standing at that autoclave, both do exactly those seven things.

SOPs are easily confused with the standardised work of lean production. According to the Lean Enterprise Institute, standardised work is a precise procedure for each operator's work, built on three elements: takt time (the production rhythm that customer demand calls for), the sequence of operations within that rhythm, and the standard amount of work in process that keeps the line running smoothly; once posted at the workstation, it becomes the baseline for improvement. [2] An SOP needs none of those three elements: it records the sequence and the conditions of a task, and is not tied to the rhythm of a line. An SOP is not a checklist either. The FAA treats a checklist as a tool for reducing errors when configuring the aircraft for each phase of flight, which makes it a verification step inside a procedure, not the procedure itself. [1]

An SOP works by turning each person's way of doing a job into a shared standard, written down and visible. Once there is a shared standard, two people doing the same job will do it the same way, and when one of them does it differently, the difference shows, because there is now something to compare it with. The FAA describes exactly this for flight crews: coordination between crew members depends on a shared mental model of each task to be done, and that model rests on SOPs. [1] But the mechanism only runs while the document still matches the real work. When the document stands still while the work changes, what is being standardised is no longer a way of working, only a piece of paper, and the people doing the work learn that procedures are only for reference.

Why We Need It

Three shifts do the same job in three different ways, each learned from whoever showed them the ropes on their first day. Whether the result is good depends on who is on shift; how good a new starter becomes depends on who trained them; and when the result goes wrong, nobody knows which shift did something different, because no way of working has ever counted as the standard to compare against. An SOP blocks all three with one shared document. The change that comes with it is measured most clearly in the quality of the task itself: in Finland's helicopter emergency medical service, after two of its five bases introduced an SOP for prehospital anaesthesia, the rate of successful intubation on the first attempt rose from 87.3% to 96.5%, according to an observational study. [3] In aviation, the FAA writes that SOPs are widely recognised as the foundation of safe operations, and yet accidents and incidents keep happening because crews do not follow SOPs, particularly in critical phases of flight. [1]

What Are Its Limits

An SOP standardises exactly what is written into it, including when what is written is wrong. An SOP for treating septic shock published in 2006 included the drug rhAPC in its protocol [4]; in 2011, the manufacturer withdrew the drug from the market worldwide after a large trial failed to show that it reduced deaths. [5] The document makes everyone do the same thing. It does not check whether that thing is right.

The document can also drift away from the real work, even where the law requires the work to be done to the letter. Aircraft maintenance in the United States is one such place: federal regulations require maintainers to use the methods in the manufacturer's manual or methods acceptable to the regulator. Yet according to a 2022 FAA review, investigations estimate that not following procedures contributes to between 40.5% and 87% of maintenance-related events, depending on the database. When asked, only 18% of maintenance technicians agreed that the manual describes the easiest way to do a job, and only 13% agreed that the manual understands how they actually work; in a different survey, 50.4% said documentation sometimes misled them and 17% said it often did. [6] According to its abstract, Andrew Hale and David Borys's review of how safety rules and procedures are managed at work describes two ways of seeing them. The first treats procedures as fixed and complete limits, imposed from the top down on people at the front line, and treats every departure from them as negative behaviour to be stamped out. The second treats procedures as something constructed on the spot, by the people doing the work in their role as experts, and treats competence as the ability to adapt procedures to the variety of real situations. [7] An SOP written only from the first view has no route for information to travel back from the front line to the document, and the gap between paper and work grows without anyone seeing it.

The gaps in an SOP come in three kinds, and each is closed in a different way. Missing content, such as no step for stopping halfway through or no definition of "enough people", can be fixed by writing more carefully: the FAA counts abnormal and emergency situations as part of an SOP's scope. [1] Even for situations nobody foresaw, a good SOP can still set out when to stop, whom to tell, and who decides the next step. Missing updates, such as equipment that has been replaced while the document stays the same, need a change management process. The third kind belongs to the nature of a document: a signature on paper does not prove that the work was done; only checking where the work happens can prove that.

Examples

The two examples below show two ways an SOP comes loose from the real work: a coffee chain with a set of procedures so thick that nobody uses it, and an oil refinery in Texas with a startup procedure that no longer matched the very equipment it described.

Forty Pages in a Drawer, Nine Lines by the Espresso Machine

An illustrative scenario: a coffee chain with twelve shops is given a warning by food safety inspectors about how its espresso machines are cleaned. The operations team writes a 40-page opening-shift SOP with 112 steps, sends it to every shop, and requires each member of staff to sign that they have read it. Three months later the signatures are all there, and every shop's weekly self-inspection form is ticked from top to bottom. But at the busiest shop, the morning shift actually works from a nine-line sheet that the shift manager wrote and stuck beside the machine, listing exactly the things that must be done before the first cup is sold. The 40 pages sit in a drawer.

The nine-line sheet works well until the shift manager leaves and the sheet is lost one day when the counter is wiped down. Nobody left on the shift knows which page of the 40 holds the step of flushing water through the filter every morning. The step is skipped for two months, scale builds up inside the machine, and the water pump fails. The chain has an SOP, and it has proof that everyone has read the SOP. What actually held the way of working together in that shop was a sheet of paper nobody approved, and when the sheet went, the way of working went with it. The set of documents was written to answer the inspectors; the person behind the counter at six in the morning needed nine lines.

Nineteen Startups, One Within Limits

On 23 March 2005, while the raffinate splitter tower of the isomerisation unit at BP's Texas City refinery was being restarted after maintenance, operators pumped flammable liquid into the tower for more than three hours without draining any out, contrary to the instructions in the startup procedure. The 52-metre tower overfilled, the relief valves opened, liquid shot up out of a blowdown stack that was not connected to a flare, and the vapour cloud ignited. The explosion killed 15 people, injured 180, and caused financial losses of more than 1.5 billion dollars. [8]

The US Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board (CSB) did not stop at the fact that the operators had departed from the procedure. Computer data from 19 startups between April 2000 and March 2005, including the one that ended in the explosion, showed that the procedure called for filling to 50% on the level transmitter, but in 15 of the 19 startups the liquid level went above the range of the instrument; pressure went above the alarm threshold written into the procedure in 14; and only one startup kept both the liquid level and the pressure within limits. The operators had a reason for doing this: the liquid level in the tower swings sharply during startup, and losing the level could damage the furnace, so they deliberately kept it high. The procedure said nothing about those swings, was not updated when the relief valves were derated, allowed "not applicable" to be written against any step, and contained a step to "ensure adequate staffing" without defining "adequate"; a trainee had ticked that step even though no one had analysed anything. Management still certified every year that the procedures were current and complete. None of the 18 earlier startups was treated as abnormal or investigated. [8]

The CSB concluded that outdated and ineffective procedures had led operators to believe that procedures could be altered, or did not have to be followed, during startup. The explosion had many other causes that this article does not go into: a level transmitter giving false readings, a high-level alarm that did not sound, a blowdown stack installed in the 1950s and never connected to a flare, trailers placed too close, operators who had worked 12-hour shifts for 29 or more days in a row. [8] The part that belongs to the SOP is clear: a document that reality had left behind long ago, signed off every year, while the data showing it had been left behind sat ready in the computer.

Some Research on Standard Operating Procedures

The studies this article could find that directly measure what an SOP changes all come from healthcare, where mortality figures are already there to be measured. The two studies below measure the same kind of intervention, bringing an SOP into an emergency care process, using two designs of different strength. The second also exposes a risk that only became visible years later.

The study: In 2024, Ljungqvist and colleagues published in Acta Anaesthesiologica Scandinavica a retrospective observational study of Finland's helicopter emergency medical service, covering January 2012 to August 2019. Two of the five bases introduced an SOP for prehospital anaesthesia during 2015-2016. According to the abstract, patients were grouped by when they were anaesthetised, before, during or after implementation, giving 3,902 intubations without the SOP, 430 during implementation and 1,525 afterwards; the abstract does not say which group the cases at the three bases that did not implement it were placed in. The authors used logistic regression to check for confounding factors. [3] In 2006, Kortgen, Niederprüm and Bauer published in Critical Care Medicine a study in a ten-bed intensive care unit at a university hospital: 30 patients with septic shock treated under an SOP from September 2002 to December 2003, compared with 30 earlier patients, from January to August 2002. The SOP bundled four components: early goal-directed resuscitation, blood glucose control, stress-dose hydrocortisone, and rhAPC (drotrecogin alfa (activated)) for selected patients. [4]

Findings: In Finland, first-attempt intubation success rose from 87.3% to 96.5%. Mortality on the first day was lower during implementation (odds ratio 0.56, 95% confidence interval 0.37 to 0.81), was only a trend without statistical significance once implementation was complete (0.84, 0.68 to 1.04), and no statistically significant difference was found in 30-day mortality (1.10, 0.92 to 1.30). [3] In the intensive care unit, what was actually done changed sharply once the SOP was in place: hydrocortisone was given to 30 of 30 patients against 13 of 30, blood glucose on day four was on target in 26 of 28 against 13 of 25, and rhAPC was given to 7 of 30 against none. Mortality at 28 days fell from 53% to 27%. [4] In 2011, the placebo-controlled PROWESS-SHOCK trial in 1,680 patients with septic shock found 28-day mortality of 26.4% with rhAPC and 24.2% with placebo, a difference without statistical significance; the manufacturer withdrew the drug from the market worldwide. [5]

What it shows: In both places, bringing in an SOP came with changes that could be measured, but those changes sit on three levels that need to be kept apart. The first level is compliance, meaning whether what the document says is actually done: in the intensive care unit, every patient was given hydrocortisone. The second level is the quality of the task: in Finland, first-attempt intubation success, a measure that does not directly record who completed every step. The third level is the patient's outcome. Both are observational studies, so every change here is an association, not yet evidence that the SOP itself caused it, and in these two studies the association weakens as it moves from the first level to the third. The near halving of mortality in the intensive care unit comes from 30 patients compared with 30 patients from an earlier period, with four treatment changes made at once, so the share that belongs to the document alone cannot be separated out. The Finnish study, with a much larger sample and adjustment for confounders, found a short-term mortality signal only during implementation, and no difference at 30 days. And an SOP is worth no more than what is written into it: one component of the intensive care SOP was later withdrawn from the market for lack of efficacy. The scope of the two studies is narrow: both are in emergency medicine, both are observational studies without randomisation, and they say nothing about SOPs in an office or on a production line.

The counter-case: Defenders of SOPs have two strong arguments. First, asking for a trial that proves an SOP reduces deaths is asking for the wrong thing: an SOP is a way of bringing a task that already has a reason to be done into uniform practice, so the right measure of it is whether the task is done more consistently and better, and first-attempt success rising from 87.3% to 96.5% is the answer; in aviation, the FAA treats SOPs as the foundation of safe operations. [1] Second, rhAPC was a failure of the medical evidence of the time, not a failure of the SOP, and precisely because the protocol sat in a single document, the hospital had only one place to change when the evidence changed. This article accepts most of the first argument: changes in compliance and in the quality of the task are the most solid evidence there is about SOPs, even if they are still only associations, and they have real value. It does not accept the step from those two levels to outcomes: the Finnish study found no difference in 30-day mortality, so better technique has not been shown to turn by itself into more people alive. The second argument is right in its first half. The second half holds only when the organisation has a way of knowing that the evidence has changed and someone responsible for changing the document; at Texas City, the startup procedure stood still for years even though the data showing it was out of date sat ready in the system. [8]

Why this is useful: It tells you what to ask when someone presents a new SOP along with a figure for improvement. Does that figure measure whether people follow the document, the quality of the task, or the end result, meaning fewer people harmed, fewer defective products, fewer complaints? All three are worth having, but an earlier level does not guarantee the next. And before you sign off any SOP, ask yourself one more question: which step in this document rests on knowledge that could change, and when it does, who will find out and change the document?

How to Apply It

An SOP works by turning a way of doing a job into a shared standard so that everyone does it the same way and deviations show up, so using it well means keeping the document close to the real work, and treating each deviation as data for fixing either the document or the working conditions. It fits work that is repeated, that has one way of doing it clearly better than the others, and where doing it differently has real consequences. For work that is different every time, a detailed SOP only produces more paper.

  • Write from how the best performer actually does the job, together with the people who will follow it, then verify and trial it before it is finalised: Watch people work rather than only asking them: the real way of working usually contains steps the people doing it do not think are worth mentioning. The FAA's review of aircraft maintenance names two reasons documentation tends to fail: the writers are not familiar with the working environment, and because there is no time for trials, feedback often arrives only after the procedure has gone into use. But the person with the best results may still be using a method that carries a risk nobody has seen yet: at Texas City, operators kept the liquid level high for fear of losing it, and according to the CSB they knew the hazards of a low level but not those of a high one. So the observed way of working has to be checked against the technical and safety requirements, and then tried out by someone other than the writer, as the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) guidance on preparing SOPs recommends, before it is finalised [9]
  • Keep it short, one instruction per step, and replace every vague word with a criterion that can be checked: The criterion can be a number, a reference photo, or a state of the equipment that can be seen. The FAA recommends short sentences, active verbs, each step written as an instruction, and only the information needed to do the job; the reason for each step belongs in training material. But the people doing the work must understand that reason, because it is one of the conditions for an effective SOP that the FAA itself lists. A step that says "ensure adequate staffing" without saying how many is adequate is a step anyone can tick
  • Treat every deviation as data, ask why it happened, and only then decide whether to fix the document, fix the reminder, or fix the working conditions: Not every deviation comes from a wrong document. According to the FAA review, most cases of not following procedures in aircraft maintenance are unintentional slips such as forgetting a step, while deliberate violations are estimated at 16% to 34%. A forgotten step calls for a better reminder: put that step exactly where the worker's hand has to go. A deliberate departure because the document cannot be followed calls for fixing the document. The obvious response is to tighten discipline and punish whoever did it differently, but that does not work when the document itself has drifted away from reality: the Texas City report cites James Reason's idea of "necessary violations", departures made because the job cannot be done otherwise, and punishing people for departing without fixing the document leaves the reason they departed exactly where it was. Skipping a step must be a decision made by someone with the authority to make it, with a risk assessment: at Texas City, operators could write "not applicable" against any step and then do it another way, without anyone assessing that change
  • Give each SOP one owner, keep exactly one version at the place of work, and measure by results instead of by signatures: The EPA guidance proposes that each SOP carry an identification number, a revision number and a date; that the organisation keep a master list; that someone be responsible for making sure only the current version is in use and old versions are withdrawn; that the document be revised whenever the way of working changes and reviewed periodically, for example every one to two years; and that the current version be available right where people do the work. [9] At the coffee chain above, the fix is to make the nine-line sheet the official version at the counter, carrying the same revision number as the full set, and to retrain staff whenever the revision number changes. "Read and understood" signatures and self-inspection forms ticked from top to bottom only measure whether the paperwork was completed; the CSB called this a "check-the-box" mentality at Texas City. Choose a result measure from the work itself, and decide in advance when the document must be revised: when the equipment changes, when a material changes, or when the same step keeps being done differently

Applying It to a Scenario

A bakery has two ovens and three bakers who take turns on the morning shift. Every morning it bakes the first six batches of bread before 7 a.m., and roughly one batch in nine fails and has to be thrown away or sold at a discount. The owner wants to bring the failed-batch rate below 5%, and to stop that rate depending on who is at the oven. The figures in this scenario are illustrative.

  1. Settle what to standardise, and measure first: Only the first six batches of the day are standardised, because that is when the ovens are still cold and the failure rate is highest. A "failed batch" is defined before counting starts: a batch that has to be thrown away or sold at a discount. The first four weeks come to 168 batches, 19 of them failed, about 11.3%, and the rates of the three bakers differ by a factor of four
  2. Watch all three bakers work, rather than only asking them: The baker with the lowest failure rate measures the oven temperature with a separate thermometer, because the gauge on oven two reads about 15 degrees lower than the real temperature, and proves the dough for longer on cold mornings. The other two read the oven gauge and prove for a fixed time. When asked, the best baker does not mention the thermometer, because for that baker it is no longer a step
  3. Check the best method and fix the equipment before writing anything: The owner checks the best baker's two habits against the guidance from the oven maker and from the maker of the yeast in use, and both match. A technician is called in to recalibrate the gauge on oven two, because writing the workaround into the document would leave the faulty gauge where it is; the document still keeps the step of checking with the separate thermometer as an independent check, because a gauge that has drifted once can drift again
  4. Write the draft with all three bakers, short, every step checkable: Eight steps, stuck beside the ovens. "Oven hot enough" becomes "separate thermometer reads 200 to 205°C"; "proved enough" becomes a small table of proving times by room temperature measured at 5 a.m. The reason for each step is written on the back of the sheet, for new bakers to read while being trained
  5. Trial it for two weeks and record every deviation: There are six deviations. Four fall on rainy mornings when the room temperature drops below 22°C, lower than any row in the proving table, so the baker has to guess; two rows are added to the table. The other two are cases of forgetting to check with the separate thermometer; the thermometer is hung right on the oven door handle, so nobody can open the oven without touching it
  6. Finalise the second version, give the document an owner, and set when it must be revised: The document carries a revision number, a date and the name of the person who approved it; the owner is responsible for revising it, and the old sheet comes down from beside the oven the moment the new one goes up. The document must be reviewed when an oven is changed, when the type of flour is changed, or when the same step is done differently three or more times in a month
  7. Measure four weeks later with exactly the same definition, then look separately at the week the best baker was away: Both periods fall in the same season, with the same number of batches and the same definition of a "failed batch". During the second period the best baker takes a week's leave, so that week is the test of whether the way of working now lives on the sheet or still lives in one person

In the week the best baker was away, the other two baked 42 batches and 2 failed, about 4.8%, still below the target. The before and after periods have no control group, and part of the improvement may come from all three bakers paying more attention while they knew they were being measured; so the owner keeps recording failed batches for another three months, to see whether the rate holds once being measured has become routine. The conditions for that result were that the method was taken from the best performer by watching rather than by interviewing, and checked against the technical specifications before being standardised; that faulty equipment was repaired instead of merely worked around; that every vague word was turned into a criterion that could be checked; that the deviations during the trial were used to revise the document; and that the result was measured in real batches of bread under one unchanged definition.

Summary

Key Point: An SOP turns each person's way of doing a job into a shared standard, written down and visible, so that everyone does it the same way and deviations show up; that mechanism only runs while the document still matches the real work. The most counter-intuitive point is that an outdated SOP is not merely useless but teaches people that procedures are something they can skip: at Texas City, the startup procedure was departed from in almost every startup for five years, and was still certified every year, until the tower overflowed. The trustworthy way to use it is therefore to write from the real way of working, together with the people who do it, and verify it before standardising; to keep each step short and checkable; to treat every deviation as data for fixing the document, the reminder or the working conditions; to give the document one owner who keeps exactly one version in use; and to measure by the results of the work itself instead of by the number of signatures.
