Gantt Charts

A bar has two sharp ends; the work behind it does not

Updated: October 9, 2026

Definition

What Is a Gantt Chart

A Gantt chart lays a plan out on a calendar: a column of tasks on the left, a date axis on the right, and on that axis each task is a horizontal bar, starting on the day the work kicks off, running exactly as long as the estimate, ending on the promised date. Open the Gantt view in a tool such as Microsoft Project and that is the picture you get. In the Monday morning stand-up, when somebody points at the blue bar straddling the last two weeks of the month and says "this will be done before the 28th", what they are pointing at is a drawing; what the room hears is a promise.

The name belongs to Henry Laurence Gantt (1861-1919), an American mechanical engineer working in Frederick Taylor's orbit, but he did not invent the bar chart. Patrick Weaver traces the time-scaled bar chart back to Joseph Priestley's Chart of Biography of 1765 and to the harmonogram of the Polish engineer Karol Adamiecki in 1896. [1] In 1912, the construction programme for the Langwies viaduct on the Chur-Arosa mountain railway in Switzerland was already a bar chart laid out week by week, with a table of material requirements alongside it. [1] [2] What Gantt actually built was a system of production management charts for the machine shop, with most of the information filled in after the shift ended, to set actual output against promised output. A record of the past set against a promise, more than a drawing of the future. [1] Wallace Clark, a colleague at his consulting firm, put Gantt's name on that system in a book published in the early 1920s, after Gantt had died. [3] It was not until the 1930s that people began calling any picture with bars and a time axis a Gantt chart. [1]

The mechanism of the tool lies in what it puts in the foreground. A bar on its own directly encodes two points and one length: which day it starts, which day it ends, and therefore how much time it occupies. Whether two tasks overlap already has to be read by comparing two bars against each other. The frame around them can carry a good deal more, and today's software carries quite a lot: dependency links, milestones, the name of whoever owns the task, percent complete. [4] But all of it is optional, somebody has to put it there deliberately, and it sits in the background of a picture where the bar is what takes the reader's eye.

Why We Need It

Take a plan of fifteen tasks split across four departments and write it as a list with start and end dates. Every line reads correctly and nobody can see the whole: which week piles three tasks onto the same team, which late task will drag the handover date with it, who your department is actually waiting on. A Gantt chart turns that list into a picture. Every task sits on the same date axis, so overlaps and pile-ups show at a glance, without anyone adding and subtracting dates. That is the useful side of pushing time into the foreground. Elaine Yakura, watching how teams used schedule charts inside a technology consulting firm, found a further effect: precisely because the picture is simple and leaves room for interpretation, groups that think about time in different ways can still negotiate a schedule with each other on one sheet of paper. [5] On a production planning problem with all its data supplied, the difference can be very large: in MacNeice's 1951 experiment, only 1% of students solved the problem by intuition, while with the chart everyone produced a solution. [6] And a century on, it still sits among the tools project managers use most. [7]

What Are Its Limits

The other face of the same mechanism is whatever gets pushed into the background. Three things have no default way to show on a bar: how certain the estimate is, why a bar slipped when it slipped, and whether this piece of work has to be done over and over. Software lets you add them, for instance by showing a custom text field right beside the bar, [4] but somebody has to set that field up and the update routine has to require it to be filled in; the ready-made template prompts nobody to do so. Because a bar has two sharp ends, what the viewer takes away is a picture that looks far more certain than the information used to draw it. Yakura recorded exactly that: participants assign these drawings a permanence they do not have, and although they are only sketches of an uncertain future, they get treated as concrete realities. [5] Figure 1 above draws two of those three gaps, the uncertainty and the missing reason for a slip, along with the way the today line gets read as work completed.

In one respect, the original was richer than today's version. Gantt built his charts to measure the distance between promise and result, and he himself mocked shops that planned their work beautifully on paper with no means of finding out whether the plan was being kept. [1] The two main sources behind this piece do not entirely agree on the history, but both lead back to the same book by Clark, and the version in it has a column today's template no longer keeps. Beside every departure from plan, a letter recorded why the work had slipped: W for waiting on set-up, M for lack of material, H for lack of help. Joana Geraldi and Thomas Lechter say plainly that the only significant difference between that version and ours is that reason column. [6] What fell out of the tool over the past hundred years is therefore not a decorative detail; it was the place set aside for learning from deviation.

Examples

The two examples below set side by side two ways of reading the same kind of picture: a familiar progress meeting, where progress is read off the today line, and the US Army Ordnance Department in 1917, where the chart was used to read the quantity actually delivered and the cause of every delay.

The Blue Bar Marked Testing, Two Weeks Long

An illustrative scenario: Monday of the second week of testing. The project manager puts the chart up, the cursor stops on the bar marked "Testing" running from the 6th to the 17th, and asks the familiar question: are we on track. The today line sits halfway along the bar, so the answer more or less produces itself: about half done.

But that half is half of the elapsed time. The work is somewhere else: the test team has only worked through the easy cases, found fourteen defects, and one of them cannot be fixed without touching a part signed off last month, which means retesting from the start once it is fixed. The chart on the screen holds only the planned schedule, with no actual completion data at all, so there is nowhere on it for that sentence. The bar is still two weeks long, the today line still sits halfway, and the room leaves the meeting with the impression that everything is running exactly as drawn.

The Ordnance Department, 1917: The Charts Said There Was No Labour Shortage

Before the United States entered the war in 1917, the Frankford Arsenal was already using a simple chart system that set promises against performance. Once America was in, General William Crozier, Chief of Ordnance, began extending that system across the whole department, so that he could see at any moment how far each subordinate had got with the work assigned to him. Each line was an item to be delivered. A heavy line grew with the quantity actually delivered, so the distance between today's date and the end of that line showed whether the item was ahead of or behind schedule, and by how much; the short lines were the items that needed attention. According to Gantt, by the time Crozier was removed from his post in December 1917, most of the department's activities had been charted this way. [8]

In late November 1917, Herman Schneider, a dean at the University of Cincinnati, was called into the department to deal with the labour problem. Before deciding how to tackle it, he looked at the chart systems of three production sections, and in early December wrote to his superior that, except in isolated cases, there was no evident shortage of labour. In the same letter he noted that the charts gave a picture of the progress of the whole ordnance programme, including the delays and their causes; brought together in one office and kept up to date, they would tell the department at any time what it needed, now and soon, in men, materials, transport and equipment. Crozier's successors let these methods lapse. [8]

The story comes from Gantt's own book, written by someone with a direct interest in the charts being seen as a success, and the book gives no before-and-after production figures. The more trustworthy part is the description of how the charts were used, because it comes with an actual department chart: progress read from the quantity delivered rather than from elapsed time, and every delay carrying its cause, which are exactly the two things the Testing bar in the previous example lacks.

Some Research on Gantt Charts

The evidence for the Gantt chart's value is thinner than its popularity: one experiment from 1951 that can only be read through two relays, plus surveys measuring how much project managers use it. Geraldi and Lechter's 2012 analysis rereads both and then asks a different question: what assumptions does a tool built for repetitive production carry with it when it is moved across to projects.

The study: In 1951, E. H. MacNeice gave three hundred management students a complex production planning problem, and compared how many could solve it by intuition with how many produced a solution once they had a Gantt chart in hand. The result reaches this page through two relays: Geraldi and Lechter take it from Wilson's 2003 review, and Wilson takes it from MacNeice's book. [6] On popularity, Besner and Hobbs surveyed 750 project managers in 2008 on how far they used 70 tools and techniques. [7] And Joana Geraldi (University College London) and Thomas Lechter (Stevens Institute of Technology) published a conceptual analysis in the International Journal of Managing Projects in Business: they went back to the original sources, including Gantt's own two books and Clark's, reconstructed the tool's journey from the machine shop to the project, extracted the principles built into how it operates, then tested each principle against the conditions of projects today. [6]

Findings: By intuition, only 1% solved the problem, roughly three people. With the chart, all three hundred produced a solution, within fifteen minutes. [6] In Besner and Hobbs's survey, the Gantt chart ranked fourth of 70 tools by usage. [7] Geraldi and Lechter extract six principles the tool carries over from the Scientific Management era: it takes time as its only axis, it assumes there is one objective and uniquely correct description of the work, it assumes everything that will happen can be determined in advance, it assumes the whole is the sum of its parts, it assumes each part can be traced to one accountable person, and it assumes the parts follow one another linearly, one finished before the next begins, with no going back. This is a management logic the two authors read out of the tool's origins, not six technical constraints on every Gantt chart. They also note that, among the sources they surveyed, the earliest illustration of a Gantt chart used for a project is in Koepke's 1941 work, nearly twenty years after the tool took shape (the 1912 bar chart at Langwies did not carry Gantt's name and falls outside their survey), and that controlling progress with a Gantt chart rests on a logic of effort that is linear in time: a bar half elapsed is read as work half done. [6]

What it shows: If MacNeice's figure holds up, the chart helps a great deal on exactly the kind of problem it was born to solve: production planning, with all the data supplied. But this piece could not trace the original experimental design, does not know what "solved" was scored against, and does not know how correct those fifteen-minute solutions were. Besner and Hobbs's survey measures usage, not project results. And Geraldi and Lechter's analysis does not measure, and cannot measure, whether projects fail more or less often when a Gantt chart is used; the authors themselves record that the link between misapplying the tool and project outcomes is still only a hypothesis. What their analysis argues fits in one sentence: this tool is not neutral. In the authors' account, it was designed for work that is repetitive, low in uncertainty, divisible into separate parts and not subject to rework; move it to a different kind of work and those assumptions travel with it, even when nobody in the room says them out loud.

The counter-case: The defenders have four arguments, and all four come from this piece's own sources. The most substantial quantitative result, the MacNeice experiment, comes down firmly on the tool's side. The defenders read a fourth-place ranking a century on as a sign that practitioners find it useful, not merely familiar. [7] According to Yakura, in practice the chart tends to work as an intermediary object rather than an instruction: because it is simple and interpretively flexible, groups that understand time differently can negotiate with each other. [5] And Geraldi and Lechter say themselves that they are not proposing the tool be dropped; most of the problem lies in using it by default. This piece accepts all four, but each argument carries only part of the weight. The MacNeice experiment solved a problem handed over complete with all its data, so it says nothing about projects where even the list of tasks is not fully known. Reading the ranking that way is reasonable, but a usage survey cannot separate usefulness from habit and the reach of the software, and it does not measure results. The intermediary argument is sound, but it defends using the chart as a place to negotiate; using it as a measure of progress, the very use the linearity principle above describes, it does not defend. As for "the problem is the usage, not the tool", that is the easiest sentence to say and the hardest to test, because it defends every tool ever made. This piece has no data on how widespread this pattern of misuse is. But if the same misreading recurs in many places, that pattern of misuse is also a fact about the tool, not only about the people using it.

Why this is useful: It changes the question you ask when looking at a schedule chart. "Are we on track", answered from the today line, only measures the distance between today and a number somebody wrote down months ago, and it always has an answer, including when that answer is meaningless. Three questions are worth asking instead: which bars are grounded estimates and which are numbers entered to fill a field, which task is waiting on which for a real technical reason, and where the reason was recorded the last time a bar slipped. The third is usually the hardest to answer, because the default layout has no place for it.

How to Apply It

A Gantt chart works by pushing time into the foreground and everything else into the background, so using it well means deliberately pulling back up the things that got pushed down. It suits work that is divisible, reasonably stable, and needs several parties looking at the same handover date. The further you get from those three conditions, the more it should be used as a place to negotiate rather than as a measure. [6]

  • Record the reason next to every bar that slips, and keep the baseline to compare against: The reason column is something Clark's version had built in and today's template lacks; software can still show it beside the bar if somebody sets up a text field for it and the update routine requires it to be filled in. A short line like "slipped four days, waiting on data from operations" accumulates over a few weeks into a picture of where things keep jamming. Gantt's charts always set the promise beside the result, and the baseline is that promise: when bars simply move, every slip has its reason, yet nobody can see any longer how far the whole plan has drifted from where it started. Before concluding where the bottleneck is, compare the bars that slipped with the bars that did not, to see whether that reason is tied to lateness at all, then look for more direct evidence such as a log of waiting time. A reason that also turns up on tasks that finished on time is not necessarily innocent: those tasks may only have made it because they had more slack
  • Measure progress by something deliverable rather than reading it off the today line: The today line only says how much time has passed. To know how much work is done, every bar has to be tied to something externally checkable, along the lines of "the report runs on real data", in place of a "60% complete" that nobody can say how it was calculated. A completion percentage is still useful once it is clearly defined what it counts and how it is checked
  • Mark which bars are estimates and which are guesses: Every bar looks identically certain, while some rest on ten similar jobs and others on an afternoon of guessing. Shade the second group differently, or write an earliest and a latest date on them
  • Separate out looping work with an unknown number of rounds, and track it by its handover conditions: A scheduled sprint or a review round already booked in draws as a bar like any other task. The test, fix, retest cycle is different: neither the number of rounds nor the outcome of each is known in advance, so a fixed bar cannot fully represent the loop or its uncertainty. [6] You can still draw an expected range and the rounds already known, then update the rest as more information comes in; what you cannot do is chop the bar into rounds from the outset, because chopping requires knowing the number of rounds in advance. Nor should the accompanying measure be a single count: open defects can rise precisely because testing is doing its job, and can fall because many minor defects get closed while the serious ones remain. Track together the serious defects still open, the share of test scripts run, and which acceptance conditions for handover have been met

Applying It to a Scenario

A 40-person company has to move into a new office before the lease on the old one runs out, ten weeks from now. The work is split across four parties: the office administration team handles contracts and furniture, the IT team handles the network and computers, an outside removals firm does the moving, and the new building's management handles the electrical works. The measurable goal: on the first Monday in the new office, before 9 a.m., all 40 people have a desk, a working computer, and can log in to the internal systems. The figures in this scenario are illustrative.

  1. Write every task as something that can be handed over: "Electrics on the new floor signed off", "40 desks assembled", "network live in both wings", instead of "do the IT bit". The list has 14 lines, and every line answers the question "when it is done, what can you see"
  2. Record estimates together with how certain they are: 10 lines are jobs the company or its suppliers have done many times, such as assembling desks in 3 days, as in the last two moves. 4 lines are guesses, the biggest being the electrical works in the new building, where nobody in the company has worked with the building management before. For these four, write both the earliest and the latest date, 8 to 15 working days for the electrics, and shade them a different colour
  3. Link two bars only when the later task genuinely has to wait for the earlier one: Network cabling has to wait for the electrics to be signed off, installing computers has to wait for the cabling and the desks, and the login check has to wait for the computers. Packing at the old office, ordering furniture or sending clients the new address run in parallel, even though stringing them end to end would look tidier. Electrics, cabling, computers, login check is the longest chain: a delay on it first eats into the slack before move day, and only when that slack is used up does move day get pushed
  4. Place the bars using both ends of the estimate range, and keep three dates apart: The hard deadline is the end of week ten, when the old lease runs out. Move day is set a week earlier, at the end of week nine, working day 45, leaving the last week as a buffer. When the longest chain finishes depends on the electrics, as the table and Figure 2 after these steps show: if the electrics take 8 days the chain finishes on day 30, three weeks before move day; if they take 15 days it finishes on day 37, leaving only 8 working days of slack. Draw an 8-day bar and the whole room only ever sees the three weeks, so the chart shows both
  5. Meet for fifteen minutes every Monday, read progress from what has been handed over, and record the reason beside every bar that slips: "Are we on track" still gets asked, but it is answered from the lines that already have something to hand over, set against the baseline fixed in the first week, both ends of the estimate range included, rather than from the today line. After four weeks, 5 bars have slipped, and the reason column reads "awaiting spending approval" 4 times and "supplier short-delivered chairs" once. One of the four approval waits was the electrical works contract, signed a week late, so the slack on the longest chain fell from 15 to 10 days if the electrics go well, and from 8 to only 3 days if they take the full 15; the other three sit on tasks running in parallel
  6. Compare the bars that slipped with the bars that did not before drawing a conclusion: Of the 14 lines, 6 need finance to approve spending before they can start and 8 do not. Slipped: 4 out of 6 in the first group, 1 out of 8 in the second. The approval log shows all four requests sitting in the queue until the regular Friday-afternoon signing session, so a request sent early in the week took about 5 working days to be signed. The comparison is only a signal to go looking; the log is what shows the mechanism: the bottleneck lies in the signing schedule, not with the parties doing the work
  7. Fix the actual bottleneck and measure again with the same definition: Administration and finance agree that spending for the move will be signed within two working days of being submitted, without waiting for the Friday session. Each remaining approval request is drawn as its own "awaiting approval" bar, running from the day it is submitted to the day it is signed, so the waiting time shows instead of disappearing into the bar that follows it. Two numbers are tracked separately: days from submission to signature, counted the same way as in the first four weeks, and the slack on the longest chain before move day

The two remaining requests were signed after 1 and 2 working days. The electrics took 13 days, inside the recorded range, so the longest chain finished on day 40: 5 working days to spare before move day, and another week on top of that before the lease ran out. The move happened as planned at the end of week nine, and on the Monday morning, before 9 a.m., all 40 people were at their desks and logged in. The chart did not make the electrical works a single day faster. It let four parties look at the same dates; the estimate range on the electrics showed from the start that there was less slack than there appeared to be; and the reason column showed that the bottleneck lay in the signing schedule, something bars that simply move would never have said on their own. What it took to get that result: bars tied to things that can be handed over, uncertainty written down instead of hidden behind a single number, and reasons recorded from the first week, early enough to still be fixed.

Summary

Key Point: A Gantt chart pushes time into the foreground and everything else into the background: overlaps and handover dates show at a glance, while the uncertainty in an estimate, the reason behind each slip and the looping nature of the work have no default way to show, and the two sharp ends of a bar make the picture look more certain than the information used to draw it. The most counter-intuitive part sits in its own history: Gantt's charts were records that set the promise beside the result, and the version in Clark's book had a column recording why each departure from plan happened that today's template no longer keeps. The way to use it worth trusting is therefore to pull those things back up: the reason behind each slip, a baseline to compare against, how certain each estimate is, and what has actually been handed over.
