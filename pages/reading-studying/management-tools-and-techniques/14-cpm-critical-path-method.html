Critical Path Method (CPM)

The critical path is a property of the schedule that was built, not necessarily of the project

Published: October 24, 2026

Definition

What Is the Critical Path Method

Every activity in a project has an estimated duration and a list of activities that must finish before it can start. The critical path method, or CPM, takes exactly those two things, adds up the durations along every chain of linked activities from the start of the project to its end, and points to the longest chain. That chain is the critical path. It sets the earliest date the project can finish, so an activity on it that slips by a week pushes the finish back by a week, unless the time is made up somewhere else. Activities off that chain have float (total float, also called total slack): the number of days an activity can slip without missing the date the calculation treats as the deadline. [1] Suppose the class committee of a graduating year is putting together a yearbook. Collecting photos takes 10 days, collecting written pieces 8 days, designing the cover 7 days, and all three run in parallel from day one. Layout takes 6 days and has to wait for both the photos and the pieces; the proof print takes 2 days and has to wait for both the layout and the cover; the final print run takes 5 days. The chain of photos, layout, proof and final print is 23 days long, and it is the critical path. If the print deadline is set at exactly day 23, the written pieces have 2 days of float and the cover has 9.

The calculation runs in two passes. The forward pass goes from the start of the project and gives each activity its earliest start and earliest finish: an activity can only start once the last of its predecessors has finished. The backward pass goes from the deadline back, and gives each activity the latest finish that does not push the deadline back. Float is the difference between the two passes, so float is measured against whatever date the backward pass starts from. Counted back from the earliest finish date, it is the number of days an activity can slip without moving the earliest finish. Counted back from a later committed deadline, it also includes the buffer between the earliest finish and that deadline, and an activity can push the earliest finish back without yet missing the deadline. James Kelley and Morgan Walker, who published the method in 1959, called an activity critical when the time available for it exactly equals its duration, and noted that a project has critical activities in this sense only when the deadline coincides with the earliest finish. [2] If the deadline is later, the longest chain still has a few days of float; it is still the chain that decides the finish date, and usually also the chain with the least float. In the words of the GAO, the US Government Accountability Office, when a schedule has no hard date constraints, meaning no start or finish date typed into the schedule as fixed instead of being left for the calculation to derive, critical activities have zero float, and the longest path is usually also the path with the lowest total float. The two ways of identifying it can part company when a schedule has hard constraints or uses several work calendars, say one crew working weekends and the next crew not; the GAO writes that when multiple calendars are in use, the longest path is the only guaranteed way to find the sequence of activities that is driving the finish date. [1] This piece takes the critical path to mean the longest path.

The method was born at the chemical company DuPont. In late 1956 the company's engineering control group began looking into whether computers could help plan large engineering projects; the computer maker Remington Rand UNIVAC assigned Kelley to help, Walker represented DuPont, and the name was chosen for the central place that critical activities hold in the method. The 1959 paper drew each activity as an arrow, and its focus was more than finding the longest chain. Each activity had a normal duration, at the lowest cost, and a crash duration, at a higher cost. For each finish date the project might aim for, the method looked for the schedule with the lowest direct cost. At each step of shortening, it expedited only the critical activities that pushed cost up most slowly, and when several critical paths ran in parallel, the set of activities expedited had to cut across all of them. [2]

The tool's mechanism is an allocation of attention based on a calculation. From dependencies and durations, it separates the activities that are deciding the finish date from those that still have float, so that a manager can put expediting, monitoring and resources into the first group. In a project with many activities running in parallel, that group is usually small. In a project that is almost entirely sequential, nearly every activity is critical, and the GAO treats a ratio of critical activities close to 100% as a sign that the schedule is overly serial or resource limited. [1] The calculation is exactly right for its inputs. But the inputs, which arrows are present and how long each activity takes, are a model built by whoever made the schedule, so the critical path is a property of that model, not necessarily of the project. The critical path method differs from a Gantt chart in that a Gantt chart is a way of laying a schedule out against a time axis, while the critical path is the calculation that produces the dates and the float to lay out; a Gantt chart can be drawn from the results of that calculation, or drawn by hand without any calculation at all. It also differs from a work breakdown structure: the breakdown answers what the project consists of, while the critical path takes the list of activities drawn from that tree and asks which must come before which, and how long each takes.

Why We Need It

In a project with a few hundred overlapping activities, almost every activity has someone reporting it as urgent. When the directors ask for an earlier finish, the natural reflex is to speed everything up: overtime, extra hires, extra payments to every contractor. Kelley and Walker described exactly that reflex. A project manager asked to cut four months, without knowing what each slice of time would cost, would object, and if pressed would quote a cost penalty many times the estimate and launch an across-the-board crash programme. [2] In the calculation, speeding up an activity that has three weeks of float does not bring the finish date forward by a single day; it only makes the project more expensive.

The critical path method fixes exactly that by showing which activities have no room to slip. On the first test project at DuPont only about 10% of activities were critical, and in the maintenance shutdowns at the Louisville plant the two authors likewise observed that only about 10% of activities were critical. [2] Across 323 schedules from 77 construction projects, the share of activities on the critical path also fell as schedules grew larger. [3] If that ratio holds for your project, most activities have float, and a day's slip there is not yet a day's slip for the project. The specific results of those two tests, and how far they can be trusted, are in the research section below. The GAO writes that the critical path is most useful as a tool for deciding which activities deserve focus and, potentially, management help. [1]

What Are Its Limits

The first built-in limit: the calculation treats each duration as a certain number, while every duration is an estimate. The GAO states plainly that the true critical path of a schedule is uncertain, because activity durations are uncertain. [1] That uncertainty piles up at merge points, where several parallel chains of activities lead into a single activity. That activity can only start when the last chain arrives, so the probability that it starts on time is the probability that every chain arrives on time. In the GAO's example, two testing paths, each with a 60% chance of finishing on time, give the completion milestone only a 36% chance of being on time; add a third path, also at 60%, and the chance falls to 22%. The GAO calls this added risk merge bias, one reason why the finish date of even a well-built schedule tends to come later than the date calculated. [1] The multiplication assumes the chains are independent of one another.

The case the GAO cites is the third National Security Cutter of the US Coast Guard: in February 2011 the shipbuilding schedule still had 3,920 activities remaining, and the "preliminary delivery" milestone that worried the contractor most was already 5 days late with 57 activities converging on it, while the programme had run no schedule risk analysis. [1] Kelley and Walker themselves describe a parallel system called PERT, built from early 1958 by the Special Projects Office of the US Navy, the office managing the Polaris missile programme, together with Booz, Allen & Hamilton and Lockheed: the same kind of diagram, but with three estimates for each activity, most optimistic, most likely and most pessimistic, instead of a single figure. [2] In 1963 Richard Van Slyke simulated PERT networks with the Monte Carlo method, rerunning the calculation many times and each time drawing every activity's duration at random from within its estimated range, and proposed a criticality index: the probability that an activity lies on the critical path, something a deterministic calculation cannot provide. [4]

The second built-in limit: the calculation assumes unlimited resources. One person can be assigned any number of parallel activities, and the critical path never knows. [1] The 1959 paper itself says so: the schedules it computes are technologically feasible but not necessarily practical, because manpower and equipment are "conspicuous by their absence" from the method. In one case the two authors describe, the schedule called for 27 mechanics when only 8 were available, and smoothing the work to fit the real crew lengthened the critical path by about 20%. [2] The chain that decides the finish date once both logic and resources are taken into account has its own name, the resource critical path, and the GAO notes that scheduling software does not derive it easily. [1]

The third built-in limit: the calculation cannot tell which arrows it is missing. An activity entered without a link to its successor can slip by any amount without pushing the finish date in the calculation, so the float the calculation gives it is never smaller than its true float and is often much larger, while in reality it may be holding up the whole project. The GAO writes that unless the schedule contains the entire scope of work and is linked with correct logic, the software will report an incorrect critical path. [1] The limit itself is built in; a schedule with missing logic, hard date constraints, or lags, a span of days inserted between two activities that belongs to neither, used in place of real work, is a fault of how the schedule was built, and the fix belongs in How to Apply It.

Examples

A restaurant about to open, where the critical path was highlighted in red once and became the only list anyone watched, alongside the paper-records transformation programme of US Citizenship and Immigration Services, where there was no integrated schedule from which to calculate a critical path for the whole programme, and both of the separate schedules examined lacked so much logic that neither had a valid critical path.

The Red Chain Finished on Time, the Restaurant Still Opened Late

An illustrative scenario: a new restaurant has printed invitations to an opening on working day 30 after the start of building work. The project manager enters three chains of activities into the software. The kitchen chain is 22 days of kitchen construction, 4 days of fitting equipment and 2 days of test cooking, 28 days in all, leaving 2 days of float. The fire safety chain is 12 days to finish the emergency exits and the fire alarm system, then 10 days for the fire safety inspection, 22 days in all, leaving 8 days of float. The staffing chain is 10 days of hiring and 7 days of training, leaving 13 days of float. The software highlights the kitchen chain in red, and from the first week the progress meeting only goes through the red items. The kitchen finishes right on day 28. At the first inspection the fire safety file is sent back for a missing drawing, and the inspection ends up taking 22 days instead of 10. The fire safety chain finishes on day 34, the restaurant opens 4 days late, and the invitations are reprinted.

The calculation was not wrong. With the durations entered, the kitchen chain really was the longest. What went wrong lay in two things the users layered on top of it. First, the 10 days for the inspection was an estimate with a wide range, sometimes double, while the 22 days for the kitchen came from a crew that knew the work; a chain with 8 days of float but a 10-day swing can decide the opening date more than a chain with 2 days of float and almost no swing. Second, no one entered the fire safety chain's progress into the schedule, so by day 25, when that chain had already become the critical path, the software was still highlighting the kitchen. The GAO describes both traps exactly: the critical path is not constant, it changes as activities slip or finish early, and management has to watch the near-critical chains too, the ones that need only a little more delay to become critical. [1] The three chains converging on the opening are also a merge point: the chance of that milestone being on time is no higher than the chance for the least certain of the three, and usually lower.

US Immigration Services: Eighteen Separate Schedules, No Integrated Schedule to Find a Critical Path

In 2005 US Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) launched a multiyear programme to move its paper-based processing of millions of applications to an electronic system, from filing through adjudication to case management. By 2011 the programme's schedule consisted of 18 individual project schedules. The programme office did not build an integrated master schedule; instead it kept a high-level tracking tool, picking key activities out of the individual schedules to report to the acquisition review board. According to programme officials, they could not build an integrated schedule because combining the many individual schedules was too complex and they lacked skilled staff, and not every organisation doing the work used scheduling software. [5] The tracking tool held dates and activities only for the first release, and nothing for the four after it. When the GAO assessed two of the schedules underneath, neither had a valid critical path: the Office of Information Technology's schedule had missing or dangling logic on over 60% of its remaining activities, and the solutions architect contractor's schedule was missing logic on 40% of its remaining activities, along with many date constraints and lags. The GAO concluded that programme officials would have difficulty predicting, with any degree of confidence, how long completing all five releases would take. [1]

This case shows the critical path method from the auditor's side: the calculation is built into every scheduling package, but it only produces something trustworthy when every activity is linked to what comes before and after it. Hand-picking the "important" activities to report does not replace the calculation either, because what management sees as important is not necessarily what is deciding the finish date. Later, at the end of 2015, USCIS gave the GAO an updated integrated schedule, and the GAO found substantial improvement in exactly these areas: sequencing logic, integration between the schedules, critical paths running through the projects, and status updating. [5] The case does not say how much the missing critical path, on its own, delayed the programme. What the GAO assessed here was the reliability of the schedule, not its effect on outcomes.

Some Research on the Critical Path Method

Direct measurements of what the critical path method is worth are far scarcer than the method is common. What follows brings together three sources. The report by the method's own two creators on the first two tests at DuPont has the most concrete figures for what the tool delivered. A 2023 study of hundreds of real construction schedules measures which way delays spread through an activity network, and that is the most direct test of the assumption that watching the critical path is enough. And a four-country survey from 2019 shows how far people in the industry use the tool today, and why.

The study: Kelley and Walker describe two tests. The first was the construction of a new chemical plant facility with a capital cost of $10 million, called Project A. A team of six DuPont engineers took a 40-hour course on the method, then broke the construction work into 393 activities, plus 156 design and delivery restraints, and ran it on a UNIVAC computer. To keep the test impartial, the team's schedule was prepared independently of the normal scheduling group and was not used to run the project; the two ran in parallel. The second test was the maintenance shutdowns at DuPont's Louisville Works, which produced an intermediate in the neoprene process; because the material is self-detonating, almost all maintenance had to be done while the line was down. [2] Vazquez and colleagues analysed 323 schedules, built in Primavera P6 at various stages of completion, from 77 construction projects with a combined value of more than $10 billion, each schedule holding between 100 and 16,000 activities. They measured two things: how the share of activities on the critical path changes with schedule size, and how widely delays spread, counted as the share of activities delayed by more than their own float; and they built a network model to explain what they measured. [3] Sixty years after DuPont, Olivieri and colleagues surveyed construction professionals in Brazil, China, Finland and the United States, keeping 532 valid responses (168, 102, 132 and 130), and asked which of three common planning systems they used: the critical path method, the Last Planner System (short-term planning done together with the people who do the work), and location-based scheduling techniques; what they used them for; and how well they worked. [6]

Findings: On Project A, the normal scheduling group had drawn up a list of critical deliveries. The test team put all 156 deliveries into the network, and the calculation showed that only 7 were critical, 3 of which were not on the normal group's list. When DuPont decided to do most of the work that had been due to be subcontracted itself, and the design changed at the same time, the plan changed by about 40%; updating the schedule then took only about 10% of the effort of setting it up. The duration authorised by traditional means was N months; the computed results showed that two months could be gained at no extra cost, and two more for a 1% increase in variable direct cost. At Louisville, after switching to the method, the average shutdown fell from 125 to 93 hours, "mainly from the better analysis provided" according to the authors; they expected that expediting and improving labour performance on critical jobs would bring it down to 78 hours. [2] In the construction schedules Vazquez and colleagues analysed, the share of activities on the critical path shrank as schedules grew, roughly as a power of the number of activities with an exponent of 0.79. The spread of delays had a threshold: while the chance that a link passes a delay on to the next activity stayed below it, fewer than 1% of activities were delayed by more than their float; above it the figure climbed steeply, in some cases to 15% of the entire project. That spread showed no statistically significant relationship with an indicator built from the size of the critical path (Pearson correlation coefficient −0.1, significance 0.7). [3] In the survey by Olivieri and colleagues, 71% of respondents used CPM, 40% location-based techniques and 28% the Last Planner System. Among CPM users, 20% gave a contract requirement as their main reason for using it, and 79% said they used critical path analysis frequently or moderately when managing schedules. On analysing production constraints, meaning everything that must be in place before an activity can be done, 46% of CPM users rated it as working well or very well, against 65% of Last Planner users. The authors also cite Galloway's 2006 survey in the United States, in which 63% of respondents named a contract requirement as their main reason for using the method. [6]

What it shows: The 1959 report is the account of the tool's own creators, written for a computer conference, with no control group. Most of the Project A figures are computed results, not achieved ones: the team's schedule was not used to run the project, and the two authors themselves write that the initial two-month difference could be explained by the error of the gross time estimates or the buffering built into them. The Louisville figure is a real result at one plant, measured before and after, but the 78 hours is only a forecast, and at the same time the plant was training people, building a library of standard shutdown schedules and collecting duration data from foremen; the report cannot separate out the part played by the calculation alone. The firmer part is a counted figure: in both tests, critical activities made up only about a tenth of the total, so on those two projects, concentrating attention in the right place was within reach. The 2023 study measures real schedules and supports that half, the larger the schedule the smaller the share on the critical path, but it adds a half that points the other way: delays spread across the whole activity network, and the critical path does not predict how far. The authors conclude that beyond a certain level of complexity, overruns are driven mainly by activities outside the critical path. The study covers construction only, its raw data is not public, and its explanation rests on a model whose results the authors themselves call approximate in places. The 2019 survey measures perceptions and use, not project outcomes. It shows that the tool is widespread, partly because contracts require it, and that users rate it as strong for critical path analysis but weaker at preparing the conditions each activity needs. Within the scope of the searches for this piece, no study was found that compares the outcomes of projects with and without critical path analysis while controlling for the other differences between them.

The counter-case: The defenders have three arguments. First, the critical path method is arithmetic: given the logic and the durations, the longest chain is a mathematical fact, and an addition needs no randomised trial to prove it correct. Second, simply knowing that nine activities in ten have room to slip is enough to change how a project team allocates money and people, and Project A shows this with a concrete list: 3 critical deliveries the normal scheduling group had missed. Third, Louisville is a real operating result, 32 hours of shutdown saved each time at a plant that shuts down many times a year, and the two authors write that this application alone had paid for the whole development of the method five times over. This piece accepts the first argument in full: the calculation is not wrong, and no one needs to measure whether addition works. But the question worth measuring is not the addition; it is whether the inputs are right and whether the attention the calculation produces makes projects go better. The USCIS case shows inputs can fail in a large programme, the 2023 study shows that delays do not keep to the chain the calculation highlights, and the arithmetic argument answers neither. The piece accepts the second as a real mechanism, but the 3 missed deliveries prove only that the computed list differed from the guessed one, not that the computed list made the project faster, since that schedule was never put to use. The third it accepts by half: 125 down to 93 hours is a real figure, but it came together with training, a library of standard schedules and new data, was reported by the tool's own creators, and comes from one plant.

Why this is useful: It changes the question you ask when you are shown a critical path highlighted in red. The question "how long is this chain" the calculation has already answered. Three others it has not: how many activities in the schedule still lack a predecessor or a successor; how many days of float the chain with the second-lowest float has, and how widely its durations swing; and how many chains converge on the final milestone. If the schedule passes those three, the float the calculation gives is worth trusting: most activities have room to slip, and you know which ones do not. For a schedule of thousands of activities, add a fourth question that the 2023 study suggests: how many activities, anywhere in the schedule, are currently delayed by more than their own float.

How to Apply It

The critical path method works by adding up durations along the dependencies to separate the activities that are deciding the finish date from those that still have float, so the effective way to use it is to keep the calculation's inputs complete and current, recalculate whenever they change, and make up for two things the calculation cannot see: uncertainty in durations, which piles up at merge points, and the limits of resources. The tool fits best with projects where most activities and their order are known from the start, such as construction or routine maintenance. The closer the work is to exploration, where the list of activities itself is still taking shape, the more the critical path should be treated as a draft that will change.

  • Link every dependency before trusting the critical path: Every activity except the start milestone must have at least one predecessor; every activity except the finish milestone must have at least one successor. Filter out dangling activities before reading any float figure. The GAO points to two more faults that break the calculation: a hard date constraint makes each constraint generate its own chain of critical activities, and a lag between two activities hides the real work that is causing the criticality, so a waiting period such as a document review or a delivery should be written as a named activity with someone tracking it [1]
  • Recalculate the critical path after every status update, and do not hand-pick a list of important activities: The critical path changes as activities slip, finish early or happen out of sequence, so a critical path is only right until the next update. The GAO stresses that an important activity is not necessarily a critical one: a routine activity such as training can sit on the critical path, while a large and costly activity can have plenty of float. A report that follows only the activities management chose in advance is the way to lose track of the very activity that has just become critical [1]
  • Watch the near-critical chains too, and ask about probabilities at every major merge point: The obvious approach is to watch only the chain with the least float, and it fails when a chain has a few days of float but durations that swing by more than those few days. Set a float threshold, scaled to the project, for calling a chain near-critical; the GAO gives 5 days as an example for a short schedule and two or three months for a programme lasting several years. At any milestone where many chains converge, ask the owner of each chain how likely their chain is to arrive on time, then multiply those chances together if the chains do not depend on one another; if the result is low, check whether any chain does not really need to wait, or run a Monte Carlo simulation of the whole schedule as the GAO recommends [1]
  • Level for people and equipment before committing to a date, and when you must shorten the project, pay per day the whole project gains: The calculation does not know that one person cannot do two things at once, so before promising a date, check whether activities running in parallel share a person or a piece of equipment. When the project must finish sooner, choose the option with the lowest cost for each day the whole project actually gains, not for each day one activity is shortened: with two critical paths both 20 days long, cutting an activity on the first to 19 days leaves the project still needing 20. The 1959 original did exactly this, each step of shortening cutting across every critical path present. Then recalculate, because, as Walker answered a question at the conference, compressing the schedule will add more critical paths, and the old one does not drop out of the picture [2]

Applying It to a Scenario

A chain of eight pharmacies is moving to new point-of-sale software and must go live across the whole chain at the start of the quarter. The measurable target is all eight shops selling on the new system, with the switch-over finished no later than the end of working day 30 from the start. Durations are in working days, "day 5" means the end of the fifth working day, and every figure, including the percentage chances, is illustrative.

  1. Take the activity list from the agreed scope and link all the logic: The first draft has six activities: agree the software configuration with the vendor (A, 5 days); clean up the drug catalogue and move it into the new software (B, 12 days, after A); buy and install barcode scanners and receipt printers in all eight shops (C, 14 days, after A); connect e-invoicing with the service provider (D, 8 days, after A); train the counter pharmacists (E, 4 days, after B, since they need real data on the system to practise with); run a parallel trial in one shop (F, 5 days, after C, D and E). Setting that list against the target, the team sees that it stops at the trial, while the target is eight shops actually trading. A seventh activity is added: carry over the sales data recorded during the trial and switch on the new system in all eight shops (G, 2 days, after F). The version entered into the software also forgot to link E to F, and the software gave training 9 days of float; in reality, no one can run the trial until training is done. Once linked, E's float matches the table just after step 2
  2. Run both passes and read the float: The forward pass gives an earliest finish of day 28, along the chain A, B, E, F, G. The backward pass from the day-30 deadline gives that chain 2 days of float: the critical path has no zero float, because the deadline is later than the earliest finish, and the longest chain is also the chain with the least float. Set a near-critical threshold and ask about probabilities at the merge point: The team's threshold is that any chain with no more than 3 days more float than the critical path counts as near-critical, and C, with exactly 2 days more, falls inside it. The trial F is the merge point of three chains, and must start by day 23 at the latest. The owner of each chain estimates the chance of their chain arriving before day 23: the chain through B and E at 90%; chain C at 80%, because the equipment supplier says 14 days is normal but it once took 20; chain D at 95%. To illustrate the multiplication, assume A finishes in exactly 5 days, since if A slips all three chains slip with it, and that the remaining risk on the three chains is independent. The chance of all three arriving in time is then 0.9 × 0.8 × 0.95, about 68%: roughly one time in three the trial would have to start late, even though every chain has at least an 80% chance of being on time
  2. Re-examine every arrow leading into the near-critical chain: The team asks again why buying the scanners has to wait for the configuration to be agreed. The software vendor confirms that the list of compatible equipment has existed from day one and does not depend on the configuration. The arrow from A to C is removed because it was never a real dependency; removing a real arrow just to make the numbers look better would be bending the model to suit. C now runs from day 0 to day 14, with a latest window of day 9 to day 23 and 9 days of float; even if it takes 20 days as in the worst case, C still arrives before day 23, so its owner raises the chance of arriving in time to 98%, and the chance at the merge point becomes 0.9 × 0.98 × 0.95, about 84%. That is the chance that F's inputs are ready in time; it does not include the uncertainty in F and G themselves, so the chance of the whole project meeting the deadline is no higher than that figure. The earliest finish is still day 28, because the chain A, B, E, F, G has not changed
  3. Check resources before committing to the date: As assigned, both B and D belong to Hanh, the chain's only IT staff member, and the two overlap from day 5. If D has to wait for B, D runs from day 17 to day 25, F cannot start before day 25 and finishes on day 30, and G finishes on day 32, missing the deadline by 2 days: the chain A, B, D, F, G becomes the resource critical path. The team hands the connection work to a technician from the e-invoicing provider; Hanh needs only half a day to supply the account details, which fits within B's 12 days, and D keeps its slot from day 5 to day 13
  4. Update, recalculate, and pick the cheapest way to gain each day for the project: At the end of day 15, the remaining work on B is re-estimated at 5 days instead of 2, because the old catalogue has hundreds of duplicate codes; B will finish on day 20. Recalculated: E runs from day 20 to day 24, F from day 24 to day 29, G from day 29 to day 31, missing the deadline by 1 day. C and D finished on day 14 and day 13, so they cannot become critical, and since there is now only one critical path, each day cut from it is a day gained for the project. Two ways to cut: hire a temporary data-entry clerk to shorten the rest of B by 2 days, for 4 million dong, or 2 million per day gained; or have the software vendor send a second trainer, splitting the pharmacists into two groups trained in parallel so that E takes 2 days, for 3 million dong, or 1.5 million per day. The team picks the second and keeps the first in reserve in case B slips again. Recalculated once more: E from day 20 to day 22, F from day 22 to day 27, G from day 27 to day 29, float back to 1 day, and the critical path is still the chain A, B, E, F, G

The trial runs from day 22 to day 27, finds two errors in the invoice template and fixes them during the trial; all eight shops finish switching over at the end of day 29, one day ahead of the deadline. The method does not make the catalogue any cleaner or the work any faster. What it does is four specific things: it forces the activity list to run all the way to the target's milestone, which brings out a switch-over activity the draft had forgotten; it exposes a dangling activity before it can turn into a surprise; it shows an arrow that was not real pushing a chain into the near-critical zone; and when B slips, it points to the one place worth paying to cut, at 3 million dong instead of speeding everything up. The 84% in step 4 is the owners' estimate, never measured, and one on-time delivery does not prove the next will be. The conditions for that result are that the activity network ends exactly at the target's milestone, the logic is fully linked before the float is read, the merge point is questioned in probabilities rather than just in dates, resources are checked before committing, and the critical path is recalculated straight after the update that shows B slipping.

Summary

Key Point: The mechanism of the critical path method is to add up durations along the dependencies to separate the activities that are deciding the finish date from those that still have float, and then to put attention, money and people into the first group instead of speeding everything up. The most counter-intuitive point is that the critical path is a property of the schedule that was built, not necessarily of the project: one missing arrow is enough to change the whole critical path, and where several chains converge on one milestone, the chance of that milestone being on time is no higher than the chance for each chain, something the deterministic calculation does not show. So trust the float only in a schedule whose logic is fully linked, that has just been recalculated, and that has been checked against the resources that actually exist.
