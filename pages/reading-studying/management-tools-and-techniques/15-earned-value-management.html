Earned Value Management (EVM)

Earned value is only as honest as the way completed work is measured

Published: October 25, 2026

Definition

What Is Earned Value Management

An ordinary budget report puts two figures side by side: the money that should have been spent by today and the money actually spent. Earned value management, EVM for short, adds a third: the budget of the work actually finished, called earned value. All three are in money, so setting them side by side separates two questions that the ordinary report mixes into one: are we on time, and are we costing more than the estimate. [1] Suppose a company takes on rooftop solar installation for 10 factory buildings in an industrial park, with a budget of 2 billion dong, 200 million per building, and a plan of one building a week. At the end of week 6 the company has spent 1.1 billion, less than the 1.2 billion in the plan, and if those two figures are all you look at, the project is saving money. But only 4 buildings have been accepted, so earned value is 0.8 billion: work that should have been done by the end of week 4, and every dong spent has produced only about 0.73 dong of work at estimated cost.

The three figures have names. Planned value (PV) is the budget of the work that should have been finished by the reporting date; earned value (EV) is the budget of the work that has been finished, that is, the work valued at its own estimate, not the revenue or business benefit it brings; actual cost (AC) is the cost incurred and recorded for that same work, not the money paid out, since an unpaid invoice is still a cost incurred. The GAO notes that a favourable cost variance can simply be cost that has not yet been recorded. Documents from the US Department of Defense and the GAO, the US Government Accountability Office, use older names for the same three things: BCWS, BCWP and ACWP. From them come two variances and two indices. Cost variance CV = EV − AC, cost performance index CPI = EV / AC; schedule variance SV = EV − PV, schedule performance index SPI = EV / PV. An index below 1 is bad, a negative variance is bad. The total budget of all the planned work, the end point of the PV curve, is called BAC (budget at completion); it excludes the management reserve held outside the plan, so it can be smaller than the total sum approved for the project. The forecast of cost at completion is called EAC (estimate at completion). The simplest forecast assumes the remaining work will be done at exactly the cost efficiency achieved so far: EAC = BAC / CPI. [1] In the solar project, CV is −0.3 billion, CPI about 0.73, SV −0.4 billion, SPI about 0.67, and if efficiency holds, the whole project will cost about 2.75 billion.

The tool's mechanism is to convert physical progress into the same unit as the budget. A metre of cable, a tonne of steel and a week of design cannot be added together, but the estimated budget for each can, and the GAO treats this as the reason EVM can pull very different kinds of work into one picture, provided the project has been divided into well-defined pieces of work. [1] Once finished work is measured by its own budget, one subtraction against the plan gives how late, one subtraction against actual cost gives how expensive, and the two no longer hide each other. The price of this mechanism is that everything depends on how someone decides how much a piece of work has "earned". No single person invented EVM. In its 1967 financial management instructions, the US Department of Defense issued 35 criteria for cost and schedule control systems, C/SCSC for short, applied to most major weapons acquisition programmes, and the core of those criteria was the concept of earned value. In August 1996 industry associations issued guidelines for earned value management systems with 32 criteria, the same as the old set in principle, and the Department of Defense formally accepted them that December. [2] In 1998 ANSI and EIA jointly issued a national standard for earned value management systems; the latest version, EIA-748-D, was revised in January 2019. Since 2006 the US Office of Management and Budget has required EVM for all major federal capital investment projects. [1]

EVM stands on two other tools rather than replacing them. The work breakdown structure gives it the list of work packages to attach budgets to; the GAO calls the breakdown a critical component of EVM. [1] The schedule and the critical path method give it the date each package should finish, which is the shape of the planned value curve. What EVM adds is measurement: the Gantt chart shows the plan and the critical path shows which work decides the finish date, while EVM tells you how much money the project has traded for how much work so far.

Why We Need It

The GAO illustrates with a contract to lay 4 miles of railroad track in 4 weeks for 4 million dollars. After 3 weeks only 2 million has been spent, and a table comparing planned spending with actual spending shows the project running under estimate. But only 1 mile has been laid, 25% of the contract. At that pace the whole contract will cost 8 million and take 12 weeks. [1] A report that compares money with money is missing exactly one figure, the budget of the work done, and without it spending less can mean saving money or can mean falling behind. Spending close to plan can even hide both faults at once, as in the solar project above.

EVM fixes that by giving an early signal, while most of the budget is still unspent. According to the GAO, studies of more than 700 defence programmes have shown limited opportunity to get a programme back on track once it is more than 15% to 20% complete. [1] As an alarm, the signal can be used at once: a CPI of 0.8 at 20% complete is an overrun that has already happened. To forecast final cost, one more condition is needed: the index measured early has to stay close to the outcome. That condition has been measured on defence contracts: at 20% complete, the cumulative CPI of somewhere between about half and more than four fifths of contracts already lay in a narrow band around its final value, depending on how "narrow" is defined. The details are in the research section below. [3] [4]

What Are Its Limits

EVM's schedule index measures time in money, and that is its first built-in limit. SV is EV minus PV, SPI is EV divided by PV. At the end of a project PV stops at BAC, and EV sooner or later also reaches BAC when all the work is done, so a project however late ends with SV at 0 and SPI at 1. Walt Lipke, of the software division at the Oklahoma City Air Logistics Center, gave the example of a project due to finish in January 2002 that finished in April: its SPI curve climbed steadily and touched 1.0 exactly in the month of the late delivery. He wrote that schedule indicators usually lose their value over roughly the last third of a project, and fail completely once the project runs past its planned finish date. [5] The GAO also notes that SPI gradually loses its predictive value and returns to 1.0 when the programme is complete. [1]

The fix Lipke proposed in 2003 is earned schedule (ES): project the current EV onto the PV curve to find the point in time when the plan should have reached that value, then compare that point with the actual time elapsed (AT). That gives SV(t) = ES − AT, in weeks or months, and SPI(t) = ES / AT; if EV falls between two periods, ES is the number of completed periods plus a linear interpolation of the period in progress. Lipke himself noted that the projection is not new, many books on EVM had drawn it; what he added was using it as a schedule index that behaves like the cost index. [5] The horizontal arrow in Figure 1 is that projection.

The second limit comes from the way EVM adds things up. The SV of a whole project is the sum over all its work packages, so getting ahead on work that has float can offset, in the total, a critical task that is running late. According to the GAO, money can be moved between cost accounts but time cannot: an account 3 months early does not make up for an account 3 months late, and a negative schedule variance may not move the finish date if it is not on the critical path. [1] EVM itself does not know which work is critical; the answer has to come from the schedule.

The third limit: every index is measured against the baseline, the time-phased budget plan that PV traces. A CPI of 0.73 says the project costs more than estimated, not whether the estimate was right, and EV measures the amount of work done at estimated cost, not how far that work meets its technical requirements. The GAO writes that if the baseline is not built on a reliable estimate or does not properly reflect the authorised work, the programme risks cost overruns and schedule slips, and advises linking earned value measures with technical performance measures. [1]

The rest is not a built-in limit: half of it is misuse, the other half is the cost of the tool. EV measured by percentages that the people doing the work report themselves measures the optimism of the reporter; the GAO requires percent complete to be based on objective, quantifiable measures of completion. Work measured by elapsed time (level of effort), such as project management, always "earns" exactly what was planned, so when it makes up more than about 15% of the budget the GAO advises a closer look, because the data are no longer objective. A full EVM system is also expensive: the GAO cites the 1 to 2 million dollars some companies have spent to build one from scratch, and notes that a project shorter than six months reporting monthly is halfway through before it has the three data points needed to see a trend. [1]

Examples

A software company measures the same progress with two ways of calculating earned value and gets two opposite pictures, set beside the US Navy's A-12 aircraft programme, where the earned value data told the truth but only the lowest forecast made it up the chain.

Five Features All at 85%, an App Four Weeks Late

An illustrative scenario: a software company builds an appointment booking app for a general clinic, with a budget of 500 million dong for ten features, 50 million each and two weeks each, ten weeks for the whole project, reported weekly. The budget is spread evenly, so by the end of week 6 planned value is 300 million. In reality 3 features have passed acceptance with the clinic, 5 are in progress, 2 have not started, and actual cost is 320 million. The project team calculates earned value by asking each feature lead what percentage is done. All five features in progress are reported at about 85%, so EV is 150 plus 5 × 42.5, or 362.5 million: CPI 1.13, SPI 1.21, and the project looks both cheaper and faster than planned. The finance department, which had set a 50/50 rule for every feature from the start, recalculates by that rule: a feature earns half its value when it starts and the other half when it passes acceptance. The five features in progress earn only 25 million each, EV is 275 million: CPI 0.86, SPI 0.92. In week 9, when the clinic runs a trial, all five features run into faults where they connect to the patient records software the clinic already uses, work nobody had counted in the 85%. The app is finished in week 14 at a total cost of 680 million.

The two reports use the same formulas, the same PV and the same AC. The only difference is who decides how much a piece of work has "earned", and when. The GAO places fixed rules such as 0/100 or 50/50 in the group meant for short work packages that start and finish within one or two reporting periods, exactly like each feature here, and requires the percent complete method to rest on something quantifiable. [1] The 85% figure rests on nothing of the sort. The 50/50 figure is not the truth either: the simplest forecast from it, 500 divided by 0.86, about 582 million, is still below the final 680 million. But it does not grow with the confidence of whoever is reporting, and it raises the alarm in week 6 instead of week 9.

The A-12 Aircraft: the Lowest Forecast Becomes the Only Forecast

The A-12 Avenger II was a stealth attack aircraft that the US Navy contracted a McDonnell Douglas and General Dynamics team to develop, under a fixed-price incentive contract signed on January 13, 1988, with a target cost of 3.981 billion dollars and a ceiling price of 4.777 billion. The cost performance reports, the contractor's monthly earned value reports, showed cost and schedule variances getting steadily worse, and yet the team kept producing best-case estimates of final cost. The administrative inquiry that Chester Paul Beach Jr. conducted for the Secretary of the Navy, reported on November 28, 1990, found one reason: the contractor was under pressure from above to protect cash flow, and progress payments would be cut if either the contractor or the government estimated an overrun. [6]

On the government side, someone read the data correctly. A cost analyst at the Naval Air Systems Command analysed each report, but by office practice her written summary recorded only one figure, the forecast based on the cumulative CPI, the lowest figure she could give; her superiors explained that practice as giving the programme manager "maximum flexibility" in presenting the programme. She still reported higher forecasts orally, and her July 1989 summary carried a footnote that a "weighted CPI" forecast an overrun of the ceiling of about 200 million dollars. The programme manager used the cumulative CPI figure as a ceiling rather than a floor, and in the DAES reports of November 1989 and February 1990 pushed it lower still, believing the contractor's performance would improve. On March 27, 1990 a deputy director for cost management in the office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition analysed the DAES reports and concluded the contract would probably overrun the ceiling by 1 billion dollars; that figure was passed to the programme manager two days later, but it was not in the slides presented to higher levels on April 24. [6] According to an analyst in that office, at 48% complete the cumulative CPI of the A-12 was 0.76 and the TCPI against budget was 1.22: to avoid an overrun, the remaining work would have to be done about 60% more efficiently than the work done so far. On January 7, 1991 the contract was terminated for default by the contractor. [3]

The previous example failed at measurement; this case did not. The index was calculated correctly and someone read it correctly. What failed lay elsewhere, in two places: the report carried one number instead of a range, and both sides choosing the number, the contractor in need of cash flow and the programme manager whom the inquiry found to lack objectivity [2], leaned towards the low one. Beach recorded that in the Department of Defense's experience, a forecast balancing CPI and SPI is usually higher than the cumulative CPI forecast, yet the closest to the outcome. [6] After the A-12, any forecast below the cumulative CPI figure had to be separately justified by the programme office [3], and in 1997 the GAO cited the case as a reason why earned value data must reach people outside the programme office. [2]

The Beach report also contains this sentence: the Department of Defense's experience on more than 400 programmes since 1977 shows, "without exception", that the cumulative CPI does not improve significantly between 15% and 85% of contract completion, and usually gets worse. [6] That sentence is the root of a belief that the research section below tests again. It is the finding of an administrative inquiry, not a study, and it does not tell us whether trusting the early data would have saved the programme.

Some Research on Earned Value Management

EVM is required for major capital investment projects of the US federal government, and according to the GAO, EVM guidelines have been adopted by government agencies in Australia, Canada, Japan, Sweden and the United Kingdom. [1] The value it promises is early warning, and the forecasting part of that value rests on a measurable assumption: the cost index at an early point says a good deal about the index at the end. The three studies below measure exactly that assumption, on US defence contracts and on projects outside defence, and they reach three results that look contradictory until they are set side by side. None of the three compares projects with EVM against projects without it. The GAO itself writes that the benefits of a good EVM system are hard to put into numbers, and cites only expert opinion that the return on investment is reasonable. [1] Within the scope of this piece's search, no study was found that compares the outcomes of projects with and without EVM while controlling for the other differences between them.

The study: In 1993 Christensen and Heise drew 155 contracts from 44 programmes out of the US Department of Defense's Defense Acquisition Executive Summary (DAES) database, from aircraft, missiles and satellites to software and submarines, carried out between June 1971 and February 1991; the sample was chosen by the researchers' judgement, not at random. They called the cumulative CPI stable from a given point of completion if, from that point to the end of the contract, the gap between its highest and lowest value was less than 0.2, and used least squares to see whether CPI moved up or down towards the end. [3] In 2008 Henderson and Zwikael retested on data from outside defence: 12 high-technology projects in Israel, 10 construction projects in the United Kingdom and 4 information technology projects in Australia had enough data to test CPI, counting only direct labour cost. Their question was the one the textbooks tend to repeat: does the final CPI lie within ±0.10 of the CPI at 20% complete, tested with the sign test, a statistical test that does not require normally distributed data, at a significance level of 0.05; each project's stability point was the point after which every CPI value lay within the 0.10 limit. [7] In 2015 Petter, Ritschel and White took 209 development and production contracts from the largest acquisition programmes (ACAT I) of the US Air Force, Navy and Army, from 1987 to 2012, after excluding contracts that had needed an over-target baseline, and tested the same dataset against all three definitions of "stable" that had appeared in the literature: a range below 0.2; a final CPI within 0.10 of the CPI at the point in question; and within 10% of the CPI at that point. Under the last two definitions, a contract counts as stable from a point only if the condition still holds at every point checked after it, every 5% of completion up to 85%; comparing the CPI at one point with the final CPI is not enough. [4]

Findings: At 20% complete, 134 of Christensen and Heise's 155 contracts had a stable cumulative CPI, 86%; at 50% it was 153 of 155. The trend lines showed the cumulative CPI usually falling rather than rising as contracts neared completion. The two authors explained that stability themselves: it comes largely from CPI being a cumulative index, so that the later it gets, the less likely each new month's data is to pull the running total of the past off course. CPI calculated over the last few months was not stable: from 50% complete, only 6% of contracts had a stable three-month CPI. [3] On the data from outside defence, none of Henderson and Zwikael's tests rejected the null hypothesis, for each group or pooled: only 3 of 26 projects had a CPI stable at or before 20% complete, the other 23 stabilised later, many after 80%. Further analysis by the two authors also found no link between early stability and a better outcome; the few projects that finished under budget were precisely those whose CPI improved towards the end. [7] On the 209 defence contracts, Petter and colleagues found 83% with a CPI stable from 20% complete under the range definition, close to Christensen and Heise's figure. Under the within-0.10 definition, the figure at 20% fell to 56%, and only at 55% complete did 81% of contracts become stable. The earned schedule index SPI(t) stabilised later still: under the within-0.10 definition, only at 75% complete did 80% or more of contracts become stable. [4]

What it shows: The three studies measure the same thing: how much the index at an early point says about the index at the end. That is exactly where EVM's forecasting value lies. The 2015 study resolves most of the contradiction. With Christensen and Heise's definition, the result repeats, 83% against 86%. With the ±0.10 definition people tend to quote, measured the way the 2015 study does it, meaning that the CPI at 20% complete and at every later point stays within 0.10 of the final CPI, only about half of contracts qualify. Christensen and Heise's own explanation shows why the two figures are so far apart: a cumulative index fluctuates less and less by itself, so keeping it inside a band 0.2 wide placed anywhere is much easier than keeping it within ±0.10 of the final figure exactly. On this piece's reading, part of the reported stability is a property of cumulative addition, not quite of the project. On scope, the 1993 paper claims to have generalised its results to other services, programmes and types of defence contract [3], while Petter and colleagues hold that the two authors did not generalise beyond their data [4]; the two sides meet only in that the 1993 paper says nothing about projects outside defence. The GAO's 2020 guide still writes as if the rule had been proven: once a programme is 20% complete, the cumulative CPI does not vary by more than 10%. [1] Three limits come with all this. The 1993 sample was chosen by judgement. The 2015 sample left out contracts that had needed an over-target baseline, about 20% of available contracts, and the authors say there is no way to predict which contracts will fall into that group; their results apply only to ACAT I contracts. [4] The 2008 sample is small, 26 projects, and counts only direct labour.

The counter-case: The defenders have three arguments. One, the rule holds exactly where it was measured: with the original definition, the 2015 data repeat the 1993 result almost point for point, and the original paper generalised only within defence contracts. Two, the 26 small commercial projects of the 2008 study say nothing about how rigorously their EVM systems were built, and Wayne Abba rebutted that study; a wobbling CPI may just be a sign of a poorly built baseline. Three, the practical value of CPI lies in the alarm, not in a precise forecast: the GAO treats BAC divided by CPI as usually the best case, so when even that case is over budget the conclusion stands, whether CPI is stable or not. This piece accepts the first argument in full, with one caveat: the original definition is also the easiest one to meet, as the two authors' own explanation about cumulative indices shows. It accepts half of the second. The 2008 sample is small and the quality of the EVM systems in it really is unclear; Abba's rebuttal this piece has read only through Petter and colleagues. But the 2015 defence data, the very kind of contract that produced the rule, also give only 56% under the ±0.10 definition. The gap with 3 out of 26 projects outside defence is real, but late stability is not peculiar to commercial projects. The third argument is the strongest, and this piece accepts it in a narrow form: when the most optimistic forecast is already over budget, it is worth acting at once. It does not accept the reverse, that BAC divided by CPI is a guaranteed floor. On defence contracts, reading it as a floor has grounds, since Christensen and Heise found the cumulative CPI usually falls towards the end [3] and the GAO notes something similar [1]. Outside defence, Henderson and Zwikael recorded projects whose CPI improved towards the end, where the forecast made midway turned out more pessimistic than the outcome. [7]

Why this is useful: It changes how you read the sentence "CPI has stabilised, so the final cost is the budget divided by CPI". Ask three things. Stable by which definition: if it is ±0.10 and must hold from 20% complete onwards, then in the 2015 study's sample of 209 defence contracts only about half qualify. Has this project ever needed an over-target baseline, since every figure in the 2015 study was measured on contracts that never had to. And is the remaining work of the same kind as the work done, since the CPI of laying foundations may say little about installing equipment. The habit that goes with it: report final cost as a range, with BAC divided by CPI as one end of it, not as the answer.

How to Apply It

Earned value management works by converting finished work into the same unit as the budget so that a single comparison can tell being late apart from being expensive, so applying it well means keeping the three figures measuring the same thing: EV calculated against verifiable criteria set in advance, PV held fixed as the yardstick, AC recorded against the right work package; and then reading the result as a forecast range rather than a settled number. The tool suits projects whose scope can be divided into work packages with a budget for each. For short projects, monthly reporting gives too few data points to see a trend in time [1], so either cut the reporting period to weekly, or keep only the core: work packages, a budget for each, and one rule for calculating EV.

  • Settle how each work package earns value before the package starts: A package that fits within one reporting period uses 0/100, a package spanning two periods uses 50/50 or 25/75, a long package is divided into weighted milestones with at least one milestone each period, and work that can be counted, like metres of cable or rack positions, is counted. Percent complete is used only when it rests on something quantifiable, and when level-of-effort work goes above about 15% of the budget, take a closer look [1]. The obvious approach is to ask the people doing the work what percentage is done, and on this piece's reasoning it fails worst exactly when CPI starts being used to judge the person reporting: at that point the percentage has a reason to grow. A completion milestone is harder to push up, but it can still fail if the acceptance criteria are lowered or acceptance becomes a formality, so the person signing off acceptance should not be the one being judged by that same CPI
  • Hold the baseline fixed as the yardstick, keep the reserve outside it, and change it only through approved change control: The GAO defines the performance measurement baseline as the time-phased budget plan against which all actual performance is measured, and describes the management reserve that managers set aside for risks unknown at the start. A portion of the reserve allocated to a specific task becomes part of the baseline, but it can be applied only to in-scope work, and cannot be used to offset or shrink a cost variance that already exists. A change in scope is only one of many legitimate reasons to change the baseline; what is required is that every change is recorded. Once the reserve is used up, any further risk that materialises can only show up as a worsening cost variance [1]. Pull PV down to match every time the work slips and SV will always sit near 0 and measure nothing; quietly moving reserve into the budget of a package that is over cost makes that package's CPI look better in the same way
  • Read the schedule variance in weeks, and set it against the critical path before drawing conclusions about the finish date: SV in money sums over all work packages, and over roughly the last third of a project it drifts towards 0 however late the project is [5]. Calculate earned schedule as well to get the number of weeks late, then ask which package the delay is in and whether that package is on the critical path; a negative SV on work with float may not move the finish date [1], while a positive SV from getting ahead on non-critical work can hide a critical task that is running late
  • Forecast final cost as a range built up from each package, and demand evidence when a forecast needs better efficiency than the past: The GAO treats BAC divided by CPI as usually the best case, notes that the formula multiplying CPI by SPI is often referred to as the worst-case forecast, and advises calculating forecasts at the lower levels of the breakdown as well so that a package doing well does not hide one doing badly. When the project team's forecast is lower than the forecasts from the indices, compare the to-complete performance index (TCPI), the budget of the remaining work divided by the money still available to spend, with CPI: a TCPI above CPI means the forecast assumes the work will be done more efficiently from now on than before, and that assumption needs evidence; by the rule of thumb the GAO cites, a TCPI more than 5 percentage points above CPI means the forecast is most likely too optimistic [1]. Do not treat the CPI at 20% complete as settled, because under the ±0.10 definition only about 56% of the defence contracts in the 2015 study reached that stability from 20% complete [4]. Report the whole range, not just one end of it, and put it in the hands of someone not responsible for the project: on the A-12, the written summary recorded only the lowest forecast, and the programme manager used that very figure as a ceiling [6]; afterwards the US Department of Defense required every forecast below the cumulative CPI figure to be separately justified [3]

B has 300 rack positions left, one week's work; C needs another week to fix the duplicate codes and rerun the test; both stand in front of D, the largest package. If D starts in week 8 at 800 pallets a week, it finishes at the end of week 12, on the very day the lease runs out, and the only week of buffer is gone
  3. Forecast final cost as a range and compare the to-complete performance index: The table just below puts four forecasting methods side by side. The first three apply a single factor to the whole 1,730 million of estimated remaining work, and give from 3,090 to about 3,723. The fourth works package by package: B and C are divided by their own CPI, while D has a fixed-price contract so it stays at 1,160, not multiplied by another package's CPI; the result is about 3,137. To stay within 3 billion, TCPI is 1,730 / (3,000 − 1,360), about 1.05: the remaining work would have to come in cheaper than its own estimate, while the CPI so far is 0.93, a gap of about 12 percentage points, far beyond the 5 points of the GAO's rule of thumb [1]. Package D widens that gap: a fixed price neither rises nor falls, so the work outside D has to carry all of it, 570 million of estimated work in the 480 million left, a TCPI of about 1.19. Those calculations do not prove that 3 billion is impossible. They show that all four forecasts exceed it, including the one with the remaining work going exactly to estimate, and the team has no plan that would make the remaining work cheaper than estimated, so the team tells the directors straight away that the project is likely to overrun the baseline. Against the 3.2 billion budget including the reserve, TCPI is 1,730 / 1,840, about 0.94, close to current efficiency; what supports that figure is D's fixed-price contract, the package that makes up two thirds of the remaining budget

C was accepted at the end of week 7, the same week B installed its last rack position and E finished training. D ran from week 8 to the end of week 11 at 1,000 pallets a week, and the old warehouse was handed back a week early. Total cost was 3,185 million, 185 million over the original 3 billion baseline and within the 3.2 billion approved. Of that 185 million, 40 is the reserve allocated to the extra trucks; the remaining 145 is overrun on the planned work itself. The money to pay it lies within the 3.2 billion approved, but on the report it is not recorded as allocated reserve, so it still shows in full in the cost variance. EVM did not make the software vendor fix its faults any faster. What it did was three specific things: keep the "90%" figure out of the report, show that the overrun sat in one package rather than spread evenly, and tell the directors from week 6, with more than half the budget still unspent, that the project was likely to overrun 3 billion and how much it would take to keep the handover date. The conditions for that result were that the way of calculating earned value was settled before the work began, that the largest remaining package had a fixed price so its forecast did not have to rest on another package's CPI, and that the delay was converted to weeks and placed on the critical path before any decision to spend more. One on-time finish does not prove the next one will be.

Summary

Key Point: The mechanism of earned value management is to convert finished work into the same unit as the budget, so that one comparison with the plan gives how late and one comparison with actual cost gives how expensive, instead of letting the money spent hide both. The most counter-intuitive point is that the famous rule "CPI is stable from 20% complete" holds up under only one definition of stability: on the 209 defence contracts that Petter and colleagues analysed in 2015, under the ±0.10 definition people tend to quote, only 56% of contracts reached that stability from 20% complete. Meanwhile, the money-based schedule index always returns to 1 when a project ends, even a late one. So trust earned value only when the way it is calculated is settled before the work begins, read delays in weeks against the critical path, and report final cost as a range built up from each work package.
