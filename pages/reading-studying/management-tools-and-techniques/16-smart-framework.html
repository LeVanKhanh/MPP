SMART Framework

SMART checks whether a goal is clear and doable; it does not ask whether the goal is hard enough, or whether it measures what actually needs to be achieved

Published: October 25, 2026

Definition

What Is the SMART Framework

SMART is a checklist of five criteria for a goal statement, one criterion per letter. A common version reads the five letters as specific, measurable, attainable (or achievable), relevant and time-bound. The original version from 1981, described below, reads A as assignable and R as realistic, and every goal statement in this piece is checked against that original. Suppose the head of sales at a medical equipment distributor writes a single line into the annual plan: "grow sales". Put that line through the five letters of the original and it has to answer five questions: grow where, measured in what and how much counts as success, who does it, is that level realistic with the resources at hand, and by when. Rewritten, it becomes "grow sales of blood pressure monitors to pharmacies in three Mekong Delta provinces from 2 billion to 2.6 billion dong during 2027, owned by the Mekong Delta sales team lead". Nobody can mark the first line as met or missed. Anyone who opens the books at the end of the year can mark the second.

The word SMART, attached to goals, appears in a two-page article in the November 1981 issue of Management Review. Its author was George T. Doran, at the time president of a management consulting firm in Coeur d'Alene, Idaho, and before that planning director at Washington Water Power Company. Doran's five letters do not match the common version. S is specific: aimed at a particular area that needs improving. M is measurable: quantified, or at least pointing to an indicator of progress. A is assignable: it says who will do the work. R is realistic: it says what result can realistically be reached with the resources available. T is time-related: it says when the result will be reached. [1] The article cites no research. Within the scope of this piece's search, it is the earliest text to use SMART for goals.

SMART only works on one goal statement at a time. It does not say which goals to set, it does not link goals across the levels of an organisation the way OKR does, where each objective comes with a few measurable key results, and it does not keep continuous track of one aspect of operations the way a KPI does. A key result in an OKR or a KPI threshold can be written to pass SMART, but SMART cannot stand in for either tool.

Why We Need It

Without a frame like this, goals in organisations tend to stop at "try harder", "improve quality", "do your best". Locke and Latham, after thirty-five years of research on goals, wrote that people told to do their best do not do their best: the instruction has no external reference point, so each person defines it in their own way, and a very wide range of performance levels all count as acceptable. A specific goal removes the ambiguity about what is to be achieved, and in doing so narrows the spread of performance. [2]

SMART's mechanism is to force an intention into a sentence an outsider can check. S and M remove the ambiguity about what is to be achieved. T sets a deadline, and according to the same review, tight deadlines produce a faster pace of work than loose ones. [2] Doran's A ties the sentence to one person, so that when the deadline arrives someone has to answer for it. In Locke and Latham's review, specific, difficult goals produced higher performance than a do-your-best goal, with effect sizes (d, the distance between two groups measured in standard deviations) of 0.42 to 0.80 in meta-analyses, that is, studies that pool the data of many experiments. [2] That figure measures goals that are both specific and difficult. What follows shows that SMART checks the first half far better than the second.

What Are Its Limits

The first built-in limit: in neither the original nor the common version does any letter ask how hard the goal is. Locke and Latham state plainly that specificity alone does not necessarily lead to high performance, because a specific goal can be hard or easy; what it reliably does is narrow the spread of performance. They found the relationship between goal difficulty and performance to be linear, with the hardest goals producing the highest effort and performance, at effect sizes of 0.52 to 0.82; performance levels off or falls only when it reaches the limits of ability, or when people stop being committed to a goal that is too hard. [2] Doran's R only asks for a result that is realistic with the resources available [1], so a very hard goal can still pass SMART. But on this piece's reading, the common version's A, achievable, pulls the writer towards safe goals: someone judged against a goal has every reason to pick a number they are sure to hit, and nothing in the checklist stands in the way.

The second built-in limit: SMART asks whether a goal can be measured, not whether the measure coincides with what needs to be achieved. Doran himself warned that his criteria do not require every objective at every level to be quantified, that in some situations, particularly for middle managers in staff roles, quantification is not realistic, and that people can lose the benefit of a more abstract objective just to get a number. [1] The common version's R, relevant, asks whether the goal fits the broader direction; it still does not ask whether the chosen number measures what you actually want.

The third built-in limit lies in the type of work. With new, complex work, where people do not yet know how to do it, a specific performance goal can do harm. Locke and Latham cite an experiment by Kanfer and Ackerman in 1989 on an air traffic control simulation: the performance goal kept participants from acquiring the knowledge the task required, and the group told to do their best did better. Winters and Latham in 1996 showed that the fault lay in the type of goal: a specific learning goal, such as discovering a number of different ways to do the task, produced higher performance than a do-your-best goal on complex work. [2] On this piece's reading, M and T pull the writer towards an outcome number with a deadline, and none of the five letters points out that new work may need a learning goal first.

What remains are gaps in how it is used. SMART checks a sentence, whereas Doran wrote that what really matters is an objective together with its action plan. [1] Locke and Latham add conditions without which goals weaken: people must be committed to the goal, and they must get feedback telling them where they stand against it. [2] A sentence that passes all five letters can still miss all three. How to make up for them is set out under How to Apply It.

Examples

Two examples. A recruitment team writes two goals that both pass all five letters and gets two different results. Then the sample objective from the 1981 article itself, set beside forty websites twenty years later, shows how far the five letters have drifted.

Twenty Days to Hire a Salesperson

An illustrative scenario: an electronics retail chain with 18 shops takes an average of 30 days to hire a salesperson, and every shop manager complains of being short-staffed. At the start of Q3, the head of HR sets a goal: "Cut the average time to hire a salesperson from 30 to 20 days in Q3, owned by the recruitment team lead." The sentence passes all five letters: a specific task, a measurable number, someone accountable, a reduction within reach of a four-person team, a deadline. At the end of the quarter, the figure is 19 days. The method was simple: the team dropped the trial selling shift in the shop and took the first person who got through the interview. In Q4, the shops report back: of the 40 people hired in Q3, 14 had left before their 60th day, against 6 of 38 in Q2. The posts are empty again, and the next round of hiring starts from scratch.

The following year, a different team lead receives the same 20-day target but adds two lines. The first is a plan: keep the trial selling shift, merge the two interview rounds into one, and keep a standing list of backup candidates for the shops that are often short. The second is a guardrail metric: the share of new hires leaving before day 60 must not rise above the previous quarter's. At the end of the quarter, time to hire is 21 days, missing the target by a day, and 5 of 37 new hires leave before day 60.

Both goal statements pass SMART equally. The difference lies in two things the checklist does not ask about. What the shops need is someone who can work the counter, and days to hire is only an indirect measure of that; when the measure is the only goal, the quickest way to hit it is to drop the screening. And the second statement comes with an action plan, exactly what Doran saw as the other half of an objective. [1]

The A of 1981 and Forty Websites

The sample objective in Doran's article runs, in substance: by 31 December 198_, develop and put into use an inventory system that cuts inventory costs by 1 million dollars, at a cost of no more than 200 person-hours and 15,000 dollars of initial outlay. The sentence has an area to improve, a number, a deadline and a resource limit. It does not say who will do it. Doran also wrote that the acronym he proposed does not mean every objective must meet all five criteria, only that the closer it comes, the better the objective. [1]

Twenty years later, Robert S. Rubin of Saint Louis University came across a management training manual that read the T of SMART as "trackable" rather than time-bound. He looked at the first 40 websites a search engine returned for "SMART goals", from fitness sites and state agency planning manuals to business articles and university counselling centres. The most common version, on about 10 sites, was Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, Time-bound. The rest scattered. Of all the readings he found, only one touched on difficulty: S read as "specific with a stretch". A had nine more readings, from acceptable, action-oriented, accountable and agreed-upon to assignable, Doran's original meaning; T had well over a dozen variants built around time, plus "tangible" and "truthful". Rubin called this acronym drift, suspected that some versions went little further than a do-your-best goal, and said plainly that his search was not systematic. [3]

What you can use here: two teams that both say "the goal is SMART" may be checking two different things. The assignable A asks who does it, the achievable A asks whether it can be done, and a goal that passes one can still fail the other. Before an organisation adopts SMART as a scoring standard, the first job is to write down what its own five letters mean.

Some Research on the SMART Framework

SMART is often taught as a condensed version of goal research: Rubin calls it a tool for quickly communicating years of robust research on what makes an effective goal. [3] The two founding texts do not cite each other. Doran's article cites no research [1]; Locke and Latham's 2002 review, built on thirty-five years of empirical work, does not mention SMART in its body text [2]. So the value of SMART has to be read on two levels: the evidence about the kind of goal SMART aims at, and the tests of SMART itself. The second level is thin. A randomised trial of 130 obese patients with multiple chronic conditions set SMART goals inside an intervention package that also included behavioural coaching and pedometers; after two months, the intervention group had lost 0.2 kg while the waitlist group had gained 0.1 kg, a difference that was not statistically significant, and because the intervention was a package it cannot isolate SMART's share. [4] A 2023 narrative review by Swann and colleagues, meaning a review that synthesises and critiques the literature rather than pooling data, concludes according to its abstract that SMART is not based on scientific theory, is not consistent with empirical evidence, does not consider the type of goal, is applied inconsistently, lacks detailed guidance, has redundant criteria, is not used as originally intended, and carries a risk of harm. [5] Within the scope of this piece's search, the experiments that test SMART by name cluster in physical activity, and no study was found that compares outcomes in organisations with and without SMART.

The study: In 2002, Locke and Latham summarised their research programme: specific, difficult goals had been tested on more than 100 different tasks, with more than 40,000 participants in at least eight countries, in the laboratory, in simulations and in the field. [2] Swann and colleagues (2020) tested SMART directly: 78 adults, mean age 55.88, were randomly assigned to four groups: an open goal ("see how far you can walk"), a SMART goal, a do-your-best goal, and a control group told to walk at their normal pace; each person did one baseline measurement and then two trials of a six-minute walk. [6] Hawkins and colleagues (2020) repeated the design with 36 people, 18 sufficiently active and 18 insufficiently active by the guidelines, except that each person went through all four conditions; their SMART goal was the baseline distance plus 16.67% for the second trial, then the second trial's distance plus 8.33% for the third. [7] Taking a different line, Schweitzer, Ordóñez and Douma (2004) had 154 students make words from seven letters, over seven rounds of one minute each. One group was told to do its best. The other two were set a goal of nine words per round, the 90th percentile of a pilot group, so that only about one in ten earlier attempts had reached it, and were told the goal was "difficult, but realistic"; one of the two groups was paid 2 dollars for each round in which it met the goal. Participants marked their own work and reported whether they had met the goal, in a setting they believed to be anonymous. [8]

Findings: In the meta-analyses Locke and Latham cite, specific, difficult goals outperformed a do-your-best goal with effect sizes of 0.42 to 0.80; on the most complex tasks, the effect size dropped to 0.41, against 0.77 on the simplest. [2] In Swann and colleagues' experiment, all three goal groups walked further than the control group in both trials; the abstract does not report which of those three groups walked further than which. SMART goals and open goals made the session more enjoyable, but SMART goals brought higher feelings of pressure and tension, while open goals brought higher perceived performance and a stronger wish to exercise again. [6] In Hawkins and colleagues' experiment, insufficiently active people walked further with an open goal than with a SMART goal, while sufficiently active people did the reverse, both differences statistically significant (p < 0.001). [7] In the word task, the two goal groups made an average of 5.83 and 6.17 words per round, against 5.46 for the do-your-best group, a difference that was not statistically significant. The share of people who claimed, at least once, to have met the goal when they had not was 10.5% in the do-your-best group, 22.7% in the unrewarded goal group and 30.2% in the rewarded group. Each goal group differed significantly from the do-your-best group; between the rewarded and unrewarded groups, no significant difference was found. Overstatement clustered around near misses: in rounds where participants made eight words, one short, the share of rounds reported as nine words was 14.81%, 34.48% and 48.48% respectively. [8]

What it shows: The strongest evidence that SMART usually gets attached to is the evidence for goal-setting theory, and that evidence concerns goals that are both specific and difficult; SMART checks the specific half, not the difficult half. The direct tests show that a SMART goal beats no goal on a six-minute walk; as far as the abstract reports, they do not show it beating a do-your-best goal on distance in the same study; and they show its effect on distance flipping with the person receiving it: better than an open goal for people already used to exercise, worse for those who are not. The word experiment shows that a specific goal described as difficult but realistic increases the number of people who overstate, especially after a near miss, even with no money involved. On scope: the SMART experiments are supervised six-minute walks with samples under 80; the overstatement experiment involved university students, small rewards and a single session.

The counter-case: Defenders have three arguments. One, SMART has never claimed to be a theory; it is a mnemonic for quick teaching, and its value lies in making people sit down, set goals and talk them through with others, as Rubin himself acknowledges. [3] Two, a six-minute walk is a long way from a year-long business goal; the evidence worth weighing is the more than 40,000 people in goal-setting studies, and S and M match that theory's condition of specificity, so SMART inherits most of that evidence. Three, overstatement and poor results are failures of use: Locke and Latham write that when goal effects fail to replicate, the cause is usually an error such as a goal that does not match the performance measure, a lack of feedback, a lack of commitment, or a performance goal set where the work needs a learning goal. [2] This piece accepts the first point in full: as a mnemonic, SMART does its job. It accepts half of the second. S and M do match the condition of specificity, but the effect sizes in the meta-analyses measure goals that are specific and difficult, so SMART inherits only half of that condition, which is not enough to lay claim to most of the evidence. It accepts half of the third as well. Locke and Latham's list of errors is sound advice, but most of those errors sit outside the five letters, so passing SMART does not mean a goal avoids them. And overstatement is not only about money: the share of people who overstated in the unrewarded goal group was still twice that of the do-your-best group, and the experiment found no significant difference between that group and the rewarded one, even though it was a study of students with small rewards.

Why this is useful: It changes how you read the sentence "this goal is already SMART". Ask four more questions. How hard is this number compared with what is being achieved now, and who chose it. Is this measure the result you want, or just something easy to count that stands next to it. Is this familiar work or new work, and if it is new, do the people doing it need a learning goal before they take on a performance goal. And when the person judged by the number falls just short, who checks that number. The habit that goes with it: write the guardrail metric and how the number will be checked directly beneath the goal statement, before handing it to anyone.

How to Apply It

The SMART framework works by forcing a vague intention into a sentence that an outsider can mark as met or missed: what, measured how, by whom, by when. So the effective way to apply it is to use it exactly there, as a check once the goal has been chosen, and then to supply the questions it does not ask yourself: difficulty, whether the measure is right, the type of work, and the plan that goes with it. The tool fits best with familiar work, with results that can be measured early enough to change course, and with one person who genuinely controls the result.

  • Choose the result first, then the measure, then write it to pass the five letters: Ask "if this number is hit and everyone is still disappointed, why?", then turn the answer into a guardrail metric written directly beneath the goal. Doran himself warned that forcing quantification can lose the benefit of a more abstract objective [1]; when the measure is only something easy to count that stands next to the result, as days to hire stands next to someone who can work the counter, the quickest way to hit it may be exactly the way to ruin the result, as in the recruitment example above
  • Set difficulty deliberately, against current performance: Write the current level beside the target level and say why you chose that gap. Performance rises with difficulty until it reaches the limits of ability or people stop being committed [2], so achievable should be read as "not beyond ability and resources"; reading it as "certain to be hit" is lowering the goal's ceiling with your own hands. This breaks when people do not yet have the right way of working: Locke and Latham note that with the wrong strategy, a hard performance goal produces worse performance than an easy one [2]
  • For new or complex work, set learning goals and proximal goals before performance goals: A learning goal is a number of ways of working or things to find out, for example finding three ways to approach new customers within six weeks, each one recorded with the kind of customer it works for and the evidence behind it, before sales come into it. On complex tasks, specific learning goals produce higher performance than a do-your-best goal, while performance goals can get in the way of learning [2]; Locke and Latham also cite a business game experiment in which adding proximal performance goals, stage by stage, alongside a distal goal produced higher profits than the distal goal alone [2]
  • Write in the action plan, the feedback schedule and who checks the numbers: Doran treated an objective and its action plan as a pair [1], and goals lose their effect when people do not know where they stand against them [2], so write down who does what, on which dates the numbers are reviewed, and where the numbers come from. The obvious approach is to let the accountable person report the result, and it breaks exactly when the goal is close but not yet met: in the word experiment, in rounds one word short, nearly half the rounds in the rewarded group and more than a third in the unrewarded group were reported as met [8]. Take the numbers from a source the person being judged cannot write into by hand. The experiment found no significant difference between rewarded and unrewarded groups, so the advice not to hang a large bonus on the exact goal threshold is only a management inference from that result

Applying It to a Scenario

A single-site gym sells new members a three-month package. Management notices that many people pay and then tail off from the third week. The measurable problem: of the 120 new members who joined in Q1 2025, only 54, or 45%, were still training at least four times across weeks 7 and 8 counted from their joining date. The goal: raise that share to at least 60% for the new members who join in Q3 2025, with the figure closed on 30 November 2025, when the last member to join in the quarter has just passed week 8. All figures are illustrative.

  1. Choose the result first, then fix the measure and say that it is only a measure: The result management wants is new members renewing when their three-month package ends, but that figure arrives too late to change course. The team picks "still training at week 8" as the early measure and fixes the definition once for every period: still training means at least 4 check-ins at the door across weeks 7 and 8 combined, with weeks counted from the joining date, and the denominator is every new member who joined in the quarter, including those who never came once. The numbers are exported by accounts from the check-in system, not taken from trainers' reports. The renewal rate of the same group of members will be reviewed when their packages end

Training time makes almost no difference, so a crowded gym at peak hours is not the main lead. A one-to-one induction session with a trainer in the first 14 days does: 70% against 32.5%. Q3 is expected to bring about 130 new members, or about 10 sessions a week; the gym has 4 trainers, each taking on about 2 to 3 extra sessions a week, placed in the morning slots that are usually quiet, with no new hires needed. Alongside it goes a proximal goal for weekly feedback: at least 90% of new members get their induction before day 14
  3. Set a guardrail metric and decide who checks the numbers: Trainer hours moved to new members could leave existing members neglected, so the team also tracks the renewal rate of existing members whose packages expire during the quarter, and requires it to stay no lower than Q1's 78%, that is, 156 of 200 members. No bonus is tied to the 60% threshold; the figure is exported by accounts, and the trainers cannot adjust it by hand
  4. The goal is met: about 61.5% of Q3's new members are still training at week 8, above the 60% mark, while the renewal rate of existing members holds at about 79%. In Q3 the year before, measured by exactly the same definition, the figure was only about 43.9%; comparing the same quarter weakens the seasonal explanation, but it does not rule out things that may have changed between the two years, such as package prices, the trainer team or where new members come from. The group with an induction now stays at about 65.3%, below Q1's 70%, which fits the hypothesis that part of the old figure came from self-selection, though it does not prove it. This result rests on three conditions: trainers with free morning hours to take on extra inductions, a definition and data source that do not change between periods, and figures produced by someone outside the trainer team. It is not a controlled trial, so it does not prove that the induction caused the rise. And it is still only the measure: the real result, the renewal rate of the Q3 group, will only be known when the three-month package of the last member to join runs out, at the end of December 2025.

Summary

Key Point: The SMART framework works by forcing a vague intention into a sentence an outsider can mark as met or missed: what, measured how, by whom, by when. The most counter-intuitive point is that the five letters were born as practical advice, with no evidence attached: Doran's 1981 article cites no research and presents no test of the five criteria, his A means assignable rather than achievable, and no letter asks about difficulty, the very thing Locke and Latham's review links to the highest performance. The principle: use SMART to check the goal statement once the goal has been chosen, then ask yourself the questions it does not: is it hard enough, does it measure what needs to be achieved, is this work familiar or new, and who checks the number apart from the person judged by it.
