Objectives and Key Results (OKR)

OKR puts every team's objectives on one board so they can correct course mid-quarter; a 0.7 only means something when nobody loses a bonus over it

Published: October 25, 2026

Definition

What Are Objectives and Key Results

OKR, short for Objectives and Key Results, sets goals in pairs: an objective says where you want to go, and a handful of key results say how you will know you have arrived. Google's guide describes objectives as ambitious, the kind that may leave the person setting them a little uncomfortable; key results are measurable and can be graded with a number on a scale from 0 to 1.0. Each person or team picks three to five objectives, with roughly three key results each, and OKRs are public across the organisation so that everyone can see what everyone else is working on. [1]

John Doerr, who worked at Intel and later introduced OKRs to Google, compresses an OKR into one sentence: "I will (objective) as measured by (key results)". His What Matters site splits OKRs into two kinds. A committed OKR is expected to be fully achieved when it is graded at the end of the cycle. An aspirational OKR, sometimes called a moonshot, is one whose path is still being worked out. The same site notes that, unlike old-style Management by Objectives (MBO), OKRs are kept apart from pay and bonuses. [2] The 0.6 to 0.7 range that Google's guide calls the "sweet spot", along with its remark that anyone who always fully achieves their OKRs is not being ambitious enough [1], is a way of grading that ambitious kind of goal, not a pass mark for every OKR. Whichever kind an organisation uses, it should settle how that kind is graded before the cycle starts.

Suppose a company that makes an English-learning app for children sets a quarterly objective: "Parents see their child learning every week." Three key results go with it: the share of accounts with at least three lessons a week rises from 34% to 50%; the share of parents who open the weekly report rises from 20% to 40%; the monthly renewal rate rises from 61% to 70%. The objective has no number, because it describes a direction. The three lines below it can be graded by anyone who opens the data at the end of the quarter: if the weekly-learning rate stops at 46% and the team grades by distance travelled from the starting level to the target, that line covers 12 of 16 percentage points, a grade of 0.75. Grading by distance travelled is a convention this article uses because it is easy to follow, not a rule of OKR; every organisation has to settle its own grading before the cycle starts.

Google's guide traces the tool back to High Output Management by Andy Grove, Intel's former CEO, where the whole system comes down to two questions: where do I want to go, which gives the objective; and how will I pace myself to see if I am getting there, which gives the milestones, the key results. [1] Doerr's What Matters site calls Grove the father of OKRs, places the tool as a successor to the MBO that Peter Drucker introduced in the 1950s, and says Doerr presented OKRs to Google's two founders in 1999. [3] Google's guide dates the same moment to early 2000. [1] At Intel in those years, the people involved still called the system the MBO process, as the members of the campaign steering committee in the Examples section do. [4] This article could not read Grove's book in the original, so everything it says about how to use OKRs is checked against Google's public guide.

OKR sits close to two other tools in this series and differs from both. The SMART framework checks whether a single goal statement is clear and doable; a key result can be written to pass SMART, but SMART says nothing about linking the goals of several teams, grading them each quarter or making them public. Google's guide lists "business-as-usual OKRs", OKRs that simply restate current work instead of what matters most, among the mistakes to avoid. [1] On this article's reading, whatever has to run steadily every day, such as the on-time dispatch rate of a warehouse that is already stable, belongs to ongoing indicators such as KPIs; OKRs are for the few places an organisation wants to change this quarter.

Why We Need It

In a company with no way for departments to see each other's plans, each head of department keeps the annual plan on their own laptop. Marketing runs a discount in the very month the warehouse is short of stock, engineering piles people onto a feature that sales has already stopped promising, and nobody finds out until customers complain or the year-end review arrives. Having OKRs does not make this go away on its own: in a unit of about 4,000 engineers inside a large multinational software company, after roughly two years of using OKRs, 76% of survey respondents did not know where to find other teams' goals when they needed them, and 59% said they wanted or needed to see other teams' OKRs. [5]

The mechanism of OKR is to set an objective beside a few checkable key results, put both where every team can see them, and grade them again after a short cycle. Separating "where are we going" from "how will we know we have arrived", then answering the second question with numbers, leaves little room at the end of the cycle to argue over whether the goal was met. Making them public lets a dependent team know in advance where another team is putting its effort, and according to Google, each team links its OKRs to at least one organisational objective rather than mirroring every objective above it. [1] The quarterly rhythm and the grading act as a feedback channel: Locke and Latham's summary of goal-setting theory notes that goals lose their effect when people get no feedback on where they stand against them. [6] The grading scale and the aspirational label add something a checklist like SMART does not: they allow a hard target to be set, so that falling short at 0.7 is not treated as failure. That only holds as long as the OKR grade is not a grade of the person.

What Are Its Limits

The first inherent limit: OKR does not choose the objective. It writes down a choice already made and measures how far that choice has gone. An objective that is wrong, measured very precisely, is still the wrong objective, and a public OKR board only lets the whole company see that it is wrong sooner, if the key results measure what actually needs achieving.

That "if" is the second inherent limit. A key result is a measure standing next to the outcome, and Google's guide supplies its own test for this: could the OKR score 1.0 under reasonable circumstances without delivering any direct benefit to the organisation? [1] If so, it is a low-value objective; rewrite it. The test has to be run by hand, because the tool cannot tell on its own whether a number measures an outcome or an activity.

The third limit lies in the stretch setting the tool encourages. In two experiments in which participants ran a simulated airline, stretch goals, compared with moderate ones, helped a few people reach higher results, but many others chose policies that led to bankruptcy, or dropped the goal once they saw that risk coming; results spread out more widely, while the difference in medians between the two groups was not statistically significant. [7] Google's 0.6 to 0.7 range accepts in advance that most aspirational OKRs will not be fully achieved; the experiment shows that falling far short of a target also changes how people decide while they are still working.

The rest is a matter of how the tool is used. Google lists a mistake called sandbagging: a team meets all its OKRs without needing all of its capacity. [1] On this article's reading, that mistake has every reason to appear whenever the OKR grade goes into a bonus formula, because picking a target that is sure to be met is then the sensible choice for the person being graded. And writing OKRs takes real work: in the 4,000-engineer unit mentioned above, setting and writing OKRs was the most frequently mentioned difficulty, and more than half of managers said they were not very effective at turning their own managers' OKRs into goals for their team. [5] Better use reduces both problems, as the How to Apply It section describes; the cost of writing cannot be removed entirely.

Examples

Two examples. A bookshop chain uses OKRs for two quarters in two different ways and gets two very different kinds of grades. Then Intel's 1980 campaign, which Doerr retells as an OKR story, read again through the words of the people who ran it.

Nine Shops, Nine Near-Perfect Grades

An illustrative scenario: a chain of nine bookshops starts using OKRs in the first quarter. The director sets a shared objective: "Parents bring their children back to the shop every month." Each shop manager writes three key results for their own shop, and HR makes the OKR grade 40% of the quarterly bonus. At the end of the first quarter, the 27 key results average 0.97, and 24 of them score a full 1.0. Many lines are activities: "hold four weekend story sessions", "put up campaign posters on both floors". The few lines with numbers have numbers below what the shop had already reached the previous quarter. Meanwhile, the share of members with young children who come back within 30 days stands at 31% across the chain at the start of the quarter and 31% at the end.

In the second quarter, the director takes the OKR grade out of the bonus formula and posts all nine shops' OKRs in the chain's shared group. Each shop rewrites its three lines to measure what has to change, such as the number of members with young children who return within 30 days, instead of the number of sessions held; two shops look at each other's boards and merge their story sessions into one for their neighbourhood. The average grade at the end of the second quarter is 0.61. The chain's return rate rises to 35%.

The two quarters also differ in season, in new titles and in plenty of other ways, so those four percentage points prove nothing about the tool. The useful part is in the first quarter. A rate of 24 out of 27 lines fully met is, on its own, only a signal to check how hard and how good those lines are. What strengthens the suspicion of sandbagging, which Google describes as meeting every OKR without needing all of a team's capacity [1], is the targets set below what the shops had already reached the quarter before; and they appear exactly when the OKR grade decides part of a bonus. When the whole board is close to perfect and the organisation's own number does not move, the first question is whether the key results measure outcomes or the work that was done.

Two Thousand Design Wins and a Trip to Tahiti

At the end of 1979, Intel's 16-bit 8086 microprocessor usually came third when customers picked a chip for a product they were designing, behind Motorola's 68000 and Zilog's Z8000. An eight-page telex from Don Buckhout, a New York district sales manager, set out the losses and pulled the executive staff in; Bill Davidow, then running the microcomputer systems division, was given the programme, called Crush, which ran through 1980. [4] In Doerr's telling, the corporate objective was to establish the 8086 as the highest-performance 16-bit microprocessor family, and the target was two thousand design wins, the number of times a customer decides to use an Intel chip in its own product. [8]

In 2013, five members of the campaign's steering committee sat down to tell the story for the Computer History Museum in Mountain View, and what they said shows how that key result was built. The first number put forward was 1,000, and according to House it "seemed like an enormous number"; then, during planning, it became 2,000. Each salesperson got a quota of roughly one design win a month, about three times the earlier rate according to Jeff Katz, and anyone who hit the quota, provided their whole district hit it too, earned a trip to Tahiti. The committee expected about half the sales force to make it, but by Katz's account about 98% went, and the cost overran the close to one million dollars that had been budgeted. A design win only counted once the customer bought a development system or a board to start working with; a letter of intent from the customer's engineering manager was trusted only for some customers. The result, according to Katz, was something over 2,300. When Rosemary Remacle, who moderated the session and had worked at Intel, recalled that the MBO process was used to take the campaign down into the trenches, House replied: like everything at Intel. McKenna and House also remembered the weekly meetings where everyone had to stand up in front of their peers and say whether their action items were done. [4]

The part that is told less often comes afterwards. Katz recalled that when the campaign ended and the money was spent, chip revenue still had not arrived, because the designs were still being developed on the customers' side; at every quarterly review with the executive staff he was asked where the money from all those design wins was, until IBM PC clones came onto the market around 1983 or 1984. On the most important design win of all, the IBM PC, Dave House said he did not know of any direct link with Crush. [4] Intel's history page, for its part, says the campaign brought in almost 2,500 design wins, including the IBM PC. [9] Doerr writes that by the end of that year Intel had routed Motorola, and that by 1986 the 8086 family had recaptured 85% of the 16-bit market. [8]

This case has three limits: it is told 33 years later by the people involved, Doerr is an advocate for OKRs, and Crush was a whole campaign that included seminars for customers' engineers, a catalogue of future products and two million dollars of advertising, so the part owed to the way goals were set cannot be separated out. [4]

The case teaches two things. Crush's key result was carefully defined, counted by something the customer had done, buying equipment to try out, and it was an early indicator: the revenue came years later. Anyone who sets a key result like that has to accept that for a long time a high score will not come with money. The second is this article's reading. A target that the people setting it found enormous, then doubled, was still beaten by more than 15% when tied to a large reward that almost the whole sales force won together. The accounts do not say whether the target was treated as committed or aspirational; if it was aspirational, then under Google's later scale, beating it like that is a reason to look again at how hard the target was, not enough to conclude it was set low. [1] The Tahiti trip is also a counterexample to this article's own advice to keep grades away from bonuses: a sales quota tied directly to a reward went along with a campaign the people involved regard as a success. It does not overturn that advice, because this was a quota that paid a reward, not an aspirational grade used for learning and course correction, and the quota's share of the result cannot be separated out. But it does show that keeping goals apart from rewards is not a necessary condition for every goal system that works. And in this case, the number grows with each teller: from something over 2,300 in the committee's account to almost 2,500 on the company's page, with the IBM PC folded in, a design win that one committee member said he did not know was directly linked.

Some Research on Objectives and Key Results

Most of the stories about the value of OKRs that this article came across are told by the people who use them: Grove at Intel, Doerr at Google and the companies he invested in. Google's own guide says OKRs are not a comprehensive way to evaluate an individual or an organisation [1], and it offers no data comparing teams that use them with teams that do not. The studies of OKR by name that this article found are all case studies based on interviews and self-reported surveys. One followed two software teams at a Norwegian agency of about 50,000 employees at the end of 2020, when both teams were working remotely: the researchers observed 21 meetings and interviewed all 17 members. OKRs helped the teams prioritise and understand what other teams were doing, but writing measurable key results was the hardest part; the quarterly OKR workshop came to be seen as a tedious event; many people were not convinced OKRs had substantially improved their work; and it took more than a year before the teams began to see benefits. The OKR tracker was open to the whole project, but many people did not spend time reading other teams' OKRs. [10] Another study interviewed 204 staff at 26 Thai organisations that had adopted OKRs and found that OKRs helped people accept performance indicators and align them better with strategy; the author notes that the qualitative approach limits how far the results generalise, and suggests checking them again once OKRs have been used for longer than six months. [11] Within the search for this article, no study was found that compares the results of organisations with and without OKRs. The research below therefore works at two levels: the study with the largest sample among those that measure OKRs directly, and an experiment on stretch goals, the kind of goal that Google's guide emphasises.

The study: Jenna Butler, Thomas Zimmermann and Christian Bird, three researchers at Microsoft, studied a unit of about 4,000 engineers and programme managers inside a multinational software company with tens of thousands of developers. The paper does not name the company. The unit had been using OKRs for about two years. The researchers held 47 semi-structured interviews, then sent a survey to about 4,000 people and received 512 responses, a response rate of about 13%. The paper also notes that in 2021 Microsoft bought the OKR software company Ally.io and, at the time of writing, sold OKR software under the name Viva Goals [5]; Microsoft retired Viva Goals on 31 December 2025 [12]. At the level above, Michael Shayne Gary, Miles Yang, Philip Yetton and John Sterman (2017) had participants act as the CEO of a simulated airline, People Express, modelled on a real company, over 40 quarters, with cumulative profit as the goal. In the first study, 134 managers on an Executive MBA course, with an average age of 36 and more than ten years of experience, were randomly split into 50 teams of two or three; each team was randomly given either a moderate goal, 60 million dollars of cumulative profit after ten years, or a stretch goal, 315 million, taken from the 90th percentile of pilot teams told to do their best, a level only about one in ten pilot teams reached. Each team played three rounds, the first two for learning; the analysis used the third round of the 39 teams that completed all three. The second study had 59 students each run their own airline, added yearly goals alongside the year-ten goal, and paid a small sum for each year the goal was met. [7]

Findings: In that unit, only 45% of teams thought they were effective at measuring goals, and more than 50% of managers said they were not very effective at turning their managers' OKRs into goals and metrics for their team. 76% of respondents did not know where to find other teams' goals when they needed them, 59% said they wanted or needed to see other teams' OKRs, and the organisation was using 12 different tools to track OKRs because each team did it its own way. Setting and writing OKRs was the most frequently mentioned difficulty, almost twice as often as any other, including the shift from measuring output to measuring outcomes. [5] In Gary and colleagues' first study, 61% of moderate-goal teams met or exceeded the target and 33% went bankrupt; among stretch-goal teams, 19% met the target, 43% went bankrupt, and the rest survived without reaching it; the paper reports no statistical test of the difference in bankruptcy rates. Median cumulative profit, the profit of the team in the middle when all are ranked, was 6 million dollars in the stretch group and 120 million in the moderate group, but no statistically significant difference was found (p = 0.81). The standard deviation, a measure of spread, was 219 million against 113 million, a significant difference. The second study repeated the pattern: medians of 185 million against 138 million, with no significant difference found (p = 0.19); standard deviations of 526.6 million against 114.5 million; risk-adjusted profit, mean profit divided by its spread, significantly lower in the stretch group; and goal commitment, the same in both groups before the first round, fell to 3.99 on a 10-point scale in the stretch group before the last round, against 6.12 in the moderate group. [7]

What it shows: Butler and colleagues' study shows that, in a unit that had used OKRs for two years, the hard part lay in two places: writing key results that measure outcomes, and making OKRs actually visible across teams; public in theory does not become public in practice without one shared place. The Norwegian study, which Butler and colleagues cite, is consistent with this from the other side: even with a shared place, people still did not read it. [10] Butler and colleagues' study does not show that OKRs improve results: the data are self-reported, from one company, collected with an unvalidated survey, as the authors themselves note. [5] In a complex environment with delays and many feedback loops, Gary and colleagues' two experiments found no statistical evidence that stretch goals raise median results compared with moderate goals; what they did measure was a wider spread of results and, in the second study, falling commitment. On scope: the experiments do not name OKR, the goals were not reset each period, and there was only one profit goal. [7]

The counter-case: The defenders have three arguments. First, Intel and Google are living proof: two companies that used OKRs over decades and grew to a scale few companies reach. Second, the airline experiment is not OKR: the goal ran over ten years, was not reset each quarter, and in the second study was tied to money, whereas OKRs are reset every quarter and kept apart from bonuses. Third, goal-setting theory has already shown that hard goals go with higher performance, with effect sizes, the distance between two groups measured in standard deviations, of 0.52 to 0.82. [6] This article accepts the first point for exactly what it proves: OKRs can work in very large organisations. But those two companies were chosen as stories after they had already succeeded, and Gary and colleagues name this very error: sampling on success and generalising the benefit to every company. [7] The second point the article accepts in part. The authors list non-adaptive goals as a limitation themselves; but the second study had already added yearly goals, graded once a year, and the pattern held, while the first study was a classroom exercise and the paper describes no reward at all. [7] Whether resetting the target each quarter would prevent people from giving up has, within the search for this article, not been tested by anyone. The third point is true at the aggregate level, but the same summary notes that the effect is smaller on the most complex tasks, 0.41 against 0.77 on the simplest, and that performance levels off when people stop being committed to a goal that is too hard [6]; that drop in commitment is exactly what the airline experiment measured. The two sources are more consistent with each other than opposed.

Why this is useful: It changes the question you put to an aspirational OKR. The question stops being whether the target is high enough and becomes what happens if the team falls well short of it, and who carries that. Where falling short only means getting less, or getting there later, than hoped, a stretch target is fine. Where falling short could mean a blown budget, lost customers or an unsafe coach journey, relabelling the OKR as committed solves nothing: a committed target set too high creates exactly the same pressure. Limits on safety, quality and budget have to stand as guardrail metrics under both kinds of OKR, and the committed label belongs only where there is already a basis and the resources to deliver. Before concluding that OKRs are working in your organisation, ask three people in three other teams whether they can find your team's OKRs. And keep two timing questions apart: the cost of writing OKRs, the quality of the key results and whether teams can see each other can be checked within the first quarter or two, while the benefits need longer to follow; the two teams in Norway took more than a year to start seeing them. [10]

How to Apply It

OKR works by setting an objective beside a few checkable key results, putting them where every team can see them, and grading them each quarter, so the effective way to implement it is to keep the numbers honest and early enough to correct course: define the measure before setting the target, keep grades away from bonuses, choose deliberately what is aspirational and what is committed, and let each team link its own OKRs to the organisation's. The tool fits best with the few changes an organisation wants to make in a quarter, when results can be measured quickly enough and several teams have to pull in the same direction.

  • Write key results as outcomes, with a definition of the measure and its data source: Google's guide says key results should describe outcomes, not activities, and that words like "consult", "help" or "analyse" usually signal an activity [1]. Write down once for the whole cycle what is counted, what the denominator is, and where the number comes from. Crush only counted a design win once the customer had bought a development system, and trusted letters of intent only for some customers [4]; a key result that the people doing the work can record by hand tends to be met sooner than the real outcome
  • Keep OKR grades out of bonuses and individual appraisals, and say so: This is what keeps the grading range of an aspirational OKR meaningful, but separating them on paper is not enough: if the head of department still opens the OKR board when deciding bonuses, the people writing OKRs will know, and the whole board will drift back towards perfect scores, as in the bookshop chain's first quarter. When nearly every line scores a full 1.0, put each target next to what was reached in the previous cycle; a target below the old level is the warning sign, while a high score on its own says nothing
  • Label each OKR as committed or aspirational, and tell the teams that depend on it: For aspirational OKRs, Google tells dependent teams to expect delivery on only about 70% of what was promised [1]. The obvious approach is to set every OKR high because "hard goals bring high performance", and it breaks down in complex work where falling far short could be disastrous: in the airline experiment, the stretch group had a higher share of bankrupt teams, 43% against 33% in the first study, and there was no statistical evidence of a higher median profit [7]. That experiment only warns about targets set too high; it did not test committed OKRs, so changing the label does not by itself resolve the risk. Write limits on cost, quality and safety as guardrail metrics under both kinds; use the committed label only where there is already a basis and the resources to deliver, and set the improvement beyond that level as a stretch, where the consequences of missing it are acceptable
  • Let teams link themselves to the organisation's OKRs, publish them in one place, and review them mid-quarter: According to Google, the organisation settles its objectives first, then each team sets its own OKRs to support at least one of them; no team has to mirror every OKR above it, and teams revisit their OKRs several times a quarter to drop what will no longer happen and concentrate on what is borderline [1]. Copying OKRs down through every level is a common gap, because middle managers are the ones who have to translate goals into work, and in the 4,000-engineer unit that Butler and colleagues studied, more than half of managers said they were not very effective at it. In the same place, where twelve tools for tracking OKRs ran side by side, the authors recommended a single place to store OKRs for the whole organisation [5]

Applying It to a Scenario

A sleeper-coach operator runs the Ho Chi Minh City to Da Lat route, 24 departures a day, with three departments: dispatch and drivers, maintenance, and ticketing and customer care. Complaints about timing are the most frequent kind of complaint. The measurable problem: in the third quarter of 2025, out of 2,208 departures, only 1,523 left on time, meaning not before the time printed on the ticket and no more than 10 minutes after it, about 69.0%. What the operator needs to achieve in the fourth quarter of 2025: fully meet one committed OKR, at least 80% of departures leaving on time, and cover about 0.6 to 0.7 of the distance on an aspirational OKR about arrival times, without the number of speeding events going up. All numbers are illustrative.

  1. Write the objectives, key results and measurement definitions before setting targets: The directors write two OKRs. A committed OKR, with the objective "Coaches leave at the time printed on the ticket", and one key result: the share of departures leaving on time, meaning not before the printed time and no more than 10 minutes after it, because a coach that leaves early makes passengers miss it. An aspirational OKR, with the objective "Passengers can trust the arrival time too", and two key results: the share of journeys reaching the final stop no more than 30 minutes after the expected arrival time printed on the ticket, with early arrivals counted as on time, and the number of timing complaints per 1,000 passengers. Departure and arrival times come from the journey-monitoring device on each coach, not from drivers' logbooks; the denominator is every scheduled departure; complaints are counted per passenger because passenger numbers in the fourth quarter may differ from the third. Two guardrail metrics come with them. A safety guardrail: speeding events per 10,000 km driven must not rise above the third quarter's 4.1 (272 events over about 662,400 km, that is 2,208 journeys of about 300 km each). A schedule guardrail: all 24 departures a day must run

Achieved: the committed OKR scores 1.0 with about 81.2% of departures on time, the aspirational OKR scores about 0.63, and speeding events fell from about 4.1 to about 3.9 per 10,000 km; this guardrail catches one kind of trade-off against safety, it does not show the journeys are safer. The 0.63 sits in the expected range for an aspirational OKR, but most of it comes from the complaints line, and complaints may have fallen thanks to ticketing's delay messages; the on-time arrival line only reached about 0.45, so there is not yet enough to say passengers trust the arrival time more. Ticketing's own OKR fell short, at about 88% against 90%. In the fourth quarter of the previous year, measured with the same definition and the same schedule, only about 67.8% of departures left on time, so the season can hardly explain the improvement; that comparison still cannot rule out other things that may have changed, such as new drivers or a stretch of road under repair. The on-time rate among coaches with a turnaround of 90 minutes or more held at about 83%, close to the third quarter's 84%, and most of the overall gain comes from that group now making up 95% of departures, which fits the lead from step 2 but does not prove it is the cause, because this was not a controlled trial. The result was achieved under three conditions: the schedule had room to reorganise the rotation without adding coaches, the definition and data source did not change between periods, and OKR grades were in nobody's bonus formula. What the operator really wants, passengers coming back for their next trip, will only be known once it calculates how many fourth-quarter passengers booked again in the first quarter of 2026.

Summary

Key Point: OKR works by setting an objective beside a few checkable key results, putting them where every team can see them, and grading them each quarter. The most counter-intuitive point is that stretch goals, the part Google's guide emphasises with an expected grade of 0.6 to 0.7, have thinner evidence than their reputation suggests: Gary and colleagues' two simulated-airline experiments, which do not name OKR, found no statistical evidence that stretch goals raise median profit compared with moderate goals, while results spread more widely and commitment fell; and the studies of OKR by name that this article found are all case studies based on interviews and self-reported surveys within single organisations, none comparing organisations with and without the tool. The principle: define the measure before setting the target, keep OKR grades out of bonus formulas, write limits on cost, quality and safety as guardrail metrics for every OKR, set stretch targets only where the consequences of missing them are acceptable, and check whether other teams can actually find your team's OKRs.
