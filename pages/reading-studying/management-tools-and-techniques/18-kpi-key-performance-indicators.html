Key Performance Indicators (KPI)

KPIs pile an organisation's effort onto a few numbers, and that same pressure both improves the real work and makes the numbers easy to bend

Published: October 25, 2026

Definition

What Are Key Performance Indicators

In any organisation, almost everything can be counted: orders, calls, hours worked, complaints. A KPI, short for key performance indicator, is one of the few indicators picked out of that pile because it tells you whether the organisation is doing well at the thing its success depends on. BSI, introducing the ISO 22400 standard on KPIs for manufacturing operations management, defines KPIs as quantifiable and strategic measurements that reflect an enterprise's critical success factors. [1] The glossary of NIST, the US National Institute of Standards and Technology, puts it more briefly: a KPI is a metric of progress toward intended results. [2] Neither definition requires a KPI to have a target. In common use, and in the main cases in this article, each KPI comes with a target or a threshold and is reported period by period.

Suppose a water utility tracks more than two hundred numbers each month. The board picks three: the share of water lost from the network, the average hours of supply interruption per household, and the number of days it takes to install a new meter. Each has a green, amber and red threshold. The other two hundred numbers are still measured, but only these three are read out at the start of the management meeting, and when one of them changes colour, someone has to stand up and explain. Those three numbers are the company's KPIs, not because they measure more precisely, but because they were chosen.

Within the scope of this article's search, the term KPI is not tied to any one originator or founding text, so the article does not tell its history. Every use of KPIs here is checked against the studies and audit reports later in the piece, not against any particular how-to book.

KPIs sit close to two other tools in this series. The SMART framework checks whether a goal statement is clear and doable; a KPI threshold can be written to pass SMART, but SMART says nothing about which indicators to track. OKR sets a few key results for what an organisation wants to change in a quarter, then grades them at the end. On this article's reading, KPIs watch what has to hold steady over many periods, such as the share of emergency calls an ambulance reaches in time; when an organisation decides to push a KPI to a new level next quarter, that indicator can become a key result in an OKR. This boundary is the article's own way of dividing the work; none of the sources it read draws it.

Why We Need It

An organisation that has not singled out any indicator as key is rarely short of data; it is short of people looking at the data. Bevan and Hamblin, discussing systems for measuring hospital performance, quote a criticism from 1917 of hospital reports packed with tables of illnesses and lists of operations, produced to impress the organisations and subscribers and not used by anybody. [3] Putting the data in a manager's hands does not guarantee much either. The two authors cite a controlled experiment in south central Wisconsin: quality rankings for one group of hospitals were spread widely among the local public, a second group received only a private report for its managers, and a third received nothing. The group whose results went public made significantly greater efforts to improve quality than the other two; the managers of the poorly ranked hospitals in that group acted out of concern for their hospitals' reputations. [3]

The mechanism of a KPI is to pick a few indicators out of the many things that can be measured, give each a target or threshold, report it regularly, and let the number carry consequences for the people who answer for it, so that attention and resources flow to wherever the number is off. The word "key" does most of the work. In England from 2002 to 2005, every ambulance service was given a public star rating based on a small set of key targets and a set of secondary targets; failing a key target could pull a service down to zero stars, get it publicly named as failing, and put its chief executive's job at risk. Reaching 75% of life-threatening calls within 8 minutes was a key target. According to the figures the services reported, the number of services meeting it rose from 3 out of 32 in 2000-2001 to 26 out of 31 in 2004-2005. In those same services, the share of less urgent calls reached within 8 minutes, which was not part of the rating, barely improved: in every year only one service reached the 75% mark. [3] Choosing which indicator is key is choosing where effort will pile up. That pressure does not distinguish between two ways of making a number look better: doing the real work better, or adjusting the number.

What Are Its Limits

The first built-in limit: a KPI shows where something is off, not why. A falling response rate might come from a shortage of vehicles, badly arranged shifts, roadworks or a change in how times are recorded; the number cannot tell these apart, and whoever reads the dashboard has to look for the cause with other tools.

The second built-in limit: every indicator measures only part of the work, and the part that is not measured is easily pushed aside. In Bevan and Hamblin's data, the target for reaching less urgent calls within 14 or 19 minutes sat among the secondary targets; when it stopped being a target, in the first six months of 2004-2005, performance worsened. Staff in many places also alleged that some services concentrated ambulances in densely populated areas, where most calls could be reached within 8 minutes, at the expense of rural areas; the two authors call this a logical response to the target, and a trade-off between efficiency and equity that the people who set the target would surely not have wanted. [3]

The third built-in limit lies in the very act of using numbers to decide. In 1976 the social scientist Donald Campbell wrote that the more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures, and the more apt it will be to distort the very processes it is meant to monitor. His examples: in some places, police judged by their clearance rate did not record crimes they had not solved; in an employment office, rating staff by the number of people placed led staff to concentrate on the easiest cases and leave behind those who most needed help, according to a study he cites. Campbell noted that his own evidence was predominantly anecdotal. [4] Charles Goodhart, analysing the British government's reliance on money supply targets to control inflation, said something close: any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed on it for control purposes. [3] For KPIs, this means that the link between an indicator and the real result, the very reason for choosing the indicator as key, can weaken precisely because the indicator was chosen.

The rest are gaps in how the tool is used, and those can be fixed. Loose definitions are one: in England, which calls counted as life-threatening was left to each service, and the share of calls classified that way varied fivefold between services, a gap that persisted from 2001 to 2005. [3] Numbers recorded by the very people being measured are the second. Both have remedies in the How to Apply It section.

Examples

Two examples, at two scales. A made-up driving school, where a KPI rises by twenty percentage points without anyone teaching better. Then the 2014 system-wide audit at the US Department of Veterans Affairs, where a 14-day wait-time target was met on paper by changing the starting point of the measurement.

Ninety Per Cent of Ninety People

An illustrative scenario: a driving school runs one course of 120 learners each quarter for the standard car licence. For the March course, the school books all 120 for the driving test when they finish; 84 pass at the first attempt, or 70%. The director sets the instructors a KPI: a first-time pass rate of at least 85%, counted towards the quarterly bonus. The June course also has 120 learners. This time the instructors book the test for only the 90 they feel sure will pass; the other 30 are advised to take extra driving lessons, for a fee, and sit the test later. 81 of the 90 pass at the first attempt, or 90%. The KPI is met and the instructors get their bonus.

Put three numbers side by side and the story changes. Out of 120 enrolled learners, the June course has 81 holding a licence after the first test, or 67.5%, against 84, or 70%, for the March course. The two courses differ in their learners and in many other ways, so that gap of 2.5 percentage points does not show that the later course was taught worse. What is certain is that the KPI rose by 20 percentage points while the number of people with a licence did not rise, because the denominator shrank from 120 to 90. Nobody altered the data. The instructors only chose who would be counted, the same concentration on easy cases that Campbell described in an employment office.

This KPI is harder to game when it is calculated on enrolled learners, within a fixed period from the date of enrolment, and has a guardrail metric beside it, that is, an indicator that catches a number being met while the real work suffers: here, the number of learners whose test was put off. When a rate rises fast, the first question is whether the denominator is still the same.

Fourteen Days and the Desired-Date Field

In 2014, at the direction of the Secretary of Veterans Affairs, the Veterans Health Administration audited scheduling practices across its whole system, to find out whether allegations of improper scheduling were isolated cases or something systemic. From 12 May to 3 June, audit teams went through 731 facilities and interviewed staff face to face with a confidential questionnaire. At the centre of the story was a target of no more than 14 days' wait for a new appointment, a target written into performance contracts. Waiting time was calculated from the "desired date", the date the veteran wanted to be seen, which scheduling staff entered into the system. [5]

Of the 3,036 people who answered this question, 13% said they had been told by supervisors or others to enter in the desired-date field a date different from the one the veteran had asked for; at 107 of the 140 parent facilities, or 76%, at least one person gave that answer. Respondents at 24 sites said they felt threatened or coerced into entering specific dates, and at 14 sites someone said they had been disciplined over scheduling. They described a numbers-driven system with unrealistic performance measures, creating a highly stressful place to work. [5]

Asked what got in the way of veterans being seen on time, staff rated the lack of provider appointment slots highest, followed by thin clerical staffing and the 14-day standard itself. The report concluded that, with the slots available, meeting the 14-day target for new appointments was simply not attainable, and that imposing it before working out the resources required was an organisational leadership failure. The action plan attached to the report included removing the 14-day goal from performance contracts and suspending the administration's executive performance awards for fiscal year 2014. [5]

The report sets out its own limits: it was a rapid review, not an investigation; it could not interview every employee, did not assess intent or individual culpability, and the 13% includes changes of date that were within policy, such as a follow-up date set by a provider. [5]

The case teaches two things about KPIs. The starting point of the measurement was entered by the very organisation being judged, so when the target could not be met by doing the work, it was met by changing the starting point. And the target was set without checking it against the capacity behind it; the report gives an example, a veteran entirely happy with an appointment the following month could still count as "failing" the 14-day standard. The report's own conclusion is that the agency must re-examine its entire performance management system, including whether its access measures and targets are realistic. [5]

Some Research on Key Performance Indicators

Among the studies this article found, few take the word KPI as their subject. What gets studied is management by indicators with targets and accountability attached, under other names: targets, performance measures, performance measurement systems. That is not every KPI, but it is exactly the way of running KPIs this article analyses. In retelling the sources on English health care, the article keeps the word "target" for an indicator that has a level to reach. Within the scope of this article's search, no study was found comparing the results of organisations with and without KPIs. The comparison available is between different ways of using the same indicator. The star ratings in England's public health service offer such a comparison, with two rare advantages: within the same organisations there were key targets, secondary targets and indicators outside the rating; and across the countries of the UK, the same ambulance target was used in different ways. What follows reads the ambulance study as the main one, then two studies of the four-hour target in emergency departments for the defenders. All of this evidence comes from public health care, where the consequences were mainly public rankings and fines on whole hospitals; applying it to a company that pays individual bonuses on KPIs is a step of inference, not something that has been measured.

The study: Gwyn Bevan of the London School of Economics and Richard Hamblin of the Healthcare Commission, the body that ran the star ratings in their final years, looked at ambulance services across the countries of the UK between about 1999 and 2006. The shared target: 75% of emergency calls that may be immediately life-threatening, known as category A calls, must be reached within 8 minutes. In England from 2002 to 2005, this target and the target of 95% of category A calls within 14 minutes in urban areas or 19 minutes in rural areas were the two key targets of the star ratings; reaching category B and C calls within 14 or 19 minutes was a secondary target; reaching category B and C calls within 8 minutes was not in the ratings at all. In 2004-2005, reaching fewer than 65% of category A calls within 8 minutes counted as significantly underachieving, enough on its own to pull a service down to zero stars. In Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, the 8-minute target was not tied to any public ranking. The two authors compare the published figures of English services over five years, compare figures across countries, and draw on the Commission for Health Improvement's reviews of gaming, including an analysis of how response times were distributed around the 8-minute mark. [3]

Findings: In 2000-2001, before the star ratings, only 3 of 32 English services met the 8-minute target for category A calls; in 2004-2005 it was 26 of 31, and the range between the best and worst services narrowed from 42-87% to 68-88%. The target for reaching category B and C calls within 8 minutes, outside the ratings, was met by just 1 service every year. For England as a whole, the 75% level was reached from 2003. Wales, after missing its target and lowering it, still reached fewer than 60% of category A calls in time from 2004, as did Scotland, and Northern Ireland reached 51% in 2005-2006. The other side is in the same data. A Commission for Health Improvement review found that at one service, responses taking more than 8 minutes had been "corrected" to under 8 minutes; a reanalysis of all the data showed such manual corrections in around a third of services, given away by distributions with sharp breaks or spikes right at the 8-minute mark. The Commission estimated that the most dramatic corrections improved performance by at most 6%, while improvement since 1999 was up to 20%. In 2006 an audit found 6 of 31 services had not recorded response times accurately; in 2007 a report on the Wiltshire service found control room staff had manually changed the times of nearly 600 category A calls between April 2005 and July 2006. [3]

What it shows: Within the same organisations, indicators placed among the key targets with public consequences improved sharply in reported performance, while indicators outside that group did not; and the same system of consequences went together with manual corrections in about a third of services. The comparison between countries is weaker than the comparison within England, because the countries differ in many ways beyond how performance is measured: the Auditor General for Wales attributed the Welsh service's poor results to problems of strategy, leadership, governance, process, infrastructure and systems, people and culture. The study measures reported response times, not whether more patients survived; the two authors themselves ask how far meeting the 8-minute target translates into outcomes for patients, and suggest also measuring the rate of return of spontaneous circulation after cardiac arrest. [3] One of the two authors worked at the very body that ran the ratings.

The counter-case: The defenders have three arguments, and the third is the strongest. First, gaming is real but small: the Commission's estimate was at most 6%, against an improvement of up to 20%. [3] Second, without consequences the numbers barely move: the Wisconsin experiment shows this with a control group, and Wales, with the same target but no ranking, stayed below 60%. Third, where people could measure the final outcome too, a target with heavy consequences saved lives. Jonathan Gruber, Thomas Hoe and George Stoye studied the four-hour target in English emergency departments, in force from December 2004 and backed by fines: 98%, and from November 2010 95%, of patients had to be admitted, discharged or transferred within four hours of arrival. Using 24.7 million visits to 184 emergency departments from April 2011 to March 2013, and the bunching of times just before the four-hour mark, they estimate that the target cut the time affected patients spent in the department by 21 minutes, and cut 30-day mortality in that group by 14%. [6] Steven Kelman and John Friedman, with data from all 155 English hospital trusts between 2003 and 2006, found no evidence of the distortions people had feared from this target. [7] This article accepts the second and third arguments for exactly what they show, and only within their scope: at these hospitals and ambulance services, figures made public or tied to a ranking drew more improvement than figures that stayed on management's desk, and in emergency departments that improvement reached the final outcome. They do not show that every KPI needs an adverse consequence to work. The first argument the article accepts only in part. The 6% is one review's estimate for the most dramatic corrections; the audits of 2006 and 2007 still found inaccurate recording, in Wiltshire after at least two changes of leadership. [3] The amount of gaming is not a constant of the tool; it depends on whether anyone checks. Nor does Kelman and Friedman's result, which the article read only in abstract, mean the four-hour target distorted nothing: Gruber and colleagues, with later data and a different method, found doctors ran more tests and admitted more patients to beat the clock, those extra admissions used almost no inpatient care, as if they were placeholders, and according to the 2018 working paper, government payments for inpatient care rose by about 5% because of the target. The authors still conclude that the target reduced mortality cost-effectively. [6] Both things hold: the target saved lives, and the target distorted medical decisions. The claim that does not stand is that the distortion is small enough to need no checking.

Why this is useful: It changes the question you put to an important KPI. The question stops being just whether the number has been met and becomes four smaller ones: what happens to the cases just past the threshold; which work in the same unit has no indicator, and whether it is getting worse; who records the figures, and who checks them; and whether there is an outcome measure alongside that tells you the better number has brought the real work with it. Next time a KPI on time or on the share of cases within a threshold rises fast, ask for the distribution, not just the rate, and look just below and just above the mark. A spike right before the mark, as at 8 minutes or at four hours, does not prove cheating, because people may genuinely be hurrying to make it. But it is the first place to pull the original records and compare.

How to Apply It

A KPI works by singling out a few indicators as key, giving them a target or threshold, reporting them regularly and letting the number carry consequences for the people who answer for it, so the effective way to implement it is to make the easiest path to a better number the same as the path to better work: define the indicator so it is hard to adjust, watch what is not measured, give the indicator consequences matched to capacity, and check the data on a schedule. The tool fits best with work that recurs regularly, has results that can be measured after each period, and where the people who answer for the indicator genuinely have ways to improve it. Before all four comes the choice: which result decides the unit's success, what grounds there are for thinking the indicator moves with that result, who can do what when it changes, and whether the information is worth the cost of collecting and checking it. An indicator that fails any of these four is not worth keeping honest, however well you manage it.

  • Write a full definition for every KPI, and have someone other than the person being measured record the starting point: Write it once for every period: which cases count, where the clock starts and stops, which system the figures come from. In England, which calls were category A was left to each service, so the share of category A calls varied fivefold, and the same 8-minute target may have been measuring different sets of calls in different places [3]; at the Veterans Health Administration, the starting point of the wait was the desired-date field entered by the schedulers themselves [5]. An audit report quoted by Bevan and Hamblin observes that data that is easy to manipulate gives people both the motive and the opportunity to do so [3]
  • Put a guardrail metric beside each KPI for what it does not measure, and an outcome measure: Work without an indicator does not hold its level on its own, as with category B and C calls within 8 minutes in England [3]. When the KPI measures a step on the way to the result rather than the result itself, the outcome measure answers the question the KPI cannot: whether the better number has brought the real work with it. Bevan and Hamblin suggest also measuring the rate of return of spontaneous circulation after cardiac arrest, because if response times improve and that rate does not move, you have to ask how the number improved [3]
  • Give the KPI real consequences, and settle the target only after counting the capacity behind it: The obvious way to stop cheating is to remove all consequences, and it swaps one failure for another: in the Wisconsin experiment, only the hospitals whose results went public tried significantly harder to improve, and Wales, with the 8-minute target but no public ranking, stayed below 60% from 2004 [3]. Consequences need not be money; in England they were a public ranking and a unit's name in the newspaper. But consequences plus a target beyond capacity push people towards adjusting the numbers: the Veterans Health Administration audit concluded that the 14-day target could not be met with the appointment slots available [5]. Before settling a target, count the vehicles, the appointment slots, the staff, and ask how much more the target demands
  • Look at the distribution around the threshold and check a sample of original records on a schedule: A rate of cases within the threshold hides the shape of the data; the distribution does not. The time corrections at ambulance services came to light because the distribution of response times spiked at the 8-minute mark, and the Commission for Health Improvement tested this by comparing the number of calls actually recorded between 8 and 9 minutes with the number predicted from the shape of the distribution between 7 and 8 minutes [3]. A spike is not proof of cheating, because in English emergency departments, Gruber and colleagues argue that the bunching before the four-hour mark is not just recoding: patients really did spend less time in the department [6]; it is the first place to pull records and compare. Once a year, check whether the KPI still moves with the final result the way it did when you chose it

Applying It to a Scenario

A residential construction company is building six projects, town houses and low-rise flats, and has a purchasing department of five people. Every time materials reach a site later than the date they are needed, a crew has to stop or switch to other work. The measurable problem: in the second quarter of 2026, of the 640 approved materials requests whose date needed fell in the quarter, only 448 had their full order arrive on site no later than the date needed on the request, or 70.0%; the site diaries record 96 crew shifts spent waiting for materials out of 9,600 shifts worked, or 10.0 per 1,000 shifts. What the company needs in the third quarter of 2026: the materials KPI in the green zone, at 80% or above, without purchase prices or the quality of goods getting worse, and fewer shifts spent waiting for materials per 1,000 shifts worked. All figures are illustrative.

  1. Answer the four questions for choosing an indicator, then assign the KPI to the right place: What decides success here is that crews do not stop work for lack of materials. The number of shifts spent waiting for materials, from the site diaries, measures that directly, but it includes waits for goods the sites buy locally themselves and is only compiled at the end of the month, so the company keeps it as an outcome measure to check against. The indicator chosen as the KPI is the share of materials requests that arrive by the date needed: the second-quarter diaries give late outside purchases as the reason for most shifts spent waiting for materials; when this share changes, both the purchasing department and the site managers have something to do about it; and the data is already on the materials requests and the goods-received notes, at the extra cost of a monthly sample check. Because both sides produce this number together, it is a shared KPI for the materials supply process, tracked by the director, not a KPI belonging to purchasing alone. Separating the KPI from the outcome measure like this is the scenario's choice; another company could make the number of waiting shifts itself the KPI
  2. Write the definition and choose who records the figures before setting the target: The denominator is every approved request whose original date needed falls in the reporting period, including rush requests; a request approved at the end of a quarter whose date needed falls in the next quarter counts in the next quarter, and a request whose date needed has passed without the goods arriving still counts as late. The date needed is the date written on the request when the site manager signs it; changing it requires the site manager's signature, and the KPI still uses the original date. The arrival date is the date the site storekeeper signs the goods-received note; the storekeeper belongs to the site, not to purchasing. "Arrived by the date needed" means the full quantity, to the specification on the request, signed for by the storekeeper no later than the date needed; a partial delivery does not count as arrived until the order is complete. Thresholds: green from 80%, amber from 70% to under 80%, red under 70%. Two guardrail metrics: purchase prices, calculated by comparing each delivery with the start-of-quarter reference price for the same item code, specification and delivery terms and summing the differences by delivery value, no more than 2% above reference, so that simply buying more expensive goods does not on its own make the metric worse; and the share of requests with goods returned for wrong specification no higher than the second quarter's level of about 1.9% (12 of 640 requests)

Achieved: the KPI is in the green zone with about 80.9% of requests arriving in time, both guardrail metrics stay within their limits, and shifts spent waiting for materials fell from 10.0 to 6.0 per 1,000 shifts worked. In the third quarter of the previous year, measured with the same definition, only about 69.0% of requests arrived in time, so the season struggles to explain the rise; that comparison still cannot rule out other things that may have changed, such as two projects entering the finishing stage. Purchasing's own rate, in time among early requests, held at 87.5%, close to the 88% of the second quarter; most of the overall rise came from that group now making up about 85.7% of requests, that is, from site managers sending requests earlier. That fits the clue in step 3 but does not prove it is the cause, because this is not a controlled trial. Even so, if this KPI were called purchasing's alone, the department would be credited with a rise that its own figures place mostly elsewhere. The shifts spent waiting for materials are the outcome measure: they show the real work moved in the same direction as the KPI, not how much of that the KPI itself contributed. Checking 60 requests found 3 discrepancies that could have made the KPI look better in the very first quarter, while it was not yet tied to money; the records do not say whether that was deliberate or careless. The result was reached under three conditions: site managers had a weekly programme firm enough to order 7 days ahead, the arrival date was recorded by the site storekeeper and the date needed followed the weekly programme, and someone checked the original records every month. If the KPI goes into bonuses next quarter, that checking has to get denser, not thinner.

Summary

Key Point: A KPI works by singling out a few indicators as key, giving them a target or threshold, reporting them regularly and letting the number carry consequences for the people who answer for it, so the organisation's attention flows to exactly those numbers. The least intuitive point is that this pressure runs in two directions at once within the same system: in Bevan and Hamblin's study, English ambulance services met the key 8-minute target far more often while a target outside the ratings barely moved, and the same ratings system went together with manual corrections of response times in about a third of services. Removing consequences is not a sure way out either: in the Wisconsin experiment the two authors cite, only the group of hospitals whose results went public tried significantly harder to improve. Principles: define each KPI so the people being measured cannot record its starting point themselves, put a guardrail metric beside it for what it does not measure and an outcome measure for what really matters, settle the target after counting capacity, and look at the distribution around the threshold together with a sample of original records on a schedule.
