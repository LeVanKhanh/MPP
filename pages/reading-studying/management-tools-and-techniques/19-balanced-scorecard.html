Balanced Scorecard

The balanced scorecard puts four groups of measures on one page and ties them together with an assumed chain of cause and effect, and most of the tool's value, and most of its risk, sits in that chain

Published: October 26, 2026

Definition

What Is the Balanced Scorecard

The balanced scorecard is a way of writing an organisation's strategy down as a handful of measures sorted into four perspectives, where a measure in one perspective is chosen because whoever builds the scorecard believes it pulls a measure in another. Its original form was a single page of measures, each perspective answering one question about the organisation. In their 1992 article in Harvard Business Review, Robert Kaplan and David Norton asked four questions: how do customers see us, for the customer perspective; what must we excel at, for the internal process perspective; can we continue to improve and create value, for the innovation and learning perspective; and how do we look to shareholders, for the financial perspective. [1] [2] According to Kaplan, the article grew out of a multi-company research project at the Nolan Norton Institute on measuring performance in companies where intangible assets were central to creating value. Over the following fifteen years, as thousands of private, public and non-profit organisations took it up, the two authors extended it into a tool for describing, communicating and implementing strategy. [3] Their later articles call the third perspective learning and growth. [4] Arthur Schneiderman, who ran non-financial measurement at the semiconductor maker Analog Devices from 1986 to 1992, writes on his own website that the first balanced scorecard was created there in 1987; that is the account of someone who was in the room. [5]

This piece uses the four perspectives under their later names, and reads the scorecard as a tool for implementing strategy. Read that way, every measure on the card comes with a target, the specific work that will be done to reach it, and someone in charge of it; the scenario at the end builds a scorecard in exactly this way. The trio of measure, target and work to be done is how this piece builds a scorecard, and has not been checked against Kaplan and Norton's own wording: the piece has not read their articles or books in full, and what they wrote is checked against introductory pages, abstracts and secondary sources, named at each point.

Suppose the directors of a chain of five physiotherapy clinics build a scorecard for the coming year. Instead of the twenty numbers in the monthly report, they pick one measure for each perspective and stack them from the bottom up: the share of physiotherapists trained in writing rehabilitation exercise plans; the share of patients given a home exercise plan at their first session; the average number of sessions each new patient attends, within a ten-session course of treatment; and the average revenue per new patient. A real scorecard usually has several measures per perspective; the example keeps one so the picture stays readable. The four numbers share a page because the directors believe they link up into a chain, and that chain is their strategy written out in measures. In a 1996 article quoted by Syrus Islam, Kaplan and Norton wrote that a scorecard should contain the full set of complex cause-and-effect relationships among the critical variables, including leads and lags, and that this chain of cause and effect should run through all four perspectives. [6] Figure 1 draws the clinics' chain.

The nearest entry in this series is key performance indicators, KPIs, the few measures picked because they reflect what decides whether an organisation succeeds or fails. On this piece's reading, the balanced scorecard is a way of choosing and arranging measures like these: it forces them to come from four perspectives, and it forces whoever picks them to say why the measure below pulls the measure above. A scorecard can consist entirely of KPIs; a table of KPIs split into four columns is not yet a scorecard if nobody can draw the arrows between the boxes. OKRs set a few key results for what an organisation wants to change in a quarter; when the strategy calls for pushing a measure on the scorecard to a new level, that measure can become a key result in an OKR. This division of labour is the piece's own; none of the sources it read draws it.

Why We Need It

A company that only reads its financial statements knows very well how last quarter went, and very little about next quarter. Revenue and profit are the end result of work done earlier: training people, fixing processes, keeping customers. The 1992 article opens with the line "What you measure is what you get", then argues that traditional financial measures suited the industrial age. [1] Kaplan later wrote that if companies want to manage their intangible assets better, they have to bring the measurement of those assets into their management systems. [3]

The balanced scorecard's mechanism has two parts. The first is limit and balance: a few measures from each perspective sit side by side on one page, so a good financial number cannot hide a bad customer number, and anyone who wants to push one box up has to see which box is paying for it. The second is the chain of hypotheses: each measure on a lower level is chosen because people believe it pulls the measure on the level above, so the non-financial measures act as early signals of financial results. The second part is what separates the tool from a table of measures in four columns. It is also where the tool demands patience, because the lower measures move first and the financial results move later. In the study of 201 small and medium-sized firms in Spain described below, feedforward use of the scorecard, meaning it is brought to the table regularly, went with faster growth in sales per employee measured from 2010 to 2012 and to 2013, but not when measured to 2011, the year of the survey. [7]

What Are Its Limits

The biggest inherent limit lies in the chain of hypotheses itself. An arrow on the scorecard records what the person who built it believes; proving it is another matter, and not every arrow is a causal relation. Hanne Nørreklit pointed out in 2000 that some relations between measures are logical relations, which arise from a system built on logic, and that statistics can neither confirm nor refute them; only deduction can test them. Islam gives an example: when credit sales rise, receivables rise, because that is how the accounting system is built. At the two companies where arrows thought to be causal have been tested carefully, the results were weak. Mary Malina, Nørreklit and Frank Selto used Granger causality tests, a statistical test of whether changes in one measure come first and help predict changes in another, on the measurement system of a Fortune 500 company, one of the five hundred largest companies in the United States, that had used the same system for more than seven years, and found relatively little evidence for the assumed causal relations. Huelsbeck and colleagues found very weak evidence at another US company that had used its measurement system for more than ten years. All three studies are cited through Islam's 2016 conceptual review. [6]

The second inherent limit comes from lags. If a lower measure takes a year or two to pull the financial measure, a scorecard read every quarter will show the lower level moving long before the upper level has time to change, and in that gap nothing on the card tells you whether the arrow is right or wrong.

The default four-perspective template has no box of its own for suppliers, the community or employees as parties who share in the organisation's results. That is a limit of the template, and an organisation can add one: among the 227 articles about the scorecard published in the Netherlands between 1992 and 2004 that Geert Braam, Jos Benders and Stefan Heusinkveld examined, a few authors proposed adding a whole employee perspective. [8]

The rest are gaps in how the tool is used, and they can be fixed. Whoever evaluates a scorecard may overlook the unique measures each unit chooses to fit its own strategy, and a scorecard tied to bonuses may be pulled over towards the financial side. Both have been measured, in the Examples section, and both have remedies in How to Apply It.

Examples

Three examples. A furniture maker, made up for illustration, whose scorecard has all four perspectives and no chain linking them. Then two studies of how superiors read a scorecard that holds both common and unique measures. Then a large financial services firm that tied the scorecard to its branch managers' bonuses, and in the end dropped it.

Twenty-Four Measures in Four Boxes

An illustrative scenario: a furniture manufacturer with 300 workers hires consultants to build a balanced scorecard. Three months later the card has all four perspectives, each with six measures taken from reports the departments already produce: revenue, profit margin, customer satisfaction score, defect rate, training hours, and nineteen other numbers. At the end of the first quarter the financial box is green and the customer box is green, but training hours are down and the rate of goods returned for wrong dimensions is up. The managing director asks which number to worry about first. Nobody can answer, because no measure was chosen with a reason that connects it to another. The card is just the old reports rearranged into four boxes.

The card is rebuilt from the strategy, which this time is written out as one sentence: the company wants to win orders from hotel chains by delivering custom-designed furniture on time. Only from that sentence does a chain emerge: carpenters can read the customer's technical drawings, so custom orders come out right the first time, so hotel orders are delivered on time, so revenue from project customers grows. Twenty-four measures become eight. The rate of goods returned for wrong dimensions stays, because it sits on the second arrow. Training hours in general are replaced by the share of carpenters who have taken the drawing-reading course, because every kind of training hour counted towards the old number while only one kind sits on the chain. The numbers taken off the card are still tracked by the departments; they are simply no longer read at the strategy meeting.

The eight-measure card is not necessarily right: the new chain is still a hypothesis, and only the data of the next few quarters will show whether trained carpenters make fewer mistakes. What the example teaches is a quick test for your own scorecard: point at any measure on the card, ask which measure it pulls, and see whether anyone can answer.

The Unique Measures Evaluators Pass Over

Advocates of the scorecard recommend that each unit in an organisation build and use its own scorecard, with measures that capture that unit's own strategy. Such a card usually has two kinds of measures: common measures, which every unit has; and unique measures, which only this unit has because its strategy is different. Marlys Lipe and Steven Salterio studied how superiors use the two kinds when evaluating a unit's performance. The finding: only the common measures affected the superiors' evaluations. [9]

Rajiv Banker, Hsihui Chang and Mina Pizzini ran an experiment that took the question further, with one added condition. When evaluators were given detailed information about each unit's strategy, measures linked to the strategy weighed more in their evaluations than measures not linked to it, and more than the common measures as well. Without that information the pattern reversed, and Lipe and Salterio's result came back: evaluators relied on common measures more than on unique ones. [10] The piece read both studies through their abstracts, so it does not cite sample sizes or effect sizes.

The unique measures are where the scorecard speaks about the unit's strategy, and also where it is most likely to be skimmed when someone else reads it. The two studies point to a cheap fix: give the evaluator the unit's strategy to read first, and mark which measures connect to that strategy.

From Four Perspectives Down to One Revenue Number

Christopher Ittner, David Larcker and Marshall Meyer carried out a field study of a balanced scorecard bonus plan at a large financial services firm. The plan was subjective: superiors rated branch managers against the scorecard, but were free to decide how much weight each measure carried. That very subjectivity let superiors strip the "balance" out of the bonus. They put most of the weight on financial measures, brought in factors that were not on the card, changed the criteria from one quarter to the next, ignored measures that predicted future financial results, and gave weight to measures that did not predict the desired results. [11]

Many branch managers complained of favouritism in how bonuses were awarded and of not knowing which criteria were being used. In the end the firm dropped the system and replaced it with a bonus formula based on revenue alone. The authors argue that psychology-based explanations may be as good as, or better than, economic ones for explaining how the firm measured performance. [11]

The case shows how far a scorecard can travel once it becomes a tool for dividing money and the weights are not fixed in advance: the firm started with four perspectives and ended with a single financial number, the very thing the scorecard was built to complement. The case does not tell you whether the scorecard helped the branches perform better in the years it was running.

Some Research on the Balanced Scorecard

The balanced scorecard is widely used: according to a 2009 report by CIMA, the Chartered Institute of Management Accountants, cited by Malagueño and colleagues, adoption stood at 60% among large firms and between 25% and 31% among small and medium-sized ones. [7] Being widely used does not mean being used the way its two authors intended. In the Netherlands, a 1999 survey by a consulting firm of 120 chief executives, mostly at large manufacturing companies, found that 19% used the scorecard; asked why, about 90% named measuring performance, 80% named monitoring, and fewer than 40% said they had used it to clarify or update strategy, as cited by Braam and colleagues. [8] Within the scope of this piece's search, no randomised trial comparing organisations with and without a scorecard has been found; the comparative studies the piece could read are all observational. Braam and colleagues cite two such studies, by Braam and Nijssen and by Davis and Albright, both from 2004, and sum them up as saying that using the scorecard in support of strategy has a positive effect on performance, while using it without a link to strategy can lower performance; the piece has not read those two studies. [8] Fabien de Geuser, Stella Mooraj and Daniel Oyon, using survey data from 76 business units, report that the scorecard has a positive impact on organisational performance, mainly by tying management processes together and by empowering the people doing the work; the sources of that improvement, in their account, are strategy being translated into concrete action, strategy discussion becoming a continuous process, and departments fitting together better. The piece read this study through its abstract, and the abstract does not say how performance was measured. [12] What follows reads one study with open full text that measures exactly that question: how the scorecard is used, and how financial results follow. The evidence on the arrows inside the scorecard was covered in the Definition section.

The study: Ricardo Malagueño, Ernesto López-Valeiras and Jacobo Gómez-Conde surveyed firms with 10 to 250 employees in the food and beverage industry in Spain. Of 5,814 firms in the SABI database of financial statements, 2,979 had an email address; the questionnaire was sent between February and May 2011, and 201 firms returned usable answers. Seventy firms, or 35%, said they had adopted the scorecard. The main variable was not whether a firm had a scorecard but how far it used the scorecard for feedforward: regularly looking at the gap between actual results and targets in order to discuss, learn and change course, rather than reviewing it now and then to grade results already past. The variable adds up scores on four questions about learning, creativity, discussion and management attention around the scorecard; firms without a scorecard score 0. Financial results were taken not from what respondents said but from financial statements: the change in sales per employee from 2010 to 2011, 2012 and 2013. The authors also measured managers' self-rated performance and the level of product innovation, by questionnaire. [7]

Findings: After controlling for other variables, feedforward use of the scorecard went with higher growth in sales per employee when measured to 2012, a standardised coefficient of 0.297, and to 2013, 0.287, both significant at p < 0.05; within the same model, the further a standardised coefficient is from 0, the stronger the association. For the change measured to 2011, the association was not significant. According to the authors, the improvement also did not show up in managers' own assessment of performance. Use of the scorecard also went with more exploitative innovation, meaning improvements to the design, packaging and products a firm already has, with a coefficient of 0.206; for exploratory innovation, meaning entirely new products, the coefficient was minus 0.018 and not significant. The link to financial results was stronger in long-established firms. When the financial measure was switched to the change in net income, the results did not change. [7]

What it shows: In this sample, using the scorecard more for feedforward went with faster growth in sales per employee measured from 2010 to 2012 and to 2013. That is an association, and the design does not allow anything stronger. Those two points are also not the time from adopting a scorecard to its taking effect: the questionnaire only asked whether a firm had adopted the scorecard, not since when. The authors read the result as a lagged effect; that is their reading. How the scorecard was used was measured by managers' own reports in a single survey, and firms that choose to use it this way may differ in other respects, such as the general quality of their management. The comparison group lumps together firms with no scorecard and firms that have one but use it only for feedback, so the study cannot separate the effect of having a scorecard from the effect of how it is used. Usable answers came from only about 6.7% of the firms with an email address; the authors tested for non-response bias and found no significant difference. The sample sits in a single industry that the authors themselves describe as mature, stable and capital-intensive, in a single country; they note that generalising calls for caution, and that the cross-sectional, survey-based design is a limitation. [7]

The counter-case: The defenders have two arguments, and the second is the stronger. First, this study avoids two weaknesses common to survey research: its results come from financial statements rather than from respondents, they are measured in later years rather than at the same time as the questionnaire, and they hold when the financial measure is changed [7]; the two 2004 studies cited by Braam and colleagues [8] and the study of 76 business units by de Geuser and colleagues [12] point the same way. Second, a scorecard does not need a statistically valid causal chain to be worth having. In the Fortune 500 company's measurement system, Malina and colleagues found finality relations, where an organisation picks one of several means to reach a result according to its own policy, and logical relations, standing in for causal ones; the company went on using the system that failed the tests, and its performance improved over time. At the company studied by Huelsbeck and colleagues, management looked at the test results with neither surprise nor concern, and still planned to expand its operations on the basis of that measurement system. [6] On this argument, the value of the scorecard lies in forcing management to state its strategy as a chain that everyone can see and everyone can argue with. The piece accepts the largest part of the second argument: an unproven chain can still be a good way to write a strategy down and talk about it, and Malagueño and colleagues' finding, where what went with growth was the scorecard being discussed regularly, is consistent with that reading. That part comes at a price. An untested chain can still be used to build a scenario, as long as its assumptions are stated, but it should not be treated as a confirmed relation for a confident forecast, still less used to assign responsibility when bonuses are shared out, as if the lower measure were certain to pull the upper one; and a company that keeps improving while using a measurement system that failed the tests tells you nothing about what the system contributed to the improvement. The piece accepts the first argument within its proper scope: an association that appears at the later measurement points, in small and medium-sized food firms in Spain, with feedforward use. The study has no measurement from before the firms adopted the scorecard, and its design, which lumps two groups together, cannot say whether simply having a scorecard makes a firm grow faster. The piece has not read the two 2004 studies or the study of 76 units in full, so it does not know how far their designs separate the effect of the scorecard.

Why this is useful: It changes the question you ask of a scorecard, from "do we have one" to "how is it used". In this study, what went with growth was the scorecard being brought to the table: looking at the gap, asking why, changing next period's work. A scorecard opened only for the year-end grading lands in the comparison group. And in this sample the financial gain showed up only at the later measurement points, not the first. At your next scorecard review, make the meeting end with at least one decision that changes a specific piece of work for the next period, noting the arrow on the card that decision rests on; and before reading the financial level, write down how long the arrow linking it to the level below needs to play out, given your own mechanism and business cycle, and only then choose the period to judge it by.

How to Apply It

The balanced scorecard works by putting a few measures from four perspectives on the same page and linking them with a chain of hypotheses running from capabilities, through processes and customers, to financial results, so the way to implement it well is to write that chain out before choosing any measures, keep it in front of whoever reads the card, and test each arrow instead of taking it as given. The tool suits organisations that have a strategy they can state in a sentence, enough periods of data to test against, and people with enough time to discuss the card regularly. For small firms, Malagueño and colleagues note that the kind of use that goes with better financial results demands a great deal of management attention and employee involvement, resources that small and medium-sized firms are often short of. [7]

  • Write the strategy as a chain of hypotheses first, then choose the measures: Every measure on the card must sit at the start or end of an arrow; a measure that sits on no arrow belongs in a departmental report, not on the card. This step is often skipped: in the 1999 Dutch survey cited by Braam and colleagues, fewer than 40% of respondents said they had used the scorecard to clarify or update strategy [8]. Mark which arrows are logical relations too, such as revenue equals number of customers times price, so that nobody wastes effort testing them with data, and nobody mistakes them for evidence for the strategy [6]
  • Have evaluators read a unit's strategy before they read its scorecard: The unique measures are where the card speaks about the unit's strategy, yet in Lipe and Salterio's study only the common measures affected evaluations [9]. The obvious move is to drop the unique measures so every unit's card looks alike and is easy to compare; doing that erases the reason each unit has its own card. In Banker and colleagues' experiment, when evaluators had detailed information about strategy, strategy-linked measures counted for more than even the common measures [10]. One page of strategy placed in front of the card, with a mark against each measure that sits on the chain, is enough to start
  • Fix the weights and criteria before the period starts, and only then consider tying the card to bonuses: At the financial services firm studied by Ittner and colleagues, superiors were free to set the weights, and that subjectivity pushed the weights towards financial measures, brought in factors from outside the card and changed the criteria from quarter to quarter, until the system was dropped for a formula based on revenue alone [11]. Write the weights down before the period, cap the subjective adjustments and record the reason for each one. Run the card for at least one period with no money attached, to learn whether the measures capture the right work before anyone has a motive to make them look good
  • Test each causal arrow with data that runs long enough, and revisit the strategy when an arrow breaks: In Ittner and Larcker's 2003 survey of 157 companies, cited by Islam, most companies did not test the relationships between the variables in their own measurement systems [6]. For each arrow, compare units, customer groups or periods with high and low values of the lower measure and see whether the upper measure differs, and wait out the lag of that particular arrow, judged by the organisation's own mechanism and business cycle: the 90-day cancellation rate of a cohort of subscribers can be read after three months, while the revenue from a project contract may take a year to arrive. The 2012 and 2013 points in the Spanish study [7] are the measurement points of one sample, and cannot serve as a general waiting time. A comparison like this is only a lead. When an arrow does not hold, revisit the strategy at that point before replacing the measure; swapping in another measure to make the card look better throws away exactly the information you have just gained

Applying It to a Scenario

A company sells point-of-sale software to grocery shops and small convenience stores, charges a subscription of 300,000 dong a month, and has eight support staff. New shops tend to leave very early. The measurable problem: of the 600 shops that activated the software in the fourth quarter of 2025, 132 cancelled the subscription before day 90 after activation, or 22.0%. What the company needs for the cohort of shops activating in the second quarter of 2026: a 90-day cancellation rate of no more than 18%, without cutting the subscription price, without hiring more support staff, and with the share of reopened support tickets no higher than the 10.0% of the fourth quarter. The directors build a cut-down balanced scorecard for this one goal, not a scorecard for the whole company. All figures are illustrative.

  1. Write the strategy as a chain of hypotheses: The strategy, in one sentence: keep new shops by clearing their first problem fast, because many of those problems are about e-invoicing, an area in which only three of the eight support staff have been trained. That sentence gives four levels: support staff have taken the e-invoicing course (learning and growth); a new shop's first support ticket is resolved within 24 hours (internal process); the new shop does not cancel within 90 days (customer); monthly subscription revenue from the cohort of new shops (financial). The two lower arrows are hypotheses to be tested. The top arrow is a logical relation: at a fixed price, the cohort's revenue equals the number of shops remaining times 300,000 dong, so it needs no data to test
  2. Choose the measures, write their definitions, and drop the measures that are not on the chain: The first draft of the card has 11 measures, including social media follower counts and an annual overall satisfaction score; the seven measures that sit on no arrow go back to the relevant departments' reports, leaving the card with four measures plus a guardrail metric. A quarter's cohort is every shop with an activation date in that quarter. "Cancelled within 90 days" means the shop requested cancellation, or failed to pay a fee that fell due, before day 90. The "first ticket" is the first support ticket within the first 30 days; "resolved within 24 hours" means the shop itself clicks to confirm it is resolved within 24 clock hours of sending it, so the people being measured do not record their own end point. The guardrail metric is the share of first tickets reopened by the shop within 7 days, to catch tickets closed quickly while the problem is still there
  3. Test the two lower arrows on fourth-quarter data before spending money on training: The table below breaks down the 600 shops of the fourth quarter of 2025. 480 shops, or 80%, sent at least one ticket in their first 30 days. First tickets handled by trained staff were resolved within 24 hours in 70.0% of cases; by untrained staff, in only 27.5%. Shops whose first ticket was resolved within 24 hours cancelled at 10.0%; shops that waited longer cancelled at 35.0%. Both gaps are large, and they are leads, not yet causes: the three trained staff are also the three longest-serving, and slowly handled tickets may be the harder ones, from shops that were already wavering. The cost of trying is low: a two-day course for five people, taken between shifts so nobody has to leave the support rota. The company decides to go ahead, and plans to check the two arrows again on the next cohort's data

  2. Settle how the card will be read before it runs: The card is reviewed every month at a meeting of the managing director with the support team lead and the sales team lead; the first page of the meeting pack is the strategy sentence and the four levels of the chain, and the card comes on the page after. Every meeting ends with a decision for the coming month, noting the arrow that decision rests on. This quarter the card is not part of anyone's bonus formula. The support team lead is assessed on the 24-hour measure and the guardrail metric, not on revenue, because revenue sits two levels away from the support team's work
  3. Read the card mid-period and change the work, not the definition: At the May meeting, of the 250 first tickets from the second-quarter cohort so far, 155 had been resolved within 24 hours, or 62.0%. Going back over the slow cases, the team finds that most were tickets sent after 6 pm, which sat waiting until the next morning while the 24-hour clock kept running. Someone suggests counting only working hours to make the number look better; the managing director keeps the definition, because grocery shops trade into the evening and that is when their problems happen too. The decision for the month is to put one person on an evening shift until 9 pm, rotating among the eight, resting on the arrow from fast resolution to keeping shops
  4. Score the second-quarter cohort on exactly the same definitions, look again at the two arrows, and compare with the same quarter a year earlier: The cohort for the second quarter of 2026 has 650 shops, more than the fourth quarter because of an advertising campaign by the sales team. The two comparisons give the fall more context, but they cannot rule out a change in customer mix, or other things that changed in the same quarter, such as a software update. The cohort's monthly revenue rose by 23.4 million dong, and this financial number should not be read as the reward for the whole chain. By the logical relation in step 1, if the second-quarter cohort had kept only 78% of its shops, as the fourth-quarter cohort did, its 650 shops would have come down to 507, with revenue of 152.1 million dong; half of the rise, 11.7 million dong, comes from the cohort being larger, the other half from the retention rate. It is also revenue, not profit: the cost of the course and of overtime for the evening shift, if any, is not on the card, and keeping the same headcount does not mean total costs did not rise. The result was achieved under three conditions: most of a new shop's first problems were things the course could teach; the end point of the measure was confirmed by the shop itself; and the card was discussed every month to make decisions, like the evening shift in May. The card does not yet tell you whether the shops still there after 90 days will stay until the end of the year. To know that, you would have to add another arrow, and wait a few more months of data to test it.

Summary

Key Point: The balanced scorecard works by putting a few measures from four perspectives, financial, customer, internal process, and learning and growth, on the same page and linking them with a chain of hypotheses, so that non-financial measures give early warning of financial results. The most counterintuitive point is that this chain, the part that makes the tool what it is, has weak evidence behind it at the two companies where it has been tested carefully, though two cases cannot stand for every scorecard: according to Islam's review, Malina and colleagues found relatively little evidence for the causal relations in the measurement system a Fortune 500 company had used for more than seven years. Testing is also rare: in Ittner and Larcker's survey of 157 companies, also via Islam, most did not test the relationships between their own measures. What goes with results instead is how the card is used: in Malagueño and colleagues' study of small and medium-sized food firms in Spain, regularly bringing the card to the table for feedforward went with faster growth in sales per employee at the 2012 and 2013 measurement points. The principles: write the strategy as a chain before choosing measures, give evaluators the strategy to read before the card, fix the weights before tying the card to bonuses, and test each causal arrow with data that runs long enough.
