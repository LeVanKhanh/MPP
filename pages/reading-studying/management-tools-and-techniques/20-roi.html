ROI (Return on Investment)

ROI puts every spending decision on the same ratio so they can be compared, and the price of that comparison is that scale, time and the way the return is counted all sit outside the number

Published: October 26, 2026

Definition

What Is Return on Investment

Say a company's operations department has two proposals on the table. A software package costing 200 million dong saves 100 million a year. A packing line costing 2 billion dong brings in an extra 600 million of profit a year. Both figures are after the running, maintenance and depreciation costs of the investment itself. The second proposal brings in far more money, but it also swallows ten times the capital. Return on investment, ROI for short, is the division that lets the two be compared on the same yardstick: the return divided by the capital put in. Counting the return per year, the software gives 50% and the packing line 30%.

The division comes in two common forms, and this piece uses both. The first measures a unit or a whole business: one period's profit divided by the capital tied up in that unit. According to the Hagley Museum and Library, in 1914 Donaldson Brown, then the company's assistant treasurer, built a formula that combined earnings, working capital and the capital in plant and machinery into a single index, and called it return on investment. [1] The historian David Hounshell, citing Alfred Chandler, writes that for the first time the calculation brought financial, capital and cost accounting together in one place, and that Brown did this while DuPont was diversifying hard, to measure the results of the businesses it already had. [2] Algebraically, ROI in this form splits into the product of two ratios: profit over sales, the profit margin, times sales over capital, the capital turnover. The identity holds by construction, so a unit with a low ROI may be earning a thin margin on each unit of sales, or may be leaving too much capital sitting idle.

The second form measures a specific outlay: an advertising campaign, a training course, a software package. Paul Farris, Dominique Hanssens, James Lenskold and David Reibstein define the ROI of marketing spending as the incremental financial value the spending generates, minus the spending itself, divided by the spending. The spending has to be subtracted because, as the authors note, marketing money is usually expensed in the period; it does not stay on the books as machinery or inventory the way the capital in the first form does. The word that matters is "incremental": the return has to be the part that would not exist without the spending. [3] The two forms share one frame, the return on top and the capital underneath, and they share the traps described under the limits below.

The nearest item in this series is earned value management, EVM, which is also a set of money ratios. The two tools ask different questions: EVM asks how far a project is spending to plan, ROI asks whether the money was worth spending, or how much it has brought back against what was put in. ROI can also be picked as a KPI, a key performance indicator, for a unit; the first example below shows what happens when that is done. This division of labour is this piece's reading.

Why We Need It

A company has ten requests for capital, the smallest a few tens of millions, the largest a few billion, from sales, the warehouse, IT, HR. Set side by side by absolute profit, the large requests usually win, just because they are large. Set side by side by description, each department talks about its own work in its own language, and whoever approves has no way to rank them. Without a common yardstick, the capital tends to go to whoever argues best.

ROI works by compression. It squeezes the return and the capital into a ratio with no units, so a 200 million outlay and a 2 billion outlay, an advertising campaign and a machine, can stand on the same yardstick. Farris and colleagues call this the advantage of a ratio over money measures such as net present value (NPV, the sum of future years' cash flows brought back to today's value, minus the capital put in): a money measure cannot tell whether a net profit gain of $500,000 came from an outlay of $200,000 or of $1 million. [3]

What Are Its Limits

The first built-in limit comes from the compression itself: a ratio has no scale. The 50% proposal in the opening example brings in 100 million a year, the 30% proposal 600 million. Suppose the two are mutually exclusive, since both target the packing stage, the company has the capital for either, and it uses 12% a year as its cost of capital, the minimum return it demands of a unit of capital. The cost of capital blends the price of borrowed money and of the owners' money, and should be higher for riskier investments; in the survey of chief financial officers by John Graham and Campbell Harvey described in the research section, nearly 60% of companies still used a single rate for every new project. [4] Ranked by ROI, the software wins. Take off the cost of capital and the software leaves 76 million a year, the packing line 360 million. Graham and Harvey raise the same problem with the internal rate of return, IRR, a ratio that allows for the time value of money: IRR is a ratio while net present value is an amount of money, and a manager intent on maximising IRR can reduce the value of the firm by turning down projects with a positive net present value. [4] Farris and colleagues put it more bluntly: management's job is to maximise profit, and that is not the same as maximising ROI; maximising ROI only makes sense when the budget is fixed. [3]

The second built-in limit is time. According to Farris and colleagues, the ROI of a unit or a business is almost always annual, but the ROI of a particular investment often does not say how long the return takes to arrive. [3] "50% ROI" on something that pays back after one year and on something that pays back after five years are very different investments, and the number is exactly the same.

The third limit sits in the numerator. The return in the ROI of an outlay is the increment over not spending, which makes it an estimate; the books do not record it. Farris and colleagues note that the sales that come from a marketing effort are usually easy to count, while the part of them that is truly incremental is much harder to measure precisely. [3] A return that cannot be measured is either left out of the formula or guessed, and the DuPont example below shows how the company that gave birth to ROI handled this for basic research.

A series of experiments at eBay shows how large the difficulty can be. Thomas Blake, Chris Nosko and Steven Tadelis switched off paid search advertising on keywords that did not contain the eBay name for 60 days in 68 US market areas, about 30% of eBay's US traffic, and compared them with areas where the ads kept running. Estimated by ordinary regression on pre-experiment data, which reads the link between advertising spend and sales as if advertising created all of it, short-term ROI came out above 4,000%, and at about 1,600% once day and area were controlled for. Measured from the experiment, ROI was minus 63%, with a 95% confidence interval from minus 124% to minus 3%. The reason, according to the authors: most of the advertising money went on frequent buyers, who came to eBay whether they saw an ad or not; new and infrequent users were pulled in by the ads. For keywords containing the eBay name, when the company stopped paying on Yahoo! and MSN, 99.5% of the lost clicks were made up by organic search results. ROI here is computed on eBay's revenue, worked out from public figures because the real ones are commercially confidential, and the result belongs to a brand most buyers already knew. [5]

The fourth limit belongs to accounting ROI for a whole business. Profit and capital on the books depend on how depreciation is done, so accounting ROI need not match the true economic rate of return on that capital. In 1983 Franklin Fisher and John McGowan built hypothetical firms to show this, and concluded that nothing can be inferred about relative economic profitability from accounting rates of return. The conclusion was contested the same year: William Long and David Ravenscraft, at the Bureau of Economics of the US Federal Trade Commission, argued that the examples contained errors and did not reflect central tendencies, and that there was considerable evidence that accounting profits, on average, still say a good deal about economic performance. The two also note that once the computational errors in Fisher and McGowan's examples are corrected, the spread between accounting rates is wider than the original showed. This piece cites both sides through Long and Ravenscraft's comment. [6] On this piece's reading, the two sides do not disagree that one firm's accounting ROI can drift away from its economic rate; they disagree about how large that drift remains on average across many firms.

The rest is a gap in how the tool is used, and it can be fixed. When a unit's ROI becomes the measure by which its manager is judged, the manager may turn down investments that are profitable for the company, because they drag down the unit's average.

Examples

Three examples. A restaurant chain, made up for illustration, where two branch managers are paid a bonus on ROI and both make the wrong call for the company, in opposite directions. Two experiments in which participants play the role of a manager, to see which way an ROI measure pulls investment decisions. Then DuPont itself, where ROI was born, deciding not to let a formula allocate money to basic research.

The 30% Branch That Did Not Want the 22% Machine

An illustrative scenario: a chain of six hotpot restaurants pays its branch managers a year-end bonus on the branch's ROI, the year's operating profit divided by the capital the company has put into the branch, taken at the start of the year for simplicity. The company's cost of capital is 12% a year. Branch A has 2 billion dong of capital and makes 600 million a year, an ROI of 30%. The technical team proposes a 300 million commercial dishwasher for A, replacing two dishwashers on the evening shift and saving 66 million a year after depreciation and utilities, which is 22%. A's manager does the sums: with the machine, the branch makes 666 million on 2.3 billion of capital, and ROI drops to about 29%. A's manager says no. At the same time branch B, with 2 billion of capital, makes 180 million, an ROI of 9%, and takes a 300 million conveyor hotpot unit that brings in 33 million a year, which is 11%; B's ROI edges up to about 9.3%, and B's manager is praised for improving the branch.

Put the two investments next to the 12% cost of capital and the verdict flips. A's machine, after 36 million of cost of capital, still brings the company 30 million a year; B's unit brings in 33 million but costs 36 million of capital, leaving the company 3 million a year worse off. Both managers compared the new investment with their own branch's average ROI, when the company's question was how it compared with the cost of capital. A strong branch is pushed by the measure towards investing too little, a weak one towards taking on investments that are not worth it. A yearly calculation is enough to see where the measure pushes the two managers; whether to buy a machine that lasts several years also needs the cash flow for each year and the machine's working life.

The test that comes out of the example: for each proposal turned down or approved at unit level, ask what it was compared against. If the answer is "the unit's current ROI", the measure is making the decision in the manager's place.

Two Experiments on People Judged by ROI

Markus Arnold, Robert Gillenkirch and Susanne Welker set up an experiment with two roles. Participants in the manager role make an investment decision in a period and are judged on one of three measures: profit, ROI, or residual income, the measure the authors regard as aligned with the owner's interest in investment decisions. Participants in the owner role predict in advance how the manager will decide. According to the abstract of the working paper presented at a 2006 seminar, managers judged on profit overinvest on average; managers judged on ROI underinvest on average; and under both measures most managers do not act in the owner's interest. [7]

The more striking part is on the owners' side. Before seeing how managers decide, they overestimate how well these measures get managers to act sensibly. Once they see the decisions of the first rounds, they revise their beliefs, but their predictions overall remain fairly inaccurate. [7] This piece read the experiment through the abstract, so it does not give the sample size or how large the shortfalls in investment were.

The second experiment measures a different direction. Jason Brown, Patrick Martin, Geoffrey Sprinkle and Dan Way had participants make capital investment decisions while judged on ROI or on residual income, and also measured each person's appetite for risk. According to the abstract of the 2023 paper, people judged on ROI chose riskier options than people judged on residual income, and the gap was concentrated among those who were more risk-averse to begin with. [8] The abstract does not say who the participants were or how many there were.

Both results come from the laboratory, with participants playing roles. They show which way the measure pulls decisions, in both the level of investment and the level of risk; how hard it pulls inside a real company, neither experiment measures.

DuPont Would Not Let a Formula Fund Its Laboratories

In a chapter of a volume published by the US National Research Council in 1998, Hounshell recounts how DuPont, the birthplace of ROI, wrestled with the question of the return on research. As early as 1910 and 1911, Charles Reese of the company's Eastern Laboratory reviewed four projects, compared their sales, profits and savings with the laboratory's costs over three years, and estimated that each dollar the high explosives department spent on research had brought back about three dollars. Around the same time, Irénée du Pont could not rigorously demonstrate any direct benefit from the Experimental Station, another DuPont research facility. [2]

From 1930, DuPont tied its research budget to sales. In the middle of the Depression, the executives worried that department heads, who were judged on their own departments' profit and loss statements, were cutting research too hard to keep the books looking good. In 1934 the Executive Committee, led by Walter Carpenter, changed the policy: money for research would depend on the merit of the research itself, not on a formula, and the policy held until the mid-1960s. Capital for assets, according to Hounshell, had to clear the company's 15% ROI hurdle. [2]

The question-and-answer session printed with the chapter adds two details; these are Hounshell's words in answer to questions, without source notes. Some DuPont executives built models using moving averages and smoothing functions to work out the returns on research; Charles Stine and Carpenter concluded that none of the models worked, because they could not capture all the possible kinds of benefit from basic research, and decided not to use any ROI method for basic research. And even for capital assets, management often approved investments whose projected ROI was only 8 or 10% rather than 15%; Hounshell calls the ROI calculations of the time "merely advisory", something to consult rather than a substitute for judgement. [2]

The case shows two uses of ROI living in one company: an advisory hurdle for spending on assets, where the return can be estimated in money, and a complete exclusion from basic research, where the return cannot yet be measured. The case does not say what the 1934 decision was worth to DuPont; the chapter gives no figure for its benefits.

Some Research on Return on Investment

Within the search done for this piece, no study was found comparing the results of organisations that do and do not use ROI to make decisions. The evidence on the tool's value comes from two other directions. One is how far practitioners trust it: in a survey of 194 senior marketers published by Farris and colleagues in 2010, 77% rated ROI a very useful measure, but fewer than half, 49%, said marketing ROI was useful for managing and monitoring their business. The authors, citing Ambler and Roberts, suggest that a large part of the reason is that respondents do not understand the measure well, and that they may also confuse marketing ROI with ROI in general. This piece cites the survey through the authors' own 2014 working paper. [3] The other is which techniques the people making investment decisions say they use, measured in the survey below. The evidence on behaviour when ROI becomes the measure people are judged by was given under Examples.

The study: John Graham and Campbell Harvey, of Duke University, mailed a three-page questionnaire to the chief financial officers of all 500 companies on the 1998 Fortune 500 list, the five hundred largest companies in the United States, and faxed it to 4,440 companies whose finance chiefs were members of Financial Executives International (FEI) in the United States and Canada. 392 questionnaires came back, a response rate of nearly 9%. The sample ran from very small firms, 26% with sales under $100 million, to very large ones, 42% with sales of $1 billion or more. For each project appraisal technique, respondents rated how often they used it from 0, never, to 4, always; results are reported as the share rating it 3 or 4. The list included net present value (NPV) and IRR, the two techniques that allow for the time value of money; the payback period, the number of years needed to recover the capital; and the accounting rate of return, which is ROI computed from profit and book values. The full version appeared in the Journal of Financial Economics in 2001; this piece read the full text of the authors' own shorter version from 2002. [4]

Findings: 75.7% always or almost always used IRR, a ratio, close to the 74.9% who used NPV, an amount of money. 56.7% used the payback period, and 38% used the earnings multiple, which the authors treat as a variant of payback. The accounting rate of return stood at only about 20%. Large companies used NPV significantly more than small ones; small companies used the payback period almost as often as NPV or IRR. [4]

What it shows: In this sample, when appraising a project, most chief financial officers say they rely on measures that allow for the time value of money, and only about one in five say they often use ROI computed from the books. That is what they said, not what was observed: the authors note themselves that survey results reflect the beliefs or opinions of the CFOs, and that they have no way of checking whether those beliefs match their actions. The response rate was just under 9%, although the authors note that the original paper checked and found the sample representative of the population of about 4,400 firms, without notable non-response bias. The survey went to the 1998 Fortune 500 plus companies in the United States and Canada whose finance chiefs were FEI members, so the results speak about North American businesses. Nor did the study measure whether projects appraised by NPV turned out better than projects appraised by ROI. [4]

The counter-case: The defenders of ROI have their strongest argument exactly where this piece has just criticised it. A ratio is easy for people outside finance to understand, can be worked out on a sheet of paper, and compares outlays of different sizes, the advantage over NPV that Farris and colleagues point to. Tim Ambler, cited by Farris's group, wrote that ROI is a useful way to choose between options when the total budget is fixed, and is seriously misleading only when used more broadly. [3] On this argument, the fact that only one CFO in five uses accounting ROI says nothing about the ROI of a campaign or a training course, where the budget is usually settled in advance and the only question is how to divide it. This piece accepts part of that. Within a fixed budget, ROI helps compare the options, but ranking each option by ROI and choosing from the top down does not necessarily produce the combination with the largest total profit. Take a budget of 100 million and three projects that cannot be split, with the same evaluation period and the same risk: project A needs 60 million and returns 36 million net, an ROI of 60%; projects B and C each need 50 million and return 25 million net, an ROI of 50%. Choosing by rank takes A first, the 40 million left is not enough for anything else, and the total profit is 36 million; taking B and C gives 50 million. The limit of scale comes back even inside a fixed budget. What has to be compared is the total profit of each combination that fits the budget, and that means looking at the size of each option, whether it can be split, whether the options exclude or depend on one another, and the ROI on the incremental spend when an option is scaled up; the options still have to have their returns counted the same way, over the same period. Ambler's argument also does not rescue ROI when it becomes the measure a unit is judged by: in the Arnold experiment under Examples, people judged on ROI underinvested on average. [7]

Why this is useful: If your company looks like the large firms in this sample, a proposal with a line reading "ROI 150%" will quite likely be recalculated by the finance department using NPV or IRR, that is, from year-by-year cash flows and a discount rate. Before submitting the next proposal, ask finance which measure they appraise with and what cost of capital they use, then attach a table of cash flows by year, so your ROI figure can be checked instead of taken on trust.

How to Apply It

ROI works by compressing the incremental return and the capital put in into a ratio with no units, letting outlays of different sizes and kinds be compared on the same yardstick, so the effective way to use it is to settle in advance how each side of the ratio is counted, measure the increment with a control group, and always set the ratio beside the two things it compressed away: the absolute amount of money and time. The tool fits best when comparing options within a fixed budget, over the same period and with the return counted the same way, and worst when the return cannot yet be measured in money.

  • Write down the numerator, the denominator, the period and the scope before calculating: Two people working out the ROI of the same campaign can arrive at very different numbers just because one counts revenue and the other contribution margin, or one counts a quarter and the other a year. Farris and colleagues recommend that every ROI figure come with a full statement: whether it is total, incremental or marginal ROI, for which spending, by which method, over which period [3]. Any return that cannot be measured in money goes alongside, written separately, not forced into the numerator; in 1934 DuPont stopped calculating ROI for basic research because no model could capture all of its benefits [2]
  • Measure the increment with a control group, not with all the sales that come with the spending: The sales from people who clicked an ad or attended a course include what would have happened without the spending. According to Farris and colleagues, knowing the increment takes data with and without the spending, through split tests or statistical models [3]. At eBay, switching from regression on existing data to an experiment split by area was enough on its own to take ROI from about 1,600% to minus 63% [5]. A group chosen at random not to receive the spending, measured over the same period on the same definitions, is the direct way to get that number
  • When deciding to spend more, calculate the ROI on the incremental spend alone and read it beside the absolute profit: Total ROI for the whole budget can still look good while the last unit of extra spending is losing money, because, as Farris and colleagues note, beyond some level the return on each extra unit of spending declines. In their numerical example, raising the budget from $400,000 to $600,000 lowers total ROI from 150% to about 133%, while profit after marketing costs rises by $200,000, meaning the incremental spend has an ROI of 100% [3]. The obvious move is to set a target of "the higher the ROI the better", or to raise the ROI hurdle to be safe; either can cut out profitable spending, because in that very example the first step of extra spending raises profit while total ROI falls. By the same logic, when dividing a fixed budget among projects that cannot be split, compare the total profit of each combination that fits the budget instead of picking in order of ROI rank, as in the three-project example in the counter-case
  • When judging a unit by ROI, compare each new investment with the company's cost of capital, not with the unit's current ROI: The manager of a unit with a high ROI has a reason to turn down investments that beat the cost of capital but fall short of the unit's own average, and in the Arnold experiment, people judged on ROI underinvested on average [7]. Put a money figure next to the ROI: the unit's profit minus the cost of capital on the capital it uses. Review the list of proposals turned down at unit level after the first one or two periods; in the same experiment, participants in the owner role overestimated how well the measure would lead to the right decisions, until they saw the actual decisions

Applying It to a Scenario

An online homeware retailer spends 60 million dong every four weeks on search engine advertising. The ad platform's report shows revenue "from ads" many times the spend, so the marketing team proposes raising the budget by 60%, to 96 million every four weeks, from the first quarter of 2027. What the company needs before the quarterly budget meeting: a decision on the level of spending based on the measured incremental profit, spending more only if the extra spending brings in more contribution margin than it costs. All figures are illustrative.

  1. Write down the definitions before calculating: The return is incremental contribution margin: the revenue gained over not advertising, times a contribution margin of 35%, what is left after cost of goods sold, delivery charges and the company's average rate of returned goods. The capital is the money paid to the ad platform. The period is four weeks; repeat purchases after that are not counted, and this is written directly under the figure. ROI is the return minus the ad spend, divided by the ad spend. The company also writes down how the platform counts "revenue from ads": every order from someone who clicked an ad in the seven days before buying, including people who had already meant to buy
  2. Split off a control group: The company's 60 delivery zones, each a few neighbouring wards and communes, are grouped into 20 matched triplets; the three zones in a triplet are close in revenue size, customer mix and revenue trend over the previous three four-week periods. Within each triplet the company draws lots, putting one zone in group A, one in group B and one in group C. Drawing within each triplet gives 20 independent assignments; if the 60 zones were split into three blocks and the roles drawn for each whole block, each spending level would get exactly one draw, and one lopsided block would skew the whole test. Added up, each group had revenue of around 500 million in the four weeks just before; the table rounds this to 500. In the four test weeks of September 2026, group A has its ads switched off, group B stays at its usual 20 million, and group C goes up 60%, to 32 million. Each group is compared with itself in the four weeks just before, to take out the seasonal change in revenue that every group goes through
  3. Calculate the ROI of current spending, and set it beside the platform's figure: The table just below gives the results. Compared with itself, group B is up 15 million and group A down 65 million; on this estimate, advertising at the current level brings in 15 + 65 = 80 million of incremental revenue, which is 28 million of contribution margin, on 20 million of spending: an ROI of 40%. Over the same four weeks, the platform credits group B with 180 million of revenue "from ads"; on that figure, ROI is (180 × 35% − 20) / 20, or 215%. The gap between 215% and 40% is revenue the platform credits to the ads that the test did not estimate the ads created: most likely mainly orders from people who would have bought anyway, plus the measurement and estimation error of the test itself
  4. Calculate the ROI on the incremental spend alone: Compared with itself, group C is up 30 million, group B up 15 million. Group C's 12 million of extra spending brings in 15 million of incremental revenue, which is 5.25 million of contribution margin: the ROI on the incremental spend is (5.25 − 12) / 12, about minus 56%. Group C's total ROI against group A is still positive, about 4%, but that figure hides the fact that the extra spending is losing money
  5. Convert to absolute money and decide: At the current level, advertising brings in 28 − 20 = 8 million of contribution margin after ad spend for each group of 20 zones, about 24 million every four weeks nationwide. If the whole country went up to 96 million and the extra spending performed as it did in group C, 36 million of extra spending would bring in 45 million of revenue, which is 15.75 million of contribution margin, and on this estimate the company would lose about 20 million every four weeks. The company keeps the budget at 60 million for the first quarter of 2027, and schedules a repeat of the test with a reduced-spending group, to find out whether the current level is already the best one or still too high

Nationwide equals three groups: 8 × 3 = 24; extra spending 12 × 3 = 36, revenue 15 × 3 = 45, contribution margin 15.75, difference 15.75 − 36 = −20.25.

Achieved: before the budget meeting, the company has a decision based on measured incremental profit. The measured figure is an estimate: according to the test, advertising at the current level is profitable, an ROI of about 40% over the four test weeks rather than the 215% in the platform's report, while the extra 60% gave an unfavourable result. The estimate comes from a single test and rests on three conditions. The three groups have to be truly comparable: drawing lots within 20 triplets helps, but the 15 million gap between groups C and B on a base of 500 million can still be mixed up with random variation, and the 20 triplets let the company see whether that gap repeats consistently or flips from one triplet to the next. The ads must not leak between groups: someone living in a group A zone who searches from another zone can still see the ads, and if those sightings bring in extra orders in group A, the true increment is larger than the measured one. And the return is counted over four weeks only: new customers who come back to buy again raise the long-term ROI above the figure in the table only if those repeat purchases are incremental too, and still profitable after the costs of serving and retaining the customers. The company does not wait for the differences to pass a threshold of statistical significance before deciding, because the two possible mistakes do not cost the same: if the estimate is right, spending more loses about 20 million every four weeks; if it is wrong and the extra spending is in fact profitable, holding the budget only forgoes that profit for one quarter, and the decision can be reversed as soon as the next test shows it. So the decision to hold the budget is a decision for one quarter, paired with a commitment to measure again; it does not yet say that 60 million is the right level for later quarters.

Summary

Key Point: ROI works by compressing the incremental return and the capital put in into a ratio, so every outlay, large or small, can stand on the same yardstick. The most counterintuitive point is that the compression itself spoils the decisions people most often hand to it: according to Farris and colleagues, maximising ROI is not the same as maximising profit. When ROI becomes the measure people are judged by, in the Arnold experiment, participants in the manager role underinvested on average, while participants in the owner role overestimated how well the measure would lead to the right decisions. The incremental return is also easy to overcount: in the eBay test by Blake and colleagues, ROI estimated from existing data was about 1,600%, while ROI measured by experiment was minus 63%. Principles: write down how the numerator, the denominator and the period are counted before calculating, measure the increment with a control group, calculate the ROI on the incremental spend alone next to the absolute profit, and compare a new investment with the cost of capital, not with the existing average ROI.
