SWOT Analysis

SWOT's four boxes accept anything anyone writes in them; the table only becomes analysis when every line has evidence, has a rank, and is put to use in a decision

Published: October 29, 2026

Definition

What Is SWOT Analysis

Suppose the owner of a veterinary clinic in a new residential area has to decide where to put the effort next year to do more surgery. The owner draws a cross on a sheet of paper. Top left, strengths: the only orthopaedic surgeon in the neighbourhood, a list of 900 clients who receive vaccination reminders. Next to it, weaknesses: no overnight kennels, one vet on duty in the evenings. The bottom row is for what lies outside the clinic, an apartment complex of 3,000 flats due to be handed over next year in the opportunities box and a large veterinary chain about to open 500 metres away in the threats box. SWOT analysis, short for Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats, is a way of sorting an organisation's situation into those four boxes. The line between the two rows is the boundary of the organisation itself: the top two boxes hold what belongs to it, such as its people, processes, assets and the contracts it holds; the bottom two hold conditions outside it, such as customers, competitors, partners and regulation. In either row, a line goes left or right depending on whether it helps or hinders the goal.

According to archival research by Richard Puyt, Finn Birger Lie and Celeste Wilderom, the tool was born under the name SOFT, in a 1965 report from the Long Range Planning Service of the Stanford Research Institute (SRI), written by Robert F. Stewart's research group. In that version, each manager wrote down the planning issues of his or her own unit and assigned each one, with evidence, to one of four categories: what is satisfactory and must be safeguarded (Satisfactory), an opportunity to be opened (Opportunity), a fault to be fixed (Fault), a threat to be thwarted (Threat). The label was later changed to SWOT; the authors do not record who changed it or when. They also checked the popular story that SWOT was born at Harvard Business School against the school's library archives: the 1963 business policy conference that the story points to had no session on SWOT, and the 1965 textbook Business Policy uses the separate words strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and risks but presents no such tool. [1]

In 1982, Heinz Weihrich added the step the four-box version tends to lack. He wrote that the factors themselves were not new; what was new was identifying the relationships between them systematically and basing strategies on them. He put the four lists on the two axes of a matrix, which he called TOWS, and each intersection became a type of strategy: strengths meeting opportunities (SO), weaknesses meeting opportunities (WO), strengths meeting threats (ST), weaknesses meeting threats (WT). [2] At the clinic, the orthopaedic surgeon paired with the new apartment complex becomes a round of free joint check-ups for the pets of newly arrived residents, so that the cases needing surgery are found here and operated on here. The lack of kennels paired with the chain about to open becomes a contract to board pets at a nearby pet hotel for surgical cases that need overnight observation, signed before the chain opens, so those cases do not have to go to the chain. This piece uses the common four-box version plus Weihrich's pairing step; the original SOFT version is described in the Examples section.

The two nearest entries in this series are PESTEL analysis and Porter's five forces. Weihrich suggests looking for opportunities and threats under economic, social, political and demographic factors, products and technology, and markets and competition [2], close to the way PESTEL divides the external environment. On this piece's reading, PESTEL and the five forces are ways of finding lines for the two bottom boxes; SWOT is where those lines are set beside the two top boxes and the question of what to do gets asked.

Why We Need It

Suppose a strategy meeting uses no frame at all. The head of sales talks about competitors, the head of engineering talks about old machinery, the finance director talks about interest rates, each talks about what is in his or her line of sight, and nobody has to talk about the weak spots of their own department. By the end, a competitor launching a new product, which the company cannot decide, sits in the same minutes as a pricing approval process that takes three days, which the company could fix next week.

SWOT's mechanism is sorting by two questions. Does this issue lie inside the organisation or outside it? Does it help or hinder the goal? The first question separates what belongs to the organisation from what belongs to the environment around it. That line does not coincide with the line of control: an internal weakness, such as a shortage of skilled people, can take a year to fix, while an external condition, such as the terms of a lease, can still be renegotiated. How much control there is comes in at a later step, when choosing how to respond to each line. The second question makes weaknesses and threats stand next to strengths and opportunities on the same page, rather than being scattered across what each person says. In the original SOFT version the two questions were also tied to time: what is satisfactory and what is at fault belong to present operations, opportunities and threats to the future; Puyt and colleagues write that by being written down and classified in this way, issues become openly discussable and then resolvable. [1] Sorting into boxes is only the first half. The second half is pairing and then choosing, the step Weihrich proposed [2], and when the second half is dropped the table is left as a list. What a table that leads to no choice looks like is on record: in the review described in the Research section, the SWOT tables that 14 consultancies drew up for more than 20 companies held more than 40 factors on average, were not prioritised, and nobody used their output in the later stages of the strategy process. [3]

What Are Its Limits

The first built-in limit: the default four-box template has no place for weight or evidence. "The only orthopaedic surgeon in the neighbourhood" and "cramped car park" each take one line, in the same type size. A factor that outweighs the other nine has no way of showing on the face of the table, unless whoever draws it adds a rank column or an evidence column. The original SOFT version required managers to classify issues with evidence [1]; the common four-box version does not ask for it.

The second limit: the label is a judgement by whoever draws the table, not a property of the thing itself. The chain about to open is a threat if it takes vaccination clients, but it may be an opportunity if it does not do orthopaedic surgery and refers those cases on. A strength or a weakness also only means something against a standard: the goal, what customers need, what the organisation has committed to, or the capability a plan requires. When the question is competitive advantage, that standard is the competition, and it is the hardest standard to obtain. Adam Koch, reading the SWOT tables his postgraduate students drew up for their own organisations, noted that to know how far ahead of or behind competitors you are, you need the performance levels of all your close competitors, and that many companies find that information too hard to collect, sometimes starting with deciding who the close competitors are. [4] Which way the label leans is not entirely random either. Susan Jackson and Jane Dutton ran two studies on how managers tell threats from opportunities, the second with an experimental design; according to the abstract of their 1988 paper, the results suggest a threat bias: managers are more sensitive to characteristics associated with threats than to those associated with opportunities. [5]

The third limit: the table is a snapshot of one moment. Weihrich proposed drawing several matrices at different points in time, one for the past, one for the present and, in his words, perhaps most important, several for points in the future. [2] A table the clinic draws before the chain opens goes out of date on the day the chain opens.

The rest are faults of use, and they can be fixed. A table can skip the pairing step, can put something the organisation plans to do into the opportunities box ("add an overnight boarding service" is an option the clinic chooses, so it belongs on the list of options), or can stop at the list without connecting to a plan. The fixes belong in How to Apply It.

Examples

Three examples. A constructed meeting in which the four boxes are filled without leading to any decision. The original SOFT version, as Stewart's group at SRI designed and taught it. Then the TOWS matrix Weihrich himself drew for Volkswagen in 1973-1975, to see what the pairing step looks like on a real company.

Forty-Seven Sticky Notes and a Plan Like Last Year's

An illustrative scenario: the start-of-year strategy meeting of a travel company with 60 staff. The facilitator sticks four large sheets on the wall, one letter each, and hands each of the twelve team leaders a pad of sticky notes. Forty minutes later the wall holds 47 notes. Strengths: "enthusiastic team", "long-standing reputation", "competitive prices". Weaknesses: "marketing not strong enough", "processes not professional enough". Opportunities: "tourism recovering", "launch Japan tours", "young customers want experiences". Threats: "fierce competition", "rising costs", "customers booking online themselves". Everyone nods, the secretary photographs the wall, and the photo goes into an appendix of the annual plan. The body of the plan sets a 15% revenue growth target and keeps the tour range unchanged.

Read note by note, almost none of them can answer "against what standard, on what evidence". "Competitive prices" in the strengths box and "fierce competition" in the threats box may be describing the same price war from two sides, and nobody notices. "Launch Japan tours" is something the company could choose to do; the external condition behind it, if there is one, is a demand figure that nobody brought into the room. No note is ranked above another, so all 47 weigh the same. And because there is no pairing step, no note has to answer "so what do we do". The plan looked like last year's, and the meeting had no fault anyone could easily point to: the four boxes had been filled exactly as the template asked.

A test drawn from the example: take your unit's most recent SWOT table, mark the lines that come with a number or a comparison, then mark the lines that reappear in the plan, even if only as the reason to keep an existing course. If no line has the second mark, the table was most likely a ritual, however carefully it was filled in.

A Hundred Managers, a Thousand Forms

The original version of SWOT was far heavier-handed than four boxes drawn on a whiteboard. According to Puyt and colleagues, the procedure was set out in report number 250 of SRI's Long Range Planning Service (Stewart, Benepe and Mitchell, 1965), and in a manual with forms and checklists, accompanied by a role-play case about a fictitious company called Quinta. Every manager in the company, not just the board, wrote down 8 to 10 planning issues of his or her own unit, classified each issue with evidence into one of the four categories, and answered four questions: what must be done to safeguard the satisfactory, to open the door to opportunities, to fix the cause of faults, to thwart or avert threats. A company with 100 managers therefore had about 1,000 forms. Once duplicate issues were merged, around 200 typically remained; staff planners or members of the planning committee examined how the issues related to one another and clustered them into roughly 35 to 50 families of issues. Small groups of managers discussed each family, with an instruction to take into account the needs and expectations of all stakeholders, and the groups' proposals became input for the executive planning committee to write the company's purpose and strategy. [1]

Between 1965 and 1971, Stewart's group ran more than 100 planning seminars for more than 1,100 executives and planning staff from client companies. Puyt and colleagues reconstructed the procedure from reports, manuals and correspondence in a private archive; they have no data on how far companies actually followed it, and they call for archival research on exactly that question. [1] So the example shows how the tool was designed to be used, not yet how it worked in practice.

Set beside the wall of sticky notes in the previous example, three things the common version has dropped stand out clearly: evidence for each issue, a step for merging and clustering before discussion, and a set path from a manager's form to the executives' decision.

Volkswagen in 1973-1975 in Weihrich's Matrix

To illustrate the TOWS matrix, Weihrich drew one for Volkswagen for the period from late 1973 to early 1975, mainly for the American market. Strengths: strong research and development and engineering, a strong sales and service network, efficient automated production. Weaknesses: heavy reliance on one product, rising costs in Germany (in 1973, wages rose 19% on the previous year), no experience with American unions if it built a plant in the United States. Opportunities: an increasingly affluent market wanting more luxurious cars with more options; attractive offers to build an assembly plant in the United States; Chrysler and American Motors needing small engines. Threats: the dollar falling against the Deutsche mark (from October 1972 to November 1973 the mark appreciated 35%), competition from Japanese and American carmakers, the fuel crisis. [2]

The part that teaches most is how the intersections are filled in. Each strategy carries the codes of the lines that produced it: a multi-model product line at several price levels is marked (O1 S1 S2); selling engines to Chrysler and American Motors is marked (O3 S3); improving fuel consumption with fuel injection and developing fuel-efficient diesel engines is marked (T3 S1). One and the same move, building an assembly plant in the United States, appears in three cells: in ST to reduce the effect of the exchange rate, in WO to cope with rising costs in Germany, together with hiring American managers experienced in dealing with unions, and in SO as a way of using engineering and production capability to take up the offers to build a plant. The WT cell even records options Volkswagen did not take: a joint operation with Chrysler or American Motors, or withdrawing from the American market. [2]

Weihrich drew this matrix after the fact, from strategies Volkswagen had already pursued, and chose the company because it had gone from difficulty in the early 1970s to a strong position by the end of that decade. The example shows how a matrix is filled in and paired; it does not show that Volkswagen used TOWS, or that the matrix helps anyone choose well.

Some Research on SWOT Analysis

Within the scope of this piece's search, no study was found that compares the results of organisations with and without SWOT analysis, nor any experiment that splits decision-makers into groups using and not using SWOT and then grades the quality of their decisions. A review by Marilyn Helms and Judy Nixon reaches a similar point. The two authors went through peer-reviewed articles carrying the keyword SWOT in the ABI/Inform database from June 1999 to June 2009, 141 articles according to the review's own table (another passage of the paper says 142). Most of the articles use SWOT to analyse an organisation, an industry or a country and then recommend action; the authors note that research still lacks quantifiable findings on the success of the tool itself. [6] The tool's influence is recorded more in words than in numbers: Puyt and colleagues call SWOT one of the oldest and most widely adopted strategy tools in the world, and quote Jay Barney in 1995 saying that the history of strategic management research can be understood as an attempt to fill in the blanks created by the SWOT framework. [1] The most direct evidence this piece found is a review of how the tool was actually used, in a UK government programme.

The study: Terry Hill and Roy Westbrook reviewed projects in the Manufacturing Planning and Implementation (MPI) scheme of the UK Department of Trade and Industry, which ran from 1991 to 1994 to help participating firms develop advanced manufacturing technology strategically; as a first step, firms had to review their markets with the help of consultants. Of the 50 companies the two authors reviewed, more than 20 used a SWOT, carried out by 14 consultancies as part of their approach to understanding a business from a corporate perspective. This piece reads the study through the abstract of the paper in Long Range Planning in 1997 [3], plus the abstract of a paper by the same authors in the same year on seven projects in the same scheme. [7]

Findings: All the applications showed the same characteristics: long lists, over 40 factors on average; general descriptions, in the authors' words often meaningless; no prioritisation; and no attempt to verify any of the points. What the authors found most worrying: nobody subsequently used the outputs in the later stages of the strategy process. [3] In the second paper, across seven projects, the market analyses, which included customer surveys, internal surveys, SWOT and broad reviews, mostly produced only general statements; the authors grouped the weaknesses into four areas: coverage, verification, segmentation and definition. [7]

What it shows: In one programme, in UK manufacturing firms in the early 1990s, SWOT tables drawn up by consultants were not used by anyone in the later stages of the strategy process. The study is a review with no comparison group: it does not compare these firms with firms that did no SWOT, and it says nothing about what a table with evidence, ranking and a pairing step would produce. From the abstract it is also not clear how the authors established that "nobody used the outputs". They conclude that the continued use of SWOT needs to be questioned [3]; that is their recommendation, broader than what the data of a single programme can support.

The counter-case: The defenders' strongest argument is that this sample can only measure how people use the tool. Koch read the SWOT tables his postgraduate students, mostly middle managers with about twelve years of experience on average, drew up for their own organisations in 1997 and 1998. He argues that most unsuccessful uses come from misconceptions about the nature of SWOT, poor input and inadequate skills, not from properties of the tool, and that a fair assessment would have to include cases of exemplary use as well as flawed ones. [4] Puyt and colleagues go further: SWOT stopped being used as its designer intended, and since we do not know how harmful that was, they argue it is not yet time for the "product recall" Hill and Westbrook called for; what is needed is to use the tool more properly. [1] This piece accepts the first part. Hill and Westbrook's data say nothing about the original SOFT version or about a carefully made SWOT table, so they cannot support the claim that "SWOT is useless". But the answer "the fault lies with the users" overlooks a detail in that very sample: 14 different consultancies fell into exactly the same failures. Part of that repetition may come from the shared setting, since every project sat in the same programme in the same period. Even so, when that many users fail in the same place, the simpler explanation is that the four-box template does not prevent the failure, which matches the first built-in limit in the Definition section: the template has no place for evidence or ranking. This is the piece's own reading; Hill and Westbrook's abstract does not separate the two explanations. And the defenders' side has no data behind it either: within the scope of this piece's search, no study was found showing that the careful version leads to better strategy, and Koch's account rests on teaching experience and reports no counts.

Why this is useful: Before your next planning session, give your SWOT table one rule: a line stays only if it comes with evidence, and only if it appears in at least one strategy at the pairing step or is explicitly marked as considered and dropped. After the meeting, count how many lines of the table survive into the plan; that number tells you whether the table was used, not whether it was right, since a wrong table can still pass into the plan in full. Whether it was right can only be checked later, by setting actual results beside the evidence for the lines that led to the decision.

How to Apply It

SWOT analysis works by sorting each issue into one of four boxes by two questions, inside the organisation or outside it, helping or hindering the goal, so that everyone looks at the same page, so the effective way to use it is to treat the sorting as only the first half: anchor the table to a decision, require evidence for every line, then rank and pair until every remaining line has a clear role in the decision: producing a task, keeping or ruling out an option, setting a condition on it, or naming something to monitor. The tool fits best at the start of a planning process, when the knowledge of many people about the same situation needs to be pooled; it fits worst when it is asked to stand in for the analysis, because the four boxes cannot check anything written in them.

  • Anchor the table to a decision and require evidence and a standard for every line: Something is a strength or a weakness only against a standard: the goal of the decision, what customers need, what has been committed, or the competition when the question is competitive advantage. "Enthusiastic team" cannot answer "against what standard, measured how"; "30% of peak-hour calls missed" can, without mentioning any competitor. The original SOFT version required managers to classify issues with evidence [1], and Koch notes that to know how far ahead of or behind competitors you are, you need the levels of your close competitors [4]. Mark any line without evidence as an assumption to be checked, rather than letting it stand level with lines that have been measured
  • For every external condition, write both its opportunity side and its threat side before sorting: The label is a judgement by whoever draws the table, and according to the abstract of Jackson and Dutton's research, managers are more sensitive to the characteristics of threats than to those of opportunities [5]. Writing both sides makes whoever draws the table say what tips the event one way; a big chain opening nearby may take customers away, and it may also bring more people to the whole area
  • Filter with the question "if this line were wrong, would the decision change", then rank the remaining lines by their effect on the goal: That question only filters: it shows which lines the decision is sensitive to, not which line weighs more, and the tables in Hill and Westbrook's review failed precisely by not prioritising [3]. After filtering, rank the lines by how far they push the goal up or pull it down, and mark which have been measured and which are still assumptions; Weihrich also warns that you cannot add up the plus signs in a pairing matrix to find the best pair, because each relationship carries a different weight [2]. The obvious move is to cap each box at three or five lines; the cap makes the table tidy, but whoever draws it may keep only the lines easiest to write. The filter has a failure point too: many small lines, each dropped on its own, may together change the decision, so before striking them out, ask the question once more for the dropped lines as a group
  • Pair lines into the decision, write the line codes alongside, and set in advance when the table will be redrawn: Weihrich writes next to each strategy the codes of the lines that produced it, such as (O1 S1 S2) [2]; that way any line with no role in any decision shows up, and has to be marked as considered and dropped. That role need not be a new task: a line can be the reason to keep an option, to rule out another, or to set a condition on it. Any task that does come out needs an owner and a deadline, so that the table does not end in an appendix. Because the table is a snapshot of one moment, also write down the event that will force it to be redrawn, such as a competitor opening or a contract expiring

Applying It to a Scenario

A children's swimming school rents time at two pools: the pool of an apartment complex and the pool of a district cultural house. In the third quarter of 2026, the basic course averaged 85 new pupils a month, against 120 in the same period of 2025. The directors have 300 million dong for 2027. What the school needs to do before December 15, 2026: decide how to spend that money, with every item traceable to specific lines of the SWOT table, along with measures and checkpoints for reviewing the chosen course after the first quarter of 2027. All figures are illustrative.

  1. Fix the question and gather what the people doing the work know: The question goes at the top of the page: "how to spend 300 million so that new pupils return to the level of the same period in 2025". Five people write forms: the two pool managers, the head coach, the person in charge of enrolment and the accountant. Each writes up to 8 issues in his or her own area, each with one line of evidence: a count, a document, or simply "no evidence yet". 37 forms in all
  2. Merge the forms and sort them with the two questions: Merging duplicates leaves 13 issues. Three people have each written "not enough pool time in the late afternoon" in the weaknesses box. Asking "is this inside the school or outside it" splits it in two. Since May 2026, the apartment complex's management board has cut the 6 pm to 7 pm slot to keep it for residents; the board is a party outside the school, so that decision goes in the threats box (T1), even though the school can still renegotiate with them. That the school only rents time at two pools, with no backup pool, is a weakness (W1). The form "start adult swimming classes" is moved out of the opportunities box onto the list of options, because the school chooses whether to do it; the external condition behind it, if there is one, is demand from adults around the two pools, and nobody has measured it
  3. Check the evidence for the lines about causes: Two forms give "fierce competition" as the reason pupil numbers fell. The accountant splits the figures by pool, for the third quarter of both years, together with the number of basic-course places offered each month (table below). Almost all of the fall is at the apartment pool, from 72 to 40 pupils, exactly where places offered fell from 78 to 42 because the slot was cut, while the fill rate there, new pupils divided by places offered, stayed above 90%. At the cultural house, places stayed at 60, pupils went from 48 to 45, and the fill rate from 80% to 75%. At the apartment pool, places were nearly full, so capacity is a significant constraint, and the lost places are the explanation the data available support most for the fall there. These data do not rule out demand falling at the same time, because when places are full, a drop in demand does not show up in pupil numbers. The small fall at the cultural house has not been explained, and competition from existing pools remains a possibility. The indoor pool chain the two forms mention is not due to open until March 2027, so its teaching cannot yet have caused the 2026 fall; whether it has already been advertising or taking advance registrations, nobody has checked. The "fierce competition" line is rewritten as a specific threat for next year: the chain opens 2 km from the cultural house, with published fees 15% lower and classes of 15 children (T2). The switchboard logs 30% of peak-hour calls missed because only one person is on the phones (W2). This line, together with the fill rate above 90% at the apartment pool, is a sign that demand is still there, not a measurement of it
  4. Write both sides of every external condition: The threat side of the new chain is price. But the school teaches 6 children per class, and of 80 forms parents filled in at enrolment in 2026, 46 chose "small classes" as the main reason (S1). The form only asked people who had enrolled, and only shows that they value small classes; it does not show how many would stay if somewhere else were 15% cheaper. The primary school next to the cultural house has written asking about swimming lessons during extracurricular hours for its 600 pupils (O1). Its threat side is that a large extracurricular class could push the number of children per coach above the level that makes S1 possible, so any proposal to the school must keep 6 children to a coach. The contract with the cultural house allows use of the pool from 10 am to 2 pm, a slot that currently has no classes (S2). A hotel 1 km from the apartment complex offers to rent 3 lanes of its pool in the 6 pm to 7 pm slot, exactly the slot just lost, for 18 million dong a month, on quarterly terms (O2)
  5. Filter, then rank by effect on the goal: For each of the 13 issues, the group asks "if this line were wrong, would the way we split 300 million change". Eight lines pass. The other five, such as "cramped motorbike parking at the cultural house", are asked once more as a group: if all five were wrong together, would anything change. Nothing would, so they are written at the bottom of the page marked "considered, does not change the decision". The eight lines that pass are ranked by their estimated effect on the goal, with two separate notes: whether the line has been measured or is still an assumption, and whether a deadline forces it to be dealt with early. At the top are T1 together with W1, the slot cut with no backup pool, which go with a measured fall of 32 pupils a month. Next comes O2, the most direct way to recover up to 36 lost places, provided parents are willing to go to the hotel pool. Third is T2, the new chain: its effect could be large but is still an assumption, and it has a deadline in March, so the first thing to do about it is check early. Then W2, the missed calls, counted but with no idea yet how many would have become pupils; and last S1, S2 and O1, the basis for a way of adding pupils with no figure yet on demand
  6. Pair lines into options, with the line codes: WO (O2 W1 T1): rent 3 lanes at the hotel pool in the 6 pm to 7 pm slot, 18 million a month, 216 million for 12 months, restoring exactly the 36 basic-course places a month that were lost (calculation in the note under the table), and at the same time propose a fixed 12-month slot contract to the apartment complex's board. The number of extra pupils is still an assumption: if the new pool fills like the apartment pool, about 33, but the hotel pool may be less convenient for parents, and some of the pupils there may be moving across from the two existing pools. WT (W2 T2): 60 million for an online registration page and a part-time phone operator at peak hours, running before March, so that parents who cannot get through do not go to the new chain; the person in charge of enrolment owns it. SO (S1 S2 O1): 24 million to design and trial a lunchtime extracurricular class at the cultural house for the primary school, 6 children per coach; the head coach owns it. ST (S1 T2): keep fees unchanged in the first quarter as a positioning test, state "6 children per class" on every leaflet and on the registration page, and track three figures: the share of calls that become enrolments, the reasons given by people who enquire but do not enrol, and the share of pupils who go on to the next course. The option of cutting fees by 15% is postponed, for two reasons: the chain has not opened, and the largest part of the 2026 fall goes with the places that were cut and has not been seen to be linked to price. Not every line produces a new task: S1 is the reason to keep fees unchanged, and T2 sets something to monitor from March
  7. Check the conditions before signing the lease: The contract is signed before December 31 only if three conditions are met. Money: the 300 million is the development budget, paying for the pool rental, the registration page and the trial class; the coaches' pay at the hotel pool is an operating cost, paid out of fees like every other class, and is therefore counted in the break-even calculation in the next step. Coaches: each evening 3 classes run side by side in 3 lanes, so 3 coaches must be free in the 6 pm to 7 pm slot six days a week; the head coach confirms that the people who used to teach the slot cut at the apartment pool can still take it. Measurement: the accountant fixes in advance how extra pupils will be counted, so that the figure at the end of the first quarter does not have to be argued over again. The apartment pool manager owns the signing
  8. Set the checkpoint with two separate measures: The recovery measure is new basic-course pupils across the whole school, against the same period a year earlier. The financial measure is extra pupils at the hotel pool: fee-paying pupils there, minus those who moved across from the cultural house while it still had places; a pupil moving from the full apartment pool still counts, if the place he or she leaves is filled by someone else. According to the break-even calculation in the note under the table, 20 extra pupils a month are needed. At the end of March 2027, if the average for February and March is below that level, the next quarter is not signed; a trial quarter can lose at most 86.4 million, a level the directors accept. Break-even at the hotel pool, counting only the costs added compared with not renting: coaches' pay 6 classes × 12 sessions × 150,000 dong = 10.8 million (operating cost, paid from fees); plus rent of 18 million (paid from the development budget) makes 28.8 million a month; the basic-course fee is 1.5 million, so 28.8 / 1.5 = 19.2, rounded up to 20 extra pupils; the most a quarter can lose is 3 × 28.8 = 86.4 million. Budget split: 216 + 60 + 24 = 300 million.

Achieved: before December 15, the school has a way of splitting 300 million, each item carrying the codes of the lines that produced it, a list of five lines considered and dropped, one option postponed with its reasons, three conditions to be met before signing the contract, and two separate measures, new pupils across the whole school and extra pupils at the hotel pool, with a checkpoint at the end of March 2027. The split rests on four conditions. Demand around the apartment pool has to still be there: a fill rate above 90% and the missed calls suggest it is, but the 36 places at the hotel pool are the real test, and quarterly terms limit any mistake to one quarter's loss. Keeping fees unchanged is also only a test: the parent forms do not show how many would stay when the new chain is 15% cheaper, and only the three figures tracked from March can answer that. The small fall at the cultural house, from 80% to 75%, has not been explained; it may just be one quarter's fluctuation, and it is the first line to watch when the new chain opens. And the SWOT table only shows which course is worth taking, not whether that course will pay: the threshold of 20 pupils comes from the accountant's break-even calculation, which sits outside the table, and that calculation so far covers only the rent and the coaches' pay for the new slot.

Summary

Key Point: SWOT analysis works by sorting each issue into four boxes by two questions, inside the organisation or outside it, helping or hindering the goal, so that everyone looks at the same page. The most counter-intuitive point is that a filled table says nothing about the quality of the analysis, because the four boxes accept anything: in Hill and Westbrook's review, the tables 14 consultancies drew up for more than 20 companies held more than 40 factors on average, were not prioritised, were not verified, and were not used by anyone in the later stages. The original SOFT version that Puyt and colleagues reconstructed had evidence for each issue and a set path to a decision; the common version has dropped both. The principle: anchor the table to a decision, require evidence and a standard for every line, write both sides of every external condition, filter by whether a line would change the decision and then rank by effect on the goal, then pair lines into the decision, and give any task that comes out an owner and a deadline.
