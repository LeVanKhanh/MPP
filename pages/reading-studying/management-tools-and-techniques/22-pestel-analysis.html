PESTEL Analysis

PESTEL lowers the risk of missing a whole area of the outside environment; it only becomes analysis when each factor is tied to a decision and ranked by importance and certainty

Published: November 1, 2026

Definition

What Is PESTEL Analysis

Suppose the director of a soft drinks company in Da Nang has to decide before the end of the year whether to invest in a second bottling line in 2027. The planning team draws six columns on a whiteboard, one for each group of conditions outside the company. Political: the city is stepping up its summer tourism promotion. Economic: medium-term borrowing is sitting at about 9% a year, according to quotes from two banks. Social: customers are gradually moving to low-sugar drinks, and the company's own low-sugar range has grown for three quarters in a row. Technological: the latest filling machines use 20% less power. Environmental: the dry season is getting longer, groundwater in the area is falling, and the plant's borehole is where it shows first. Legal: talk of a tax on sugary drinks, level and timing unknown. PESTEL analysis, short for Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal, is a way of scanning an organisation's outside environment under those six headings. The Chartered Institute of Personnel and Development (CIPD) describes it as a broad fact-finding exercise about the external factors that could affect an organisation. [1] The same six letters also appear as PESTLE, and the Futures Toolkit of the UK Government Office for Science (GO-Science) treats PESTLE, STEEP and STEEPV as versions of the same approach; STEEPV drops legal and adds values. [2]

The name is not tied to any original text this piece was able to read. The approach grew out of research on environmental scanning, and later studies usually take their definition from Francis J. Aguilar's Scanning the Business Environment, Harvard Business School, 1967: scanning is acquiring information about events and relationships in a company's outside environment, knowledge that helps top management chart the company's future course. [3] Many guides add that Aguilar split the environment into four groups, economic, technical, political and social, abbreviated ETPS, and that later writers reordered the letters into PEST and added legal and environmental. This piece has not been able to read Aguilar's book, and within the scope of its search found no primary source confirming that detail, so it records it only as a popular account.

At the soft drinks company, the six columns are full within half a meeting. That is where the hard part starts: putting each factor to two questions. How much does it matter to the decision about the bottling line? How certain is its outcome? Borrowing costs and the shift to low-sugar drinks are both important and fairly certain, so they go straight into the plan. The tax and the groundwater matter but are uncertain, so they become two questions that need scenarios: is the new line still worth building if the tax comes early, or if the borehole cannot supply two lines in the peak month? The GO-Science toolkit calls this two-axis sorting step driver mapping, where drivers are the external forces for change that have been gathered, each written as a short, meaningful phrase. [2] This piece uses the usual six headings for gathering, plus that sorting step.

The two closest entries in this series are SWOT analysis and Porter's five forces. PESTEL looks only outside the organisation, and only at the macro level, the conditions shared by a whole industry. The company's particular customers, competitors and suppliers have no column of their own; they are the business of the five forces. When Heinz Weihrich suggested how to find opportunities and threats for the TOWS matrix, his list of groups included markets and competition alongside economic, social, political, demographic, and products and technology. [4] On this piece's reading, PESTEL and the five forces both supply lines for the opportunities and threats boxes of a SWOT table; PESTEL's output can also take a different route, into scenarios for factors that matter but are uncertain.

Why We Need It

Without the six columns, that board meeting tends to run another way. The sales director talks about competitors and prices, the finance director talks about interest rates, the plant manager talks about machines. Each looks at the part of the environment their daily work makes them look at. The borehole's water level belongs to nobody's part, so nobody raises it, until the next dry season, when the new line is installed and the plant is buying water by the tanker.

The mechanism of PESTEL is a fixed checklist. The six headings make the people scanning walk through areas outside their usual line of sight, and an empty column becomes a question visible on the face of the table: is there really nothing here, or has nobody looked? The GO-Science toolkit uses the checklist in exactly this way when it advises people doing scanning to count how many items fall under each PESTLE heading, so as not to pile too much into one area, technology for example. [2] Research on environmental scanning offers a clue as to why breadth is worth the effort. In a study that interviewed the chief executives of 50 manufacturing companies, the heads of high-performing companies scanned more often and more broadly when facing strategic uncertainty than the heads of low-performing ones. [5] That is an observed association, not evidence that using PESTEL improves a company; the Research section sets out how far it goes.

A checklist only takes care of breadth, and only as a prompt: it walks people through six headings, but does not guarantee they see anything there, because that depends on the sources of information and the people in the room. Nor does it say which factor weighs most, so a PESTEL table that stops at six columns has only gone halfway.

What Are Its Limits

The default six-column template has no place for weights or uncertainty, and that is the first built-in limit. "The new filling machine uses less power" and "a tax on sugary drinks" take one line each, in the same size type. Order appears only if whoever draws up the table adds a sorting step; the CIPD builds a step of ranking items by potential risk into its process [1], while the six-column template does not ask for one.

The second limit: the boundaries between headings are a convention, not a property of events. A tax on sugary drinks is a political decision, a legal text, a price change, and a sign of social concern about health. Whoever draws up the table may write it in four columns and then unwittingly count it as four factors, or spend time arguing which column it belongs in when the question worth asking is how it will bite. The GO-Science toolkit merges law and regulation into one heading and accepts several ways of grouping [2]; on this piece's reading, that suggests the headings exist to make the scan complete rather than to classify correctly.

The third limit: the scope is macro. Specific customers and competitors sit outside the six headings, and in at least one study that is exactly where managers saw the most uncertainty. In the study by Richard Daft, Juhani Sormunen and Don Parks, the three sectors that generated the most strategic uncertainty for chief executives were customers, the economy and competitors, more than technology, regulation and sociocultural factors. [5] Two of those three sectors have no column in PESTEL. That is uncertainty as managers perceived it, in 50 manufacturing companies, not a measure of which sector has the stronger effect on results.

The fourth limit: the table is a snapshot. The CIPD notes that the analysis has to be repeated regularly to be effective, that the pace of change makes future developments increasingly hard to anticipate, and that data may rest on assumptions that later prove unfounded. [1]

The remaining failures lie in how the tool is used, and they can be fixed. A table may list general trends with no direction, size or date ("digital transformation"), may fill all six columns evenly for balance, or may stop at a list without connecting to any decision. The fixes belong in How to Apply It.

Thirty-Two Lines That Fit Every University

An illustrative scenario: a private university with 8,000 students hires a consultancy to write its five-year strategic plan. The fourth slide is a PESTEL table, 32 lines spread evenly across six columns, five or six lines each. Political: "education policy is changing". Economic: "a difficult economy, household incomes under pressure". Social: "young people prefer to study online". Technological: "artificial intelligence and digital transformation". Environmental: "climate change". Legal: "the legal framework for university autonomy is being completed". The university council approves the slide in ten minutes. The main body of the plan opens three new programmes and builds a 600-bed hall of residence.

Not one line is wrong, and that is exactly where it fails. Cover the university's name and the slide still holds for almost any university in the country, so it describes the times, not this university's situation. No line has a direction, a size or a date: "young people prefer to study online" means how many people, in which subjects, still rising or levelling off? No line is linked to the three new programmes or the hall of residence. The question that decides the hall, how many first-year students come from other provinces and where they live now, has no column to sit in, because it is about the university's own students, outside the six headings. And because the 32 lines are spread evenly, the technology column looks as heavy as the environment column, though for the decision to build a hall one column might deserve the whole meeting and the other not a sentence.

Try it on your own unit: take its most recent PESTEL table, cover the organisation's name, and ask whether the table could belong to another unit in the same industry. Then mark the lines that carry a number, a direction of change or a date, and the lines that reappear in the decision section. A line with no mark is a line describing the times.

From Six Headings to Two Axes: PESTLE in the UK Government's Toolkit

GO-Science's Futures Toolkit, 2024 edition, is written for policymakers and analysts in the UK government. PESTLE appears in two places. In horizon scanning, the systematic gathering of signals of change, it is the frame for sorting the items found, with advice to count how many items fall under each heading so as not to pile into one area. [2]

In driver mapping, PESTLE opens a workshop of six or more people lasting about two hours. The group lists the drivers of change already gathered, each as a short, meaningful phrase: "Sustained economic growth in China" is clearer than "The Economy". Then each driver is placed on two axes, important to the policy area or not, outcome certain or uncertain. The four boxes have four jobs: important and certain means prioritise and act; important and uncertain are the critical uncertainties, passed on to scenario building; uncertain but less important means track, because it may grow in weight later; certain but less important can be parked. The group clusters related drivers as it places them, and picks out the three to five most important. The toolkit warns that participants may not have been exposed to a wide enough range of drivers, and suggests bringing in outside experts or starting from a pre-researched list. [2]

The toolkit cites GO-Science's Net Zero Society project as its case study for this step. The project asked how society in the UK might change by 2050. [2] According to the case page, 35 people from government, business and academia took part in one driver-mapping workshop and then three scenario workshops, in February and March 2022. Each driver was scored from −4 to +4 on both importance and uncertainty. Two critical uncertainties were chosen: social cohesion together with trust in institutions, and economic growth together with technological progress. Those two became the axes of a matrix that produced four scenarios for society. [6] The case page does not say which headings the initial drivers were grouped under.

The difference from the university's 32-line slide lies in the output: in the toolkit, what moves on to the next step is three to five ranked drivers, or, as in the Net Zero project, two axes.

Some Research on PESTEL Analysis

PESTEL is used far more widely than the evidence about the tool itself would suggest. A review by Albana Berisha Qehaja, Enver Kutllovci and Justina Shiroka Pula of empirical studies on strategic management tools from 1990 to 2015 ranks PEST third among the ten most used tools, after SWOT, the most used tool in 22 of 25 studies, and benchmarking. In the review's own summary table, PEST appears in the list of most used tools in 6 studies, in the UK and New Zealand, Australia, Jordan, the Czech Republic and Turkey. Those studies used different questionnaires listing different tools [7], so the ranking says PEST is mentioned often, not how many firms use it. As for effectiveness, within the scope of this piece's search, no study was found comparing the results of organisations with and without PESTEL analysis, nor any experiment splitting decision-makers into groups that do and do not use it. The nearest evidence sits one level up, in research on environmental scanning. Chun Wei Choo summarises a series of studies linking scanning to business performance; Newgren and colleagues, for instance, compared 28 US corporations that practised environmental scanning with 22 that did not, over 1975-1980, using the price/earnings ratio normalised by industry, and found that the scanning group outperformed. That too is an observational comparison, and this piece has read it only through Choo's summary. [8] The study below was chosen because it splits the environment into sectors close to the PESTEL headings, and measures scanning sector by sector.

The study: Daft, Sormunen and Parks interviewed the chief executives of 50 manufacturing companies about the strategic uncertainty they perceived in six sectors of the environment, and how often and by what means they scanned each sector. The six sectors were customers, competitors, technology, regulation, the economy, and sociocultural factors. [5] On this piece's reading, the last four are close to the PESTEL headings, while customers and competitors belong to the industry environment. Business performance was measured by return on assets, according to Choo's summary. [8] This piece has read the study through the abstract of the article published in the Strategic Management Journal in 1988, plus that summary.

Findings: Customers, the economy and competitors generated more strategic uncertainty than technology, regulation and sociocultural factors. When uncertainty in a sector was high, chief executives scanned it more often and relied more on personal sources of information. Chief executives of high-performing companies scanned more frequently and more broadly in response to strategic uncertainty than those of low-performing companies. [5] Choo restates the last finding as the high performers increasing the frequency, intensity and breadth of their scanning as external uncertainty rose. [8]

What it shows: In that sample of 50 manufacturing companies, scanning more broadly and more often under uncertainty went together with better business performance. It is an observational study: it does not say whether broad scanning improves results, or whether companies doing well have more resources and time to scan broadly. It measures the scanning behaviour of chief executives, not the use of PESTEL or of any template. Nor does the abstract say how large the gap between the two groups was.

The counter-case: The defenders' strongest argument lies in this very data: breadth is precisely what PESTEL delivers. A fixed checklist turns broad scanning from the habit of a few good managers into something anyone can follow, and the high performers in Daft's study differed from the others in exactly that respect. This piece accepts that half. But the same data shows two places where PESTEL done by the template does not match. First, the high performers' breadth followed uncertainty: they scanned more, and more broadly, as uncertainty rose, and the data does not show them scanning every sector evenly all the time; a six-column table filled evenly is a different kind of breadth. Second, the most uncertain sectors in the sample, customers and competitors, lie outside the six headings. The defenders could reply that PESTEL was never meant to replace the five forces, and that reply is right; which is exactly why the value of a PESTEL table depends on whether it is paired with a scan of the industry environment. This is this piece's reading; Daft and his colleagues do not discuss PESTEL.

Why this is useful: It changes the question put to each column, from "what else can we write here" to "what are we unsure about here". Next time you scan, write one sentence next to each heading: for the decision at hand, what are we unsure about under this heading? Then divide the scanning time by three questions about that uncertainty: could it change the choice, how thin is the evidence we have, and how long until we must respond. A heavy uncertainty can fit in a few words, so do not measure it by the length of the sentence. A heading that yields no sentence at all needs only a quick check that there really is nothing there. And add one line for customers and one for competitors, even if the table is called PESTEL.

How to Apply It

PESTEL analysis works by making the people scanning walk through six fixed headings of the macro environment, so that areas outside the group's usual line of sight are less likely to be missed, so the effective way to use it is to keep the breadth in gathering and put the depth into choosing: anchor it to a decision, write each factor as a driver with a direction and a date, sort by importance and certainty rather than by column, then send each box to a specific job. PESTEL earns its keep when a decision that binds the organisation for several years depends on conditions the organisation does not control, and wastes the effort when it is done as a compulsory slide at the front of a plan, because six columns accept every trend without asking what the trend has to do with anything.

  • Anchor to a decision and a time frame before opening the six columns: The CIPD puts defining the scope at the start of its process [1], and the GO-Science toolkit ranks drivers by their importance to a specific policy area [2]; without a decision there is no standard for calling a factor important. The time frame should match how long the decision will bind the organisation, such as the life of a machine or the term of a contract. A factor arriving after that point is still worth recording if it reaches back inside the frame, for instance a rule announced today for ten years from now that already drags down the resale value of an asset at the end of the frame
  • Write each factor as a driver with a direction, a size or a date, and record it once even if it touches several headings: The GO-Science toolkit gives the example that "Sustained economic growth in China" is clearer than "The Economy" [2]. An event that touches several headings, such as a new tax, goes in as one line tagged with several letters, so it is not counted as several factors. Each line carries a source, or says plainly "no evidence yet", because the CIPD warns that data may rest on assumptions that later prove unfounded [1]. Keep the list at the macro level: a quote, a test run or a landlord's consent is data or a condition for one option, not a driver. Record those in a separate table, linked to the driver they quantify
  • Sort by importance and certainty, and use counts per column only to find blind spots: The obvious move is to fill all six columns evenly for balance; the table looks complete but mixes heavy factors with light ones, and whoever draws it up is tempted to add lines just so a column is not empty. Counting per column is useful for exactly one thing: an empty column or a crowded one is a question to check, as in GO-Science's advice to count items per heading. Then place each driver on two axes, important to the decision and outcome certain or not, and pick the three to five heaviest [2]. The second axis asks how certain the course of events is over the whole time frame, not how reliable today's measured figure is: a signed quote can still be out of date two years on. Record the reliability of current evidence and the deadline for responding as two separate notes, not folded into the position on the axis. Because the six headings leave out customers and competitors, the sectors Daft and his colleagues found generated the most uncertainty [5], add a scan of the industry environment before sorting
  • Send each box to a job, and write down in advance the signals that force a redo: Drivers that are important and certain go straight into the options, or into the opportunities and threats boxes of a SWOT table; drivers that are important but uncertain are where scenarios are built; drivers that are uncertain but less important are monitored [2]. Before demoting an uncertain driver to monitoring, test the choice at both ends of it, then test a plausible adverse combination as well, because several bad variables can arrive together, and a variable's effect does not always move in one direction; the GO-Science toolkit has a separate tool for testing options across several scenarios [2]. Demote it only if the choice survives both tests. If the choice changes, a shorter commitment helps only as far as the contract itself allows you to pull out or change course when the driver turns bad. For each uncertain driver, write down the signal that will show which way it has gone. The CIPD notes that the analysis is effective only when repeated regularly [1], and the signals tell you when to repeat it ahead of schedule

Applying It to a Scenario

A haulage company in Ho Chi Minh City runs 24 light trucks delivering for retailers. Eight trucks reach the end of their depreciation in 2027, each running a fixed route, A to H. What the company needs to do before December 15, 2026: decide what to replace each truck with, buy or lease, so that each choice traces back to specific drivers, together with the signals that would force the choice to be revisited. All figures are illustrative. Four jobs in the scenario connect but cannot stand in for one another: PESTEL gathers the macro drivers, driver mapping sorts them, operating data and the accountant's calculations appraise each option, and scenario testing checks whether an option still stands when the drivers turn bad.

  1. Settle the question, the time frame and the participants: The question at the top of the page: "what to replace the eight trucks due in 2027 with, over the seven-year life of the new trucks, that is, to the end of 2033". Six participants: the director, the fleet manager, the accountant, the head of sales and two driver team leaders. Each writes up to six external drivers, each as one sentence with a direction and a date, with a source or the note "no evidence yet". 31 cards in total
  2. Merge the cards, separate drivers from data: Merging duplicates leaves 19 lines. Three people wrote about the city consulting on a roadmap to restrict diesel trucks from the central districts; one put it in the political column, two in the legal column. It becomes one line, P1, tagged P and L, with the note "no start date yet". Three lines are taken off the driver list because they are data or conditions for a specific option: the results of a test run of an electric truck (D1), the dealer's quote (D2), and the warehouse landlord's letter about a charging station (D3). They move to an operating data table, to be linked to the drivers they quantify. The line "the two largest customers, 40% of revenue, require per-trip emissions reports from 2027" does not belong under any of the six headings either: it is about customers, part of the industry environment. It goes onto a separate list, K1, for use when assigning routes. That leaves 15 drivers
  3. Count per column to find blind spots: The technology column has six drivers, mostly about batteries and charging; the environmental column has none. The group does not write more just to fill the column, but asks the two driver team leaders: what weather or road conditions have disrupted routes? The answer adds one driver, En1, seasonal flooding in the inner city, and one line of data, D4: according to the 2025 dispatch log, routes B and D flood to 30-40 cm a few times each rainy season, while the electric truck under consideration can only wade through 30 cm. The list now has 16 drivers
  4. Sort by importance and certainty: Each person scores every driver from −4 to +4 on two axes. On the first, +4 means most important to the truck replacement decision; on the second, +4 means most certain about how things will unfold over the whole seven years; how precise today's measured figure is is a separate matter, recorded in the data table. The group places drivers by their average score; any line averaging between −1 and +1 on either axis, or with two scorers 4 or more points apart, is discussed again before it is placed. Five drivers go into the act box and two into the scenario box, along with the data linked to each (first table below). Four go into the monitor box, such as diesel prices and manufacturers announcing longer-range electric trucks. The remaining five are parked
  5. Appraise the options with operating data: With a 20% safety margin on the range measured in D1, an electric truck can run routes of up to 144 km a day; only the four short routes A, B, C and D fit within it, even when B and D have to take a 15 km detour on flood days (second table). The accountant works out those four routes with today's data: 840 million dong extra up front, savings of about 308 million a year, payback in about 2.7 years. Quote D2 includes an eight-year battery warranty, longer than the seven-year life, so the calculation leaves out battery replacement
  6. Test the options as the drivers turn bad: For E3, at the worst end, the electric trucks resell for nothing while the diesel trucks fetch 150 million each; the seven-year gain is still about 296 million when discounted at 12% a year. But low resale values can come together with other bad news, so the accountant tests an adverse combination as well: zero resale, electricity per km up 30%, and maintenance savings halved. The gain falls to about 21 million, with payback in about 3.4 years. The choice still stands, so E3 is demoted to monitoring, but the margin is now thin: a worse combination, say a steep fall in diesel prices at the same time, could reverse the result. For P1, routes E and F enter the central districts and are longer than 144 km, so the electric truck tested cannot run them yet. If the roadmap takes effect within the next seven years, diesel trucks bought for those two routes will be restricted with years of depreciation left; even if it is announced for a date after 2033, the resale value of diesel trucks in 2033 may already have fallen. The choice changes with the outcome, so P1 remains a critical uncertainty
  7. Combine into choices, tagged with driver and data codes: (T1 E1 E2 L1 En1, D1 D2 D3 D4, K1) Buy four electric trucks for A, B, C and D, the four routes that deliver for the two customers in K1, install the charging station at the warehouse after fire safety acceptance, and plan the detours for B and D on flood days; the fleet manager is responsible. (P1 T1, D1) Lease two diesel trucks for three years for E and F instead of buying, with two clauses the lease must contain: early termination if a roadmap restricting diesel trucks is issued for the truck's route, at a fee of no more than two months' rent, and the right to switch to an electric truck of the same payload when the lessor has a model with enough range. According to the quotes, leasing for three years costs about 40 million more per truck than buying and reselling after three years, with the resale value being the dealer's estimate. The 80 million for the two trucks buys exactly the flexibility those two clauses allow; if the lessor accepts neither, it only shortens the commitment from seven years to three, and the head of sales has to draw up a fallback that moves E and F's delivery times or routes out of the restricted zone. The director signs off on this sum. (P1 T1, D1) Buy two diesel trucks for G and H, two routes to industrial parks along the ring road, outside the restricted zone in the consultation draft
  8. Write down signals and review dates: If the city issues a roadmap with a start date, the group meets within a month about E and F, and about G and H if the restricted zone extends to the ring road. If an electric truck reaches at least 207 km in a loaded test run, the range route F needs with a 20% margin, it becomes a candidate for E and F, through the switch clause or when the leases end in late 2029. The accountant tracks the three figures in the adverse combination: asking prices for used electric trucks, commercial electricity prices, and the actual maintenance cost of the four electric trucks in their first year. The fleet manager measures the four electric trucks' range again in April 2027, in the middle of the hot season, when cab air conditioning runs hardest. The PESTEL table is redone every fourth quarter, and before the leases end

Achieved: before December 15, the company has a choice for each of the eight trucks, each carrying the codes of the drivers and data behind it, plus a list of signals and review dates. Sixteen drivers sit in four boxes, kept apart from four lines of operating data; the two critical uncertainties were tested at both ends, E3 passed an adverse combination as well before being demoted to monitoring, and P1 was handled with a short lease that has exit clauses. The choice rests on four conditions. The 180 km range was measured over only two weeks in October, not yet through the hot season; if the April 2027 measurement comes in lower, the 144 km margin shrinks with it, and route D, the longest of the four, is the first to be reviewed. The flood depth comes from one year of logs. The accountant's calculation leaves out the detour kilometres, rests on an assumption about the resale value of diesel trucks, and in the adverse combination has only about 21 million to spare. And the 80 million for the leases is money paid to wait: it is worth only as much as the clauses the lessor actually signs, and if the roadmap still has not appeared by the end of 2029, that money does not come back and the company is facing the same question again. The PESTEL table does none of those calculations itself; it only says which variables the calculations must run on, and which ones need testing when they turn bad together.

Summary

Key Point: PESTEL analysis works by making the people scanning walk through six fixed headings of the macro environment, political, economic, social, technological, environmental and legal, so that areas outside their usual line of sight are less likely to be missed. The most counter-intuitive point concerns where uncertainty concentrates: in the study by Daft, Sormunen and Parks of the chief executives of 50 manufacturing companies, the high performers scanned more broadly and more often when facing uncertainty, while the most uncertain sectors were customers, the economy and competitors, two of which sit outside the six headings. That is an observed association in scanning behaviour, not a test of PESTEL itself. The principles: anchor to a decision and a time frame, write each factor as a driver with a direction and a date and keep it apart from the data of any one option, add a scan of customers and competitors, sort by importance and certainty instead of filling six columns evenly, test each critical uncertainty at both ends and in an adverse combination before demoting it to monitoring, then write down in advance the signals that will force the table to be redone.
