Porter's Five Forces

An industry's profits are not taken by rivals alone: buyers, suppliers, substitutes and would-be entrants all take a share; the tool's job is to find which force holds the largest share, and why

Published: November 18, 2026

Definition

What Are the Five Competitive Forces

Suppose an investor in Cần Thơ is thinking of opening a plant that makes purified ice cubes for the city's cafés and restaurants. Demand looks solid: new cafés open every month, the dry season is long, and every café needs ice every day. Yet an industry everyone needs can still be an industry hardly anyone profits from. Eleven plants already sell almost the same product, and in the rainy season, when there is spare capacity, someone always cuts prices. A small café can switch suppliers with one phone call. A busy one can buy an ice machine and put it behind the counter. And anyone who wants to open the twelfth plant needs only a few machines, a small cold store, a truck and a food safety certificate. The five competitive forces are a way of reading an industry's profitability through five pressures like these: rivalry among existing competitors, the bargaining power of buyers, the bargaining power of suppliers, the threat of substitutes, and the threat of new entrants. Michael E. Porter, then a young economist and associate professor at Harvard Business School, set out the framework in "How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy", his first article for Harvard Business Review, in 1979. [1]

In 2008 Porter rewrote the framework in "The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy", also in Harvard Business Review: a reaffirmation, update and extension of the original, with corrections of common misunderstandings and guidance for practitioners. [1] This piece uses the 2008 version for every test question, because it is the version in which the tool's own author lays out the steps of analysis and the list of mistakes. Its core idea: competition for profits reaches beyond today's rivals to customers, suppliers, potential entrants and substitutes, and the five forces together make up an industry's structure. For the ice plant, then, the question worth asking is not about demand. It is about the three heaviest pressures, the entrant waiting to come in, the machine behind the counter and the plants already cutting prices: which of them is holding the price of a bag of ice down.

The two nearest entries in this series are PESTEL analysis and SWOT analysis. PESTEL scans the broad conditions shared by a whole industry; the five forces look at the parties that directly split the industry's profits with it. Porter draws this line himself when he writes that industry growth rate, technology, government and complements are factors, not forces. In his view government should not be treated as a sixth force, because government involvement is neither inherently good nor bad for industry profitability; the right approach is to trace how each specific policy affects the five forces, as patents raise barriers to entry. [1] Porter also writes that rising aggregate demand enlarges the pie, but how a bigger pie is divided is still decided by the five forces. On this piece's reading, a PESTEL line therefore reaches an industry's profitability by two routes: by making the pie bigger or smaller, and by making one of the five forces stronger or weaker; the five forces handle the second route. On SWOT, Porter writes that the five forces give a baseline against which to measure a company's strengths and weaknesses: where the company stands relative to buyers, suppliers, entrants, rivals and substitutes. [1] So the output of a five forces analysis feeds the top two boxes of a SWOT table too, not just opportunities and threats.

Why We Need It

Without the five forces, the investor in Cần Thơ tends to stop at two easy numbers: how many new cafés are opening, and what the eleven existing plants charge. Both look good. What neither number shows is what happens after the plant opens: today's attractive ice price is exactly the reason someone else will open a thirteenth plant, and a café busy enough will start weighing the price of a bag of ice against the cost of a machine. Porter calls the belief that fast-growing industries are always attractive a common mistake. Fast growth does tend to soften rivalry, but high growth with low entry barriers draws in newcomers, and in his view a fixation on growth is one of the main causes of bad strategic decisions. [1]

The tool's mechanism lies in the question of who the value goes to. According to Porter, industry structure, expressed in the strength of the five forces, determines an industry's long-run profit potential because it determines how the economic value the industry creates is divided: how much the companies in the industry keep, and how much is bargained away by customers and suppliers, capped by substitutes, or held down by potential entrants. The strongest force, or forces, determine the industry's profitability, and that force is not always the obvious one. Commodity industries are usually fiercely competitive, yet rivalry is not necessarily what limits profits: the low profitability of the photographic film industry was the result of a superior substitute, as Kodak and Fuji, the world's two largest film makers, learned when digital photography arrived. One more detail is easy to miss: it is the threat of entry, not whether entry actually happens, that holds profitability down. [1] The ice plants do not have to watch a twelfth plant open before they hold their prices; it is enough to know how easy opening one would be.

Two pieces of data explain why the industry level deserves a look. The first is Porter's own, calculated from Standard & Poor's Compustat data: average return on invested capital (ROIC) in US industries over 1992-2006 had a median of 14.3%, but the industry at the 10th percentile earned only 7.0% while the one at the 90th percentile earned 25.3%. In the table of industries he chose as illustrations, soft drinks earned 37.6%, soft drink bottling 11.7%, airlines 5.9%. [1] These are descriptive figures: they show that profitability varies widely between industries, not that the variation comes from the five forces. The second piece measures more closely: in a 1997 study by Anita McGahan and Porter, which industry a business was in accounted for about 19% of the variance in profits between business segments of US public companies. [2] The research section spells out how far that figure goes, and why it is also the tool's biggest limit.

What Are Its Limits

The first built-in limit: the five forces are an industry-level analysis. They explain an industry's average profitability, not why two companies in the same industry earn different returns. In McGahan and Porter's own study, the share of profit variance associated with the characteristics of each business segment, about 32%, is larger than the share associated with industry. [2] An aggregate figure like that can hide an imbalance: on their own data set, Gabriel Hawawini, Venkat Subramanian and Paul Verdin found that most of the firm-specific effect came from a few standout companies in each industry, those that created exceptional value or destroyed a lot of it; for the remaining majority, industry mattered more to performance than firm-specific factors. [3] The five forces therefore say a good deal about an average company in an industry, and little about the leader or the laggard. Porter does use his framework to look for positions within an industry where the forces are weaker [1], but five boxes do not tell you whether a company has what it takes to win and hold such a position. That question belongs to strengths and weaknesses.

The second limit: industry boundaries are a judgement. Porter treats defining an industry too broadly or too narrowly as the source of many strategic mistakes, and offers a rule of thumb: two products with the same buyers, suppliers and barriers to entry should be treated as one industry, while a large difference in one force, and especially in more than one, suggests two industries. But he also writes that differences between the forces are a matter of degree, so defining an industry is often a matter of judgement. [1] Whether the ice plant in Cần Thơ belongs to the industry of "ice sold to cafés in the city" or the industry of "drinks in the Mekong Delta" is a choice between two very different sets of five forces.

The third limit: the framework has exactly five boxes, and whatever is not a force has to be translated through them. Complements, products used alongside the industry's product that raise its value, as petrol stations do for cars, have no box of their own. Porter cites Adam Brandenburger and Barry Nalebuff as an example of the line of research that stresses complements, then replies that they are not a sixth force, because strong complements are not necessarily good or bad for profitability; the analyst has to trace how they affect each force. [1] That is a reasoned choice, but it puts the whole burden of translation on the analyst: a five-box table that never mentions complements does not tell you whether its author weighed them and dropped them, or never thought of them.

The remaining failures lie in how the tool is used, and Porter lists them himself. Besides defining the industry wrongly, they are listing instead of analysing, giving all five forces equal attention instead of digging into the most important one, confusing a symptom (price-sensitive customers) with its cause (the buyer's economics), static analysis that ignores trends, confusing cyclical or transient change with structural change, and using the framework to declare an industry attractive or unattractive instead of to guide strategic choices. [1] The listing habit is not only the users' fault. Michael Dobbs, who teaches the framework and works with it alongside small business owners, managers and students, counted how ten leading strategic management textbooks present it: from 4 to 21 pages, only two with an in-depth explanation, most just summarising the concepts, reprinting the five-box figure and telling examples. He argues that Porter's own presentation in the 1980 book Competitive Strategy contributed to the problem: the book has dozens of examples but very rarely attaches numbers, and the original description of the five forces is essentially a list, so users follow it with a list and qualitative ratings. [4] That is the observation and the reading of someone who teaches the tool; he does not measure how widespread the error is. The five-box figure drawn from the default template has no time axis, but Porter's process has a separate step for changes in each force, so static analysis is a failure of use, not of the framework. The fix belongs in How to Apply It.

Examples

The first example is a five forces slide that commits nearly every mistake on Porter's list. The second is Porter using his own framework to read the position of Paccar, a US heavy-truck maker.

The F&B Industry, Five Labels and a Sixth Box

An illustrative scenario: a bubble tea chain with 14 shops in Ho Chi Minh City is raising money to open 20 more over two years. Slide six of the pitch deck is the five competitive forces. The centre box reads "Vietnam F&B industry". Each box has three bullet points and a label: buyers "medium", suppliers "low", substitutes "medium", new entrants "high", rivalry in the middle "high". In the right-hand corner sits a sixth box, "digital transformation and app-based delivery". The conclusion at the bottom says the drinks market is growing fast, so the industry is attractive. The investor moves on to the next slide in under a minute.

Put the slide next to Porter's list of mistakes and almost every line matches. "F&B" is broad enough to take in wedding banquet halls and bánh mì carts, whose buyers, barriers and rivals have little in common with takeaway bubble tea. The five labels stop at listing: none says how much of the price of a cup that force takes. The sixth box is exactly what Porter calls a factor, not a force. Food delivery apps only mean something once they are translated into the boxes, for instance as a distribution channel with bargaining power that charges a fee on every order, which Porter counts among intermediate buyers. The conclusion makes growth the reason for attractiveness, the very trap he warns about, and uses the whole framework to declare the industry attractive, when the question the investor needs answered is whether this chain can keep its margins in 20 new shops. [1]

Ask again the way Porter suggests and at least three numbers have to appear: the cost of opening a competing shop right next door (the barrier to entry), the fee the delivery app takes on each order (the channel's bargaining power), and the average profitability of chains in the same segment over several years, not in their best year. A test for your own slide: cover up the "high, low" labels and see whether anything left says how much that force does to price, cost or capital. If nothing does, the table has only got as far as listing.

Paccar and the Truck Buyers With the Least Bargaining Power

According to Porter, the heavy-truck industry is structurally difficult. Many buyers operate large fleets or are large leasing companies, with both the leverage and the motive to drive down the price of one of their largest purchases. Most trucks are built to regulated standards and offer similar features, so price competition is rampant. The industry is capital intensive, so rivalry gets fiercer in every downturn. Unions exercise considerable supplier power. And although there are few direct substitutes for an 18-wheeler, truck buyers face substitutes for their own services, such as rail freight. [1]

Paccar, based in Bellevue, Washington, with about 20% of the North American heavy-truck market, chose to focus on one group of customers: drivers who own their trucks and contract directly with shippers or serve as subcontractors to larger trucking companies. These operators have little bargaining power when buying a truck, and are less price sensitive because of their emotional and economic ties to the truck, where they spend most of their time. Paccar builds them luxurious sleeper cabins, leather seats, noise-insulated cabs and sleek exteriors. Buyers choose from thousands of options at the dealer, and trucks are built to order and delivered in six to eight weeks. The trucks save fuel thanks to aerodynamic design and hold their resale value better than others; a roadside assistance programme and a parts distribution system cut the time a truck sits idle, which is vital to someone who owns a single truck. Customers pay a 10% premium. Porter sums up that Paccar found a part of the industry where the competitive forces are weaker, avoiding buyer power and price-based rivalry, and that by the time the article appeared the company had been profitable for 68 straight years, with a long-run return on equity above 20%. [1]

This is Porter reading Paccar's position through his own framework; the article does not say Paccar's management ran a five forces analysis. The two outcome figures have no comparison company, and the position covers product, dealers and parts all at once, so there is no way to separate the part that came from choosing customers alone. What the example teaches is a way of reading: split buyers into groups, find the heaviest force in the whole industry, here large buyers and price rivalry, then look for the group of customers where that same force is weak.

Some Research on Porter's Five Forces

The five forces are among the most widely used strategy frameworks, while the evidence on the framework itself is far thinner than that popularity. A review by Albana Berisha Qehaja, Enver Kutllovci and Justina Shiroka Pula of 25 empirical studies from 1990 to 2015 on the use of strategic management tools ranks five forces analysis sixth among the ten most used tools, appearing in the most-used list of 20% of the studies, 5 out of 25. According to the authors, it is, together with SWOT, among the most used tools regardless of whether a country is developed or developing. Those studies used different questionnaires listing different tools [5], so the ranking tells you the tool is often mentioned, not how many companies use it. On effectiveness, within the scope of this piece's search, no study was found comparing the results of organisations with and without a five forces analysis. The nearest evidence sits one level up, at the premise the whole tool rests on: the industry a company is in has a substantial effect on its profitability. This line of research tests that premise by splitting the variance in profits between business units into parts associated with year, industry, corporate parent and the characteristics of each unit. The study below was chosen because Porter himself is a co-author, and because its results both support and limit the tool.

The study: Anita McGahan and Michael Porter took the profits of business segments of US public companies, assigned each segment to an industry by four-digit SIC code (the US Standard Industrial Classification), and split the variance in profits between segments into four parts: those associated with year, industry, corporate parent, and the characteristics of each segment. The article appeared in a July 1997 special issue of the Strategic Management Journal. [2] This piece read the study through its abstract. According to a summary table by Spyridon Stavropoulos, Martijn Burger and Dimitris Skuras, the data run from 1981 to 1994 [6]; the sample size is something this piece has not been able to check.

Findings: Year, industry, corporate parent and segment characteristics accounted for about 2%, 19%, 4% and 32% of the total variance in profits respectively. The industry share varied by economic sector: smaller in manufacturing, larger in lodging and entertainment, services, wholesale and retail trade, and transportation. [2]

What it shows: In that sample, which industry a business belonged to went with a substantial but not a major share of the variance in profits, and that share was smaller than the one associated with each segment's own characteristics. This is a variance decomposition on observational data: it shows how far profits differ by industry, not that the difference is produced by the five forces, and it does not measure who used the tool and who did not. The four parts add up to less than 60%, and the abstract does not say what the rest consists of. One of the two authors is also the person who created the tool.

The counter-case: Defenders have an argument in two parts. Part one: 19% is not small, especially next to the 4% for the corporate parent, and Porter writes that differences in profitability between industries are remarkably persistent over time [1], so a tool that helps choose the right industry, or leave at the right moment, is working on a real variable. Part two: the largest share, each segment's own characteristics, is partly the position within the industry itself, which the five forces are also used to find, as in the Paccar case. This piece accepts part one, and another study supports it further: measuring by economic profit and the ratio of market value to book value, Hawawini and colleagues found that firm-specific factors outweighed industry only for the few leading or lagging companies, while for the majority, industry weighed more. [3] The condition is that the industry share varies by economic sector, as McGahan and Porter's own study shows, and varies with the measure. Richard Rumelt, on a different and earlier data set, 1974-1977 according to the same summary table, found industry effects small but stable and business-unit effects very large. [7] Bart Vanneste pooled 18 samples from 16 studies, 225,183 observations in all: measured by variance, the effect sizes are 0.08 for industry, 0.14 for corporate parent and 0.36 for business unit; measured by standard deviation they are 0.28, 0.36 and 0.59, and on the second measure industry plus corporate parent explain at least as much as business unit. [8] Industry's rank changes with the measure. Part two this piece does not accept. That each segment's own share reflects its position against the five forces is one reading, and a variance decomposition does not measure it; that share could just as well be operational capability, technology or luck. The honest answer is that the five forces answer well the question of why one industry earns more than another, and for a company that is neither leader nor laggard in its industry that answer weighs more than anything particular to the company; the question of why the leader earns far more than everyone else in the same industry, they answer only in part.

Why this is useful: It makes you separate the questions before opening the five boxes. Next time, before the analysis meeting, put two numbers side by side: the industry's average margin over three to five years, and the gap between your company and that average. The five forces explain the first number. The second needs a further question about position: which customers, which part of the industry the company stands in, and which force is weaker there than in general. If the gap is larger than the spread between the industries you are weighing, do not start by changing industries.

How to Apply It

The five competitive forces work by showing who the value an industry creates is divided among: the share the companies in the industry keep, the share bargained away by buyers and suppliers, the share capped by substitutes, and the share given up so that newcomers stay away, so the effective way to use them is to measure each force by its effect on price, cost and capital, find the force holding the largest share, and then choose a position where that force is weak. The tool earns its keep when the decision is which industry, which customers or which part of an industry to choose, and earns it least when the question is why your company does worse than a rival in the same industry serving the same customers.

  • Draw the industry boundary by product and by geography before filling in any box: Two products with the same buyers, suppliers and barriers to entry are one industry; a large difference in one force, and especially in more than one, suggests two industries, the way Porter separates motor oil for cars from oil for trucks and stationary engines [1]. Once the line is drawn, check it backwards: does a product left outside the boundary reappear in the substitutes box, does an overlooked rival reappear in the new entrants box, since Porter sees that as the way a careful analysis exposes its own boundary mistakes
  • Measure each force by its causes and by its effect on price, cost and capital: Porter advises looking at industry structure in numbers as far as possible, such as the share of the industry's product in the buyer's total costs, the buyer's switching costs, the share of industry sales a plant needs to reach efficient scale, and then asking of each force: how much does it take [1]. The obvious approach is to label each box high, medium or low; labels look tidy but mix symptoms with causes, and do not say which force holds the largest share. Write the cause first (buyers are price sensitive because the product is this large a part of their costs), the symptom second
  • Test the analysis against the industry's actual profitability over a cycle: The time horizon should be a full business cycle for the industry, three to five years for most industries, and what to look at is average profitability over that span, not a single year; then ask whether the analysis matches actual long-run profitability, and whether the more profitable players stand in better positions against the five forces [1]. An analysis that does not match the real numbers is usually missing a force or has drawn the boundary wrongly. Growth, technology, policy and complements have no box of their own: each is written into the force it strengthens or weakens
  • Use the result to choose where to stand, not to judge whether the industry is attractive: Porter writes that the point of industry analysis is not to declare an industry attractive or unattractive but to understand the roots of profitability [1]; the output worth having is a choice: which customers, which part of the industry has weaker forces, which force the company can influence. This approach fails when it skips the question about the company itself. An industry unattractive to the average entrant can still be worth entering for a company with reason to believe it can clear the barriers more cheaply than most, as Porter himself acknowledges, and in McGahan and Porter's study the share particular to each segment is larger than the industry share [2], and on a different data set a firm-specific share that large comes mainly from companies that lead or lag far behind their industry [3]. A company aiming for a position away from the industry average is betting on exactly that particular share, so set the five forces result against the company's strengths and weaknesses before deciding

Applying It to a Scenario

An industrial catering company in Thuận An, Bình Dương, has a central kitchen with a capacity of 3,600 meals a day and is cooking 3,000 lunches for three factories under one-year contracts, retendered every December. Gross margin per meal has fallen three years in a row. The directors have 1.5 billion dong for 2027 and two options: spend it on an extra 1,200-meal line to bid for factory D in January, or open a line of meals for private kindergartens using the 600 meals of spare capacity. What the company needs by December 15, 2026: a choice of option for the 1.5 billion and a floor price for factory tenders, with each decision traceable to the force holding each segment's profits and to the number that checks it. All figures are illustrative. The five forces do not replace an investment calculation; they show why each segment's gross margin is where it is, and whether that level is likely to hold.

  1. Draw the industry boundary: Are factory meals and kindergarten meals one industry? The product is much the same, from the same kitchen and the same wholesale market. But the buyers differ (a factory purchasing department scoring tenders every year, against a head teacher answerable to parents), the substitutes differ (a factory's own canteen or a meal allowance, against the school's own kitchen), and the rivals differ. The differences lie in more than one force, so the team analyses them as two industries. Geographic scope: meals must arrive within 45 minutes of cooking, which means within a radius of about 15 km of the kitchen
  2. Name the parties in each industry: Factory segment: 14 factories with more than 1,000 workers within that radius buy in their meals; 9 catering companies regularly bid, two of them from Đồng Nai and bidding only since 2025; substitutes are in-house canteens and meal allowances. School segment: 37 private kindergartens, 30 cooking for themselves, 7 buying in a total of 1,610 meals a day, served by 3 small companies; the substitute is the school's own kitchen; potential entrants are the other factory caterers, this company included. Suppliers are the same for both segments: the wholesale market, two gas dealers, kitchen labour
  3. Assess the causes of each force, in numbers where possible: In the factory segment, meals cost about 624,000 dong per worker per month, nearly 7% of a 9 million wage, large enough for purchasing departments to score tenders on price: of the six tenders the company has bid for since 2023, five went to the lowest bidder, and the company won two. The canteen belongs to the factory, so changing caterers takes a week. In the school segment, one case of food poisoning can cost a school its parents and its licence, so quality weighs more than price; six of the seven schools that buy in, when asked, said they had used their current caterer for 4.5 years on average and only change after an incident. The heaviest force here is the school's own kitchen: three head teachers of self-catering schools estimate their cost per meal at about 27,000 to 29,000 dong, including the cook's wages, against the 32,000 dong caterers charge; the seven schools that buy in mostly do so because they have no room left for a kitchen.   4. Identify the structure and test it against actual profitability: The factory segment is held by buyer power plus price rivalry. The company's own numbers match: the tender price fell by 1,000 dong at each retender, from 26,000 dong in 2023 to 24,000 dong in 2025, while variable cost per meal (ingredients, direct kitchen labour, transport) barely moved, so gross margin per meal went from 3,600 to 2,700 to 2,000 dong. The school segment is capped by the school's own kitchen. To get a figure measured on the same definition of gross margin, the company cooked for one school, 180 meals a day, for 44 days in September and October 2026: a price of 32,000 dong, variable cost of 25,600 dong including a separate children's menu and a small delivery van, gross margin of 6,400 dong a meal. Porter's second test, whether the more profitable players stand in better positions: according to the three head teachers, the caterer serving four of the seven schools has not lost a school in five years and raised its prices in both of the last two years, which fits a segment where buyers exert little price pressure. Both school-segment numbers are thin: one school, two months, and hearsay
  5. Analyse changes in each force: In the factory segment, the two Đồng Nai caterers that arrived in 2025 have sharpened rivalry, and there is no sign that buyer power will weaken. If the observed fall of 1,000 dong per retender continues, the price will be 23,000 dong in 2028 and 22,000 dong in 2029; this is a reference scenario, not a forecast. In the school segment, two of the seven schools that buy in say they will build their own kitchens when they move premises in 2028, so the substitute grows stronger; according to the ward's licensing list, four new private kindergartens open in 2027 in apartment complexes with no room for a kitchen, so buyers grow in number.   6. Find the places in the structure the company can influence: In the school segment, the force to weaken is the school's own kitchen, so the company picks things a school cook would struggle to do alone: menus planned by a nutritionist for each age group, a daily photo of the meal sent to parents, food samples kept and tested periodically. Two-year contracts carry a clause adjusting price to food costs, raising switching costs without pushing the price above the ceiling set by the school's own kitchen. In the factory segment, buyers are strong and the product is the same everywhere, so the company does not try to change the structure and sets a floor price instead: the kitchen's overhead works out at about 1,000 dong a meal, so it will not bid below 23,000 dong
  7. Turn it into a decision and review points: Compare the two options on one yardstick: three years of gross margin, less the added fixed costs, set against the 1.5 billion (second table). The two options are calculated on reference scenarios built differently, and that difference has to be stated: the factory option keeps the price decline observed over three years, the school option keeps the gross margin of the trial run, since there is no trend to keep yet. On that basis, the factory option, if the tender is won, brings 748.8 million in the first year but only 1,123.2 million over three years, short of recovering the investment, and by 2029 the price is below the very floor the company has just set; the school option adds up to 1,781.28 million when running at the full 600 meals. If the tender price stays at 24,000 dong, the factory option adds up to 2,246.4 million, more than the school option. The choice therefore turns on one question the five forces can answer: will the factory tender price keep falling. What steps 3 and 5 found, buyers scoring tenders on price, caterers changed within a week, two new caterers arriving, all point towards further falls, and so do the company's own three years of figures. The factory option also has to win the tender first, while the company has won only two of its last six. The school option needs at least 545 meals a day, about three average-sized schools, to recover the 1.5 billion in three years. School option: 600 × 264 days (22 days × 12 months, private kindergartens open all year) = 158,400 meals a year; 6,400 × 158,400 = 1,013.76 million; added fixed costs: nutritionist 15 million × 12 = 180 million, two drivers and vehicle insurance 2 × 10 million × 12 = 240 million, total 420 million; leaving 593.76 million a year; three years 1,781.28 million; payback 1,500 / 593.76 ≈ 2.5 years. Threshold for recovery within three years: 1,500 / 3 = 500 million a year, plus 420 million fixed costs makes 920 million of gross margin; at 6,400 dong a meal that needs 920,000,000 / 6,400 = 143,750 meals a year, 143,750 / 264 ≈ 544.5, rounded up to 545 meals a day; at 600 meals a day it needs 920,000,000 / 158,400 ≈ 5,808 dong a meal. The seven schools that buy in: 1,610 / 7 = 230 meals per school. Factory floor price: kitchen overhead 936 million a year / (3,000 × 312 = 936,000 meals) = 1,000 dong a meal; 22,000 + 1,000 = 23,000 dong. Neither option allows for the time needed to ramp up to full capacity.

Achieved: before December 15, the company has a decision for the 1.5 billion and a floor price for factory tenders, each traceable to the force holding each segment's profits: buyers plus price rivalry in the factory segment, the school's own kitchen in the kindergarten segment. The choice rests on four conditions. The 6,400 dong gross margin comes from one school over two months; if the first three schools produce a figure below 5,808 dong at 600 meals, the investment is not recovered within three years. The calculation leaves out the time it takes to sign enough schools, so every month short of schools is a month added to payback. The price ceiling set by the school's own kitchen rests on three head teachers' estimates; if the real cost of cooking in-house is lower, the ceiling is lower. And the five forces of the school segment may change precisely because the company comes in: a segment with a 20% gross margin whose only barrier is head teachers' trust is unlikely to keep its rivals few for long. The analysis does not prove that the school segment is the best choice; it shows that the factory segment's low margin has a structural cause, though three years of figures are only the lower end of the three-to-five-year span Porter recommends, and it shows which force to watch in the new segment.

Summary

Key Point: The five competitive forces work by showing who the value an industry creates is divided among: the share the companies in the industry keep, the share bargained away by buyers and suppliers, the share capped by substitutes, and the share given up so that newcomers stay away. The most counter-intuitive part is that what is easy to see is not necessarily what decides: according to Porter, fast growth does not by itself make an industry attractive, and it is the threat of entry, not actual entry, that holds profitability down. Industry is also only part of the story: in McGahan and Porter's study of business segments of US public companies, industry accounted for about 19% of the variance in profits, less than the share associated with each segment's own characteristics, about 32%. On a different data set, Hawawini and colleagues found that firm-specific factors outweighed industry only for the few leading or lagging companies; for most companies, industry weighed more. The principle: draw the industry boundary first, measure each force by its causes and by its effect on price, cost and capital, test the analysis against actual profitability over a cycle, then use the result to choose a position where the forces are weaker, set against the company's own capabilities.
