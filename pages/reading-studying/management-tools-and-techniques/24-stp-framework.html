STP Framework

Segmentation only pays when the groups respond differently to the same offer; the buyers of competing brands tend to be far more alike than marketers imagine

Published: November 19, 2026

Definition

What Is the STP Framework

Suppose an optician's in Hanoi has three kinds of regular customer. Parents bring in a child who has just become short-sighted; they need someone who tests carefully, a pair of frames that can survive a child, and a schedule of check-ups, because a child's prescription keeps rising. Office workers come in to choose frames; they want good-looking designs and their glasses the same day. People over fifty need progressive lenses and a consultation long enough to get used to them. The same leaflet, "30% off all frames", reads to these three groups like three different invitations, and only one of them really treats price as a reason to choose a shop. The STP framework is a way of working with a market like this in three steps. Market segmentation divides buyers into groups so that within each group, needs and responses to the same offer are much alike, and between groups they differ. Targeting assesses the groups and chooses one or a few to serve. Positioning chooses where the company stands in that group's mind relative to its competitors, then makes the product, price, sales channel and message all say the same thing.

STP has no single founding text and no single author. It joins two ideas born sixteen years apart. The first is segmentation: in "Product Differentiation and Market Segmentation as Alternative Marketing Strategies", in the Journal of Marketing in 1956, Wendell R. Smith set market segmentation beside product differentiation as two alternative strategies, and argued that each company should meet the particular requirements of specific segments. [1] [2] The second is positioning: Al Ries and Jack Trout set it out in a three-part series, "The Positioning Era Cometh", in Advertising Age in April and May 1972, and then in the 1981 book Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind. According to the reprint of the series, a company has to create a position in the prospect's mind, one that takes into account the positions of its competitors. The series itself does not claim to have started anything: it takes a full-page advertisement by David Ogilvy in the New York Times on April 7, 1971, in which Ogilvy put the decision on how to position a product at the top of a list of 38 things that make advertising sell, as the moment the positioning era began. [3] Within this piece's search, no original text has turned up that joins the three steps into the STP label. Zachary Anesbury, Maxwell Winchester and Rachel Kennedy describe segmentation as a common focus of marketing, part of the segmentation, targeting, positioning framework used as a basis for competitive strategy. [2] This piece uses that common three-step presentation for every question it tests.

The nearest entry in this series is Porter's five competitive forces, a framework for reading the profitability of a whole industry through the pressure from buyers, suppliers, substitutes, new entrants and rivals. In this piece's reading, the two frameworks ask two different questions: the five forces ask which industry, and which part of it, keeps its profits; STP asks, within a market already chosen, which group of buyers the company serves and why that group should choose it. The two questions meet at the targeting step, where an attractive group of customers is often one whose buyers put less pressure on price than the market as a whole.

Why We Need It

The easiest thing for the optician's to do is cut prices for everyone, print leaflets for everyone, and put every kind of frame on the same shelf. The promotion pulls in exactly the people who will move to the shop next door when that shop offers 35% off. The parents of the newly short-sighted child see no reason to choose this shop over another, because what they need, someone who tests carefully and a promise of a check-up, is not on the leaflet. The shop competes on the one dimension every shop has: price.

The tool's mechanism lies in separating the groups that respond differently to the same offer. When needs really do differ, as at the optician's, one offer for everyone easily becomes an offer that stands out for no one; separate the groups and the company can design an offer that clearly wins in one of them, choose the group it serves better than its rivals, and use positioning so that the group can see that choice.

The reason the tool is needed is also the reason to use it with care. The mechanism only works when the groups really differ, and when where they differ can be measured. In a study of more than 700 consumer packaged goods brands in the UK, Anesbury and colleagues found that large differences in the kind of buyer between competing brands were the exception: on average each year, only 12% of deviations exceeded 5 percentage points, and the exceptions the authors point out came with a difference in what the product does, such as electric toothbrushes or baby shampoo. [2] The Research section explains what that figure measures, and why it speaks to only one way of using the tool. For someone using STP, it says the tool has two jobs: to find the differences that are real, which in that data lie in needs and product function more than in kinds of people, and to stop the company spending money on differences that exist only on a slide.

What Are Its Limits

STP does not tell you whether a market is worth entering, and that is its first built-in limit. Its three steps begin after the market has been chosen; the question of profitability for a whole industry, or for a particular investment, belongs to other tools. Nor does STP create the ability to serve the group chosen: making the parents of short-sighted children the target does not give the optician's one more optometrist.

The second limit: segments are built, not found. Which variables you divide by, and with which technique (cluster analysis, which groups together people who answer alike; regression; factor analysis), decides which groups come out. Anesbury and colleagues write that it is not known whether different techniques or variables produce equivalent results, that the complex mathematics of many approaches makes the results hard to check, and that the usual practice is to assume segments exist rather than test whether they do. [2] Five customer groups with five vivid names do not prove that the market has five groups; they prove that the data can be divided into five.

The third limit is the price of choosing. The same authors point out that targeting means leaving a large part of the market outside. [2] The tool promises a trade-off: focus in order to win clearly in one group. The loss side of that trade-off is certain; for the gain side, within this piece's search, no controlled comparison has turned up that measures it.

The remaining failures lie in how the tool is used. The most common is dividing buyers by who they are (age, income, lifestyle attitudes) to find a brand's "own customers", when directly competing brands sell almost the same thing. Kennedy and Ehrenberg distinguish two things that are easily lumped together: brand segmentation, such as people who fly American Airlines against people who fly United, and product segmentation, such as economy passengers against business-class passengers (cited through Anesbury and colleagues). [2] The second failure is in implementation: the literature review and company cases examined by Sally Dibb and Lyndon Simkin show the barriers falling into three areas, the infrastructure in place at the start, the segmentation process itself, and putting the results into operation (read through the abstract). [4] A good segmentation that leaves the sales team pitching every customer the same way has not yet changed anything. The fixes belong to How to Apply It.

Examples

The two examples below go through the three steps in opposite directions. One is a staged segmentation and positioning exercise that divides buyers by who they are rather than by why they buy; the other is how Marriott used a large-scale customer study to choose a segment, position and design a new hotel chain.

"Linh, 27", and the Bottle of Tea That Stayed the Same

An illustrative scenario: a bottled green tea brand in fourth place in its category hires an advertising agency to redo its strategy. The agency surveys the attitudes of a thousand bottled tea drinkers, runs a cluster analysis, and comes out with five groups bearing five vivid names. The group chosen is "young health-conscious office workers", complete with a persona: Linh, 27, works in marketing, does yoga, reads nutrition labels. The new positioning statement is "green tea for busy people who look after themselves". Most of the advertising budget moves to social media and lift screens in office towers. The bottle, the recipe, the price and the points of sale stay the same. A year later sales have not grown. When the company sets its own user profile, taken from household panel data (a fixed group of households that record everything they buy), beside the profiles of the two leading brands, the share of buyers who are office workers under 35 differs by only a few percentage points across the three.

All three steps are there, and all three slip in the same place. The segmentation step divides tea drinkers by who they are, when green tea brands sell almost the same thing to the same people: exactly the brand segmentation that household panel data in the UK show is rarely real. [2] The targeting step does not change the product for the chosen group; it only narrows where the advertising goes, so most of the category's buyers no longer see the brand at all. The positioning step is a form of words: there is nothing in the bottle that lets buyers check it is more "for busy people" than the bottle next to it.

Redone according to the mechanism, the first question is in which situations people drink bottled tea and what each situation needs differently from the product: an office lunch, a long drive, after a workout. A situation that needs a smaller bottle or less sugar is a segment the product can answer. Try it on your own segmentation: cover up the group names and personas, and ask whether what remains says anything this group needs that your product will do differently. If not, you are choosing where to place advertising, not segmenting.

Courtyard by Marriott: A Hotel Chain Designed From 50 Attributes

According to a 2000 article from Knowledge at Wharton, Marriott hired two Wharton marketing professors, Paul Green and Jerry Wind, to carry out a large-scale customer study of both business and non-business travellers. The aim was to find an "optimal" hotel, one that would attract new customers without eating into the customers existing Marriott hotels already had. [5] The tool was conjoint analysis: respondents choose between hotel options assembled from different attributes, and from those choices you infer how much they value each attribute and what they are willing to give up for what. According to a review of conjoint analysis in the hospitality industry, the study considered 50 attributes, each with two to eight levels, using the hybrid conjoint model Green set out in 1984. [6]

The article on the project, written by Wind, Green, Douglas Shifflet and Marsha Scarbrough (of Courtyard by Marriott) in Interfaces in 1989, summarises the study as producing specific guidelines for selecting target market segments, positioning the service, and designing the hotel's facilities and services. Marriott built the Courtyard by Marriott concept from those recommendations, test-marketed it successfully, rolled it out nationally, and later used a similar approach for other lodging products. [7] The Wharton article says the chain grew from three hotels in 1983 to about 450 hotels worldwide by 2000. [5]

This piece reads the project through the article's abstract and two secondary sources. The growth figure comes from the two authors' own school, with no comparison, and a hotel chain grows on capital, locations and the Marriott name as well, so there is no separating the share owed to the study. The case is worth reading for how the tool was used: segmentation led to a different product, not just a different advertising campaign; the targeting step was constrained by the existing portfolio, since the group worth choosing was one the current hotels did not yet serve; and positioning came from the attributes guests actually traded off against each other, measured by their choices rather than by how they described themselves.

Some Research on the STP Framework

Segmentation is one of the most studied subjects in marketing, and a large item of spending: according to ESOMAR figures cited by Anesbury and colleagues, the world spent more than 40 billion US dollars on market research in 2013, and segmentation studies have long made up a substantial part of that. [2] Evidence for the value of the tool itself is much thinner. The same authors write that there is little research on how segmentation is used in practice, especially on its contribution to growth and profit, and that implementation studies comparing a segmentation-based targeting strategy with a more mass-market one are entirely missing [2]; within this piece's search, no such study has turned up either. The Courtyard case shows how the tool works when used to design a product, but has no comparison. What has been measured many times is a premise that the targeting and positioning steps stand on: whether competing brands really are chosen by different kinds of buyer. Kathy Hammond, Andrew Ehrenberg and G. J. Goodhardt asked exactly that question in 1996, on consumer panel data from more than 20 grocery categories, and found that the user profiles of competing brands differed very little on common classification variables such as demographics (read through the abstract). [8] The study below repeats that measurement on a larger scale and adds the dimension of time.

The study: Zachary Anesbury, Maxwell Winchester and Rachel Kennedy took data from Kantar Worldpanel, a panel of 15,000 households representative of the UK population by demographics and geography, for 69 consumer packaged goods categories, such as deodorant, fruit juice and nappies: more than 700 brands and 163 variables, each category followed over several consecutive years, from three to six. Of the variables, 86% were attitudinal ("I tend to look at the price per unit", "I like to treat myself", "I try to support local food producers") and 9% demographic. For each brand, the authors calculated the share of the brand's users falling into each group of a variable, for example 10% of Coca-Cola users living in single-person households, then subtracted the average for competing brands. The mean absolute deviation (MAD) across brands shows how far apart their user profiles are; according to the authors, a MAD below 5 is unlikely to mean anything to a manager. The article appeared in Marketing Letters in 2017. [2] This piece read the full text of the peer-reviewed manuscript.

Findings: In the cola category, covering the 14 largest brands each year plus an "all other brands" group from 2008 to 2012, the category's average MAD was always 2, and in no year above 5, for both demographic and attitudinal variables. The other categories gave results highly consistent with cola. Taken together, on average each year 12% of deviations exceeded 5 percentage points and only 2.3% exceeded 10 points. The main exception in stability was toothbrushes, with MAD varying from 2 to 4 over the years while the category's technology was changing: electric toothbrush brands skewed towards older couples without children, and the deviation grew over time. Functional differences produced other large deviations, such as Johnson's baby shampoo skewing well away from older households and households without children. Small brands deviated more than large ones: in cola in 2012, MAD was 1.1 for the five largest brands, 1.9 for the five middle brands and 2.3 for the four smallest. [2]

What it shows: In UK consumer packaged goods, the buyers of competing brands are almost the same kind of people when measured by demographics and attitudes, and stay that way over three to six years; the large deviations the authors could explain all came with a difference in what the product does. The authors write that this result calls into question the use of the segmentation, targeting, positioning framework as a basis for growth strategy, especially when segments are defined by demographics and attitudes. The study does not measure the profitability of using STP. Nor does it cover services, durable goods or business markets, and it does not use behavioural variables or the benefits buyers seek; the authors themselves list these limits. [2]

The counter-case: Defenders would answer with two points. The first: STP never promised that two brands selling the same thing would have two different kinds of customer; it promised that dividing by need would show which product is worth making for whom. Measuring the demographic and attitudinal profiles of buyers of competing brands tests one way of using the tool, and has not touched what the tool promises to do. The second: profiles look alike because competitors copy each other's successes, so the result speaks to competitive pressure and does not refute the value of finding a group of customers before anyone else does; and small brands, where niche strategies live, really do deviate more. This piece accepts most of the first point. The study itself distinguishes brand segmentation from product segmentation and measures only the former, and the exceptions it found, electric toothbrushes and baby shampoo, are all places where products differ in function, which is exactly the kind of difference product segmentation aims at; the Courtyard case belongs here too. The second point this piece accepts only by half. Competitors copying each other is real, and the authors also write that brands tend to copy each other's successes, so deviations do not usually last. [2] But that does not rescue the common use; it makes its limit clearer: a position competitors can copy within a few years does not hold on to a group of customers of its own. And small brands do deviate more, but in cola the MAD of the four smallest brands is 2.3, still below the threshold of 5 the authors consider meaningful to a manager. Weighed up, this evidence hits hardest at the most common use: claiming that your brand's buyers are a distinct kind of person and then narrowing advertising towards them. It does not refute using STP to decide what product to make for which need, but no controlled measurement supports that use either; the Courtyard case is one success, not a comparison. And all of it comes from UK consumer packaged goods, and says nothing yet about services or business markets.

Why this is useful: It gives you a test you can run before the next segmentation meeting. Take the user profile of your brand and of your two closest competitors, from household panel data, loyalty-scheme customer data, or a survey of buyers across the category, and set them side by side on the same variables the company plans to divide by. If most of the differences are below 5 percentage points, your "target customers" are simply the category's buyers, and the segmentation effort should move to usage situations or needs that could make the product different. The question to ask before approving a segment: does this group differ from that one in what they need, or only in who they are?

How to Apply It

The STP framework works by separating the groups of buyers who respond differently to the same offer, then concentrating product, price, sales channel and message on the group the company serves better than anyone else, so using it well means dividing by what actually makes responses differ, checking that difference against buying behaviour before choosing, and resting positioning on something buyers can check when they use the product. The tool earns its keep most when the groups need different products, and least when brands sell almost the same thing to the same buyers.

  • Divide by need and buying situation first, by age and income later: In a story Clayton Christensen told his MBA class, a fast-food chain segmented its milkshake buyers by product and demographics, asked people who fitted the profile what the ideal milkshake would be, made it, and sales did not grow; a researcher who spent a whole day in a restaurant then found that 40% of the milkshakes were bought first thing in the morning, mostly by commuters buying to take away, who needed something to keep a free hand busy and stave off hunger on a long drive [9]. The article does not report sales afterwards, but the two ways of dividing lead to two different directions for changing the product, and the first had been tried without success. In this piece's reading, demographics suit finding the way to a group already defined (where to advertise, which channel to sell in) better than defining the group
  • Check the difference on real purchase data before choosing any group: Set your user profile beside your competitors', and set side by side how the groups responded to the same offer (a price cut, a new product), before trusting a segmentation. The obvious path is to trust the cluster analysis because it produces groups with good names, but the usual practice of assuming segments exist rather than testing whether they do is exactly what Anesbury and colleagues criticise; in their data, deviations below 5 percentage points were the large majority [2]. A group that shows up in an attitude survey but not in buying behaviour is not yet a segment to spend money on
  • Choose a group to design the product for; do not assume you must narrow the reach of advertising: Anesbury and colleagues give the example of Lorna Jane, a sportswear company that makes clothes only for women: aiming advertising at women makes sense, but no segmentation technique is needed to find this group, nor does it avoid competition, because all the large companies of the same kind target exactly that group and copy each other's features [2]. The target group decides who the product, service and way of selling are designed for; it does not oblige the company to stop talking to the category's other buyers. When choosing, add the question Marriott asked: will this group eat into the customers the company already serves [5]
  • Position on a difference the target group values and can check in use, then measure by behaviour: Ries and Trout put the position in the prospect's mind, relative to competitors, and separate that new meaning from the old meaning of the word positioning, which was about the product's shape, package size and price [3]. In this piece's reading, the data on user profiles pull partly the other way: what holds a position for long is the product. In the data of Anesbury and colleagues, the large deviations the authors point out come from functional differences, while other differences do not usually last because competitors copy each other [2]. This approach fails when the difference is only a form of words, or something competitors can copy within a few months. Measure the result by the number of buyers and purchases in the target group, before and after, on the same definition, not by the share of people who remember the positioning statement

Applying It to a Scenario

A company in Da Nang has a self-drive rental fleet of 30 cars, 18 five-seaters and 12 seven-seaters. In the twelve months from October 2025 to the end of September 2026, the fleet was rented for 6,570 of the 10,950 car-days available, or 60%. That overall figure hides a trough: on weekdays (this piece counts Monday to Thursday) outside the three summer months, only 26.8% of car-days were rented. The directors have 240 million dong for 2027. What the company needs by December 15, 2026: a target customer group for the 240 million and a positioning statement for that group, with each choice traceable to real rental data, plus a review point that says when to think again. All figures are illustrative.

  1. Mark out the market: Self-drive car rental, collected in Da Nang city or at the airport, rented by the day. Chauffeur-driven cars and motorbikes are left out, because the buyers, prices and competitors are quite different. The unit of measurement throughout is the car-day, one car rented for one day, so that short-rental and long-rental groups can be compared
  2. Segment by rental situation: Going back through the year's 2,070 contracts, the management team divides them by the reason for renting written on the contract and the place of handover, instead of by the renter's age. Three groups emerge. Families on holiday: 620 contracts, 5 days on average, mostly seven-seaters, booked through a booking app intermediary. Business travellers: 500 contracts, 2 days on average, five-seaters, needing handover at the airport or hotel and an invoice for their company. Local people renting for weekends and holidays: 950 contracts, 2.6 days on average, five-seaters, often asking about price. Contribution margin per car-day (the price actually received after the channel's commission, less cleaning, handover and per-trip wear) is 640,000, 550,000 and 450,000 dong respectively (first table below)
  3. Check that the difference is real in behaviour: Divided by the renter's age, under 30, 30 to 45, over 45, the share of car-days falling on off-season weekdays is 18%, 20% and 19% for the three groups, with contribution margin per car-day between 550,000 and 560,000 dong: the three groups are practically one. Divided by rental situation, the same share is 7.7% for families, 65% for business travellers and 14.7% for local people. The second test is a common offer all three groups received: in October and November 2025, the company cut rental prices by 20% on every weekday on every channel. Compared with the same two months of 2024, with the same 30-car fleet and the same list prices, business travellers barely changed (140 to 142 car-days) but paid 20% less; local people rose from 60 to 84 car-days while contribution margin barely moved; families rose from 40 to 44. The whole promotion added 30 car-days and lost about 26.5 million dong of contribution margin (second table). Tourist demand may have differed between the two years, so this is a clue rather than a clean measurement; but its direction fits the rental situations: business travellers rent because they have a business trip, not because the price is low
  4. Choose the target market: The three groups are weighed on three things: whether the group can fill the trough, contribution margin per car-day, and whether the company has a reason to serve that group better than its competitors. Families give the highest contribution margin, but only 238 of their 3,100 car-days fall on off-season weekdays, because they travel around their children's holidays; putting more money into attracting families mainly pushes demand into the three summer months and weekends, when the cars are already 84 to 85% full. Local people do fill weekdays when given a discount, but the recent promotion showed that this filling brings no contribution margin. Business travellers fill exactly the trough: 650 of their 1,000 car-days already sit there, and 38 companies that rented at least twice in the year account for 400 of those 650 car-days. The target group is companies with staff on business trips to Da Nang. Families remain the largest source of contribution margin, about 1,984 million dong a year, so the company keeps serving them unchanged through the existing channels, it just does not put more money there; nor does it run blanket weekday discounts any more
  5. Position: The management team interviews 12 regular client companies. Nine say the most annoying thing is that staff have to advance the rental and the deposit and then wait to be reimbursed; seven need an invoice on the day; four mention price. These twelve companies say that between them they rent about 900 days of self-drive cars in Da Nang on Monday to Thursday outside the summer each year, and the company has only 250 of those days, about 28% (self-reported figures). Phoning six competitors posing as a customer: five deliver the car to the customer, two issue invoices on the same day, one takes no deposit from business customers, and none lets a company pay at the end of the month. Delivery therefore distinguishes no one; what the target group values most and no competitor yet does is spare staff from advancing money. The positioning statement: "For companies with staff on business trips to Da Nang, this is the self-drive rental where staff don't advance a single dong: no deposit, delivery to the door, invoice on the day, and the company pays at the end of the month." What makes that statement checkable is a corporate account: a credit limit of 30 million dong, open only to companies that have rented at least twice or signed a contract; five-seaters are guaranteed for corporate accounts from Monday to Thursday outside June to August, and outside that window they book like walk-in customers, so the extra demand lands exactly in the trough. Same-day invoicing is something competitors can copy quickly; end-of-month payment with a credit limit is harder for a small competitor to copy, but that is the scenario's reading, and nothing has tested it
  6. Turn it into a budget, a break-even calculation and a review point: The 240 million consists of 144 million for a year of one employee who handles handovers and looks after corporate accounts, 60 million for e-invoicing software and a booking portal for businesses, and 36 million for visiting companies. At a contribution margin of 550,000 dong per car-day, 437 extra business-traveller car-days are needed to cover it. The target for 2027 is 900 extra car-days on Monday to Thursday outside the summer: about 450 from the 38 regular companies, if the company's share of their rental days rises from about 28% to about 60%, and about 450 from 20 new companies. The part from regular companies alone just clears break-even. If the target is met, the share of car-days rented on off-season weekdays rises to about 46%, there are still enough five-seaters, and the added contribution margin, less the 240 million, leaves about 255 million dong. Review point: on March 31, 2027, count business-traveller car-days on Monday to Thursday in the first quarter, on exactly the definition used for 2026 (contract in a company's name, or a company invoice issued), against the 210 car-days of the first quarter of 2026. Below 356 is slower than the break-even pace, and the second employee has to be discussed again; 510 or more is on the target pace. Achieved: before December 15, the company has a target customer group for the 240 million, a positioning statement for that group and a review point on March 31, 2027, each traceable to real rental data: business travellers were chosen because 65% of their rental days already fall in the trough, not because they give the highest contribution margin; the positioning statement rests on what nine of the twelve regular companies said was most annoying and none of the six competitors asked yet does. The choice rests on four conditions. The 28% share and the figure of 900 rental days are self-reported by 12 companies, then extrapolated to 38; if the real share is larger, there is less room to grow. The 550,000 dong contribution margin is that of current business travellers; end-of-month payment accounts add a cost of capital and a bad-debt risk the calculation does not yet include. Competitors can copy same-day invoicing very quickly. And reserving cars for corporate accounts only stays clear of families as long as it stays outside the three summer months. The analysis does not prove this is the best group; it shows that the fleet's trough has a group of customers already used to filling it, that the group does not respond to price, and it says what to count at the end of the first quarter to know whether the choice is working.

Summary

Key Point: The STP framework works by separating the groups of buyers who respond differently to the same offer, choosing the group the company serves better than anyone else, then putting product, price, sales channel and message behind a position that group recognises. The most counter-intuitive part is that those groups rarely lie where marketers tend to look: in research by Anesbury and colleagues on more than 700 UK consumer packaged goods brands, the buyers of competing brands are almost the same kind of people when measured by demographics and attitudes, and the large differences the authors point out all come with a difference in what the product does. That evidence does not yet cover services or business markets, and within this piece's search, no study has turned up comparing the results of segment-based targeting with mass marketing. Principles: divide by need and buying situation, check differences against real buying behaviour before choosing, use the target group to design the product rather than assuming you must narrow your reach, and position on something buyers can check when they use the product.
