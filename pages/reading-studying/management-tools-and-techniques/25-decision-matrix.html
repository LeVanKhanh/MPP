Decision Matrix

The matrix gets its strength from putting every option through the same sum, far less from precise weights; when two options sit a few tenths of a point apart, the matrix has not yet decided for anyone

Updated: November 19, 2026

Definition

What Is a Decision Matrix

Suppose a family in Hanoi is choosing a school for their child's first year among three places: a public school 700 metres from home, a bilingual private school 6 kilometres away, and a high-performing public school in between. The mother thinks most about the quality of teaching, the father about the school run every morning, both worry about fees, and the child needs somewhere to stay until 5 in the afternoon. Every time they talk it over, the conversation drifts towards whichever school they visited last. A decision matrix puts all four worries on one sheet of paper: one axis holds the options, the other the criteria, and each criterion carries a weight; each option is scored on each criterion, the score is multiplied by the weight, and the products are added into one total per option.

The workflow assessment toolkit of AHRQ, the US government agency for healthcare research and quality, describes six steps: list the criteria; discuss them and keep the most important; give each criterion a weight according to how much it matters in the situation; draw an L-shaped matrix with the criteria and weights along one edge and the options along the other; score each option against each criterion; then multiply and add. The same source lists other names for it: decision grid, prioritization matrix, problem matrix, Pugh matrix. [1]

The weighted version is not tied to an original text or a single author; within this piece's search, no text has turned up that introduced it. The variant that carries a person's name does have a clear origin: the British design engineer Stuart Pugh presented his method for selecting design concepts in the paper "Concept selection: a method that works" at the International Conference on Engineering Design (ICED) in Rome in 1981. [2] As the design research group at the Institute for Manufacturing, University of Cambridge, describes it, the Pugh matrix uses no numbers: the team picks one option as the datum, compares every other option with it on each criterion, marks "+" if better, "−" if worse and "S" if about the same, then counts. A good option held back by one weakness gets improved, an option with nothing worth saving gets dropped, and the whole round repeats with a stronger datum and sharper criteria; hence the method's other name, controlled convergence. [3] This piece uses the weighted version, following AHRQ's steps, for every example and every check, because that is the version people at work meet most often; the Pugh matrix comes in where it differs from it in a way that matters.

The nearest entry in this series is the impact/effort matrix, where many tasks are placed on two axes to decide which to do first. The two tools answer different questions. The impact/effort matrix sorts a long list along two dimensions, impact and effort by default, and adds nothing up; a decision matrix ranks a set of options, most often to pick one of them, on however many criteria the situation calls for, and combines them with weights. Why We Need It

Without a matrix, a meeting to choose a supplier tends to follow the order of appearance. The option presented last is still fresh in everyone's mind, the criterion the loudest person cares about takes up most of the time, and by the time the decision comes the criteria have quietly changed: before the demo everyone says three-year cost matters most, after the demo the question becomes "which software is easier to use". Nobody is trying to get it wrong. Each person is weighing six or seven dimensions at once in their head, and according to the psychologist Robyn Dawes, weighing in the head is easily swayed by context and by irrelevant factors, so the same person does not weigh two options by the same rule. [4]

The tool's mechanism is to separate two jobs that intuitive judgement does in one go: choosing what to look at, and combining what it sees into a conclusion. The matrix leaves the first job to people, choosing the criteria and scoring each cell, then hands the second to a sum that runs identically for every option. Dawes summed up why the split works: people are good at picking out the right variables and coding them in the right direction, but bad at integrating information from diverse and incomparable sources. He cites a 1972 study by Hillel Einhorn: specialist physicians read biopsies from 193 patients with Hodgkin's lymphoma, coded each feature and then gave an overall severity score; their overall scores did not predict how long the patients survived, but the very features they had coded did, once entered into a regression model, a calculation that finds the set of weights that best fits the data already in hand. [4]

The case for the tool traces back to the figures in the research section: in the five studies Dawes and Robert Corrigan pulled together, an equal-weight sum built from exactly the variables the judges had seen predicted better than the judges' own average judgement in all five. [4] A later meta-analysis, comparing clinical judgement with combining data by formula in studies of health and human behaviour, found the formula about 10% more accurate on average, clearly ahead in 33% to 47% of the studies, while clinical judgement was clearly ahead in 6% to 16%. [5] Both are evidence about prediction, where there is a measurable answer to compare against; the research section spells out how far that carries the decision matrix.

What Are Its Limits

The matrix does not produce options or criteria, and that is its first built-in limit. It only ranks what is put into it; the best option nobody thought of has no row to win in. The Cambridge group writes of the Pugh matrix that the process is only as effective as the team's input, its choice of criteria and the quality of its initial ideas allow. [3]

The second built-in limit lies in the sum itself: it compensates. A high score in one place makes up for a low score in another, so an option that fails a requirement it cannot do without can still come out on top on the strength of the other criteria. None of the six steps AHRQ describes screens such options out beforehand [1]; anyone who wants that has to add it themselves, as a filter run before scoring. That is also why AHRQ notes that the highest-scoring option is not necessarily the right choice, and that the scores are there to guide the team's discussion towards consensus. [1]

The third built-in limit: weights and scores are judgements, and they bend to the way the question is asked. AHRQ says plainly that the tool requires assumptions, especially in the weights and the scoring. [1] Two biases have been measured. Martin Weber, Franz Eisenführ and Detlof von Winterfeldt had participants set weights on objective trees in which the same three objectives were described with three, four, five or six attributes, and found that the part described in more detail received markedly more weight than the part described briefly, across several different weighting techniques (read through the abstract). [6] Splitting "ease of use" into four rows is enough to make it heavier. Rüdiger von Nitzsch and Martin Weber showed the second bias: in theory a criterion's weight should be larger when the options differ more on it, but when the range changed, participants adjusted their weights less than the theory requires (read through the abstract). [7] A 30% weight on price means very different things when the quotes differ by 3% and when they differ by 40%. One more point, in this piece's reading: a 1-to-5 scale is ordinal, and multiplying and adding quietly assumes the step from 4 to 5 is the same size as the step from 1 to 2. On a scale with no written anchors, nobody checks that assumption.

The remaining failures lie in how the tool is used. The most common is filling in the matrix after the choice has been made, then adjusting criteria and weights until the favoured option comes out on top; at that point the matrix is a justification, no longer a selection. The second is reading a small gap as a win, like the 0.2 points in Figure 1. The fixes belong to the How to Apply It section.

Examples

The two examples below use the same weighted sum in opposite directions: a matrix for choosing warehouse management software, built after the product demonstration and adjusted until the favoured option came out on top, and the way the Denver police chose a new bullet in the mid-1970s by separating the question of values from the technical question.

Four "Ease of Use" Rows and the Expired Stock

An illustrative scenario: a medical equipment distributor needs to replace its warehouse management software and invites two vendors, X and Y, to present. X's presentation is polished, comes with a mobile app, and the director leaves the meeting room clearly taken with it. The next day the warehouse manager is asked to build a decision matrix. The first version has four criteria: three-year cost 30%, warehouse features (tracking batch numbers and expiry dates) 30%, integration with the accounting software 20%, ease of use 20%. X scores 3, 3, 4, 5 for a total of 3.6; Y scores 4, 5, 3, 3 for a total of 3.9. At the review meeting someone says "ease of use" is too vague, so it is split into four rows, interface, mobile app, drag-and-drop reports and Vietnamese language support, at 10% each, and the other three criteria are cut to 25%, 20% and 15% to keep the total at 100. X scores 5, 5, 4, 5 on the four new rows, Y scores 3, 2, 3, 5. The new totals: X 3.85, Y 3.75. The company buys X. Six months later the warehouse staff still keep a separate spreadsheet to track expiry dates, because X records the expiry date only in a notes field and gives no warning when a batch is about to expire.

The matrix has the full shape, and fails in three places its mechanism should have guarded. It was built after someone had already chosen, so every change to the criteria had a preferred direction. Splitting one criterion into four raised the share of "ease of use" from 20% to 40% without anyone deciding that ease of use now mattered twice as much, exactly the splitting bias Weber and colleagues measured. [6] And for a medical equipment distributor, tracking batches and expiry dates is a requirement it cannot do without, so it had to be a filter run first, not a row for interface scores to make up for.

Done according to the mechanism, the criteria, the must-have thresholds and the weights are fixed before the first presentation and written into the minutes, and every later proposal to split or merge criteria has to come with an answer to one question: does the total weight of this group change, and who decides to change it? Try it with your most recent matrix: cover the option names and leave only the criteria and weights. If an outsider reading the criteria can guess which option wins, the criteria were written to fit the option.

Three Dimensions, Equal Weights: How Denver Chose a Bullet

In 1974 the Denver Police Department, like many American police departments at the time, decided to change its handgun ammunition. The main reason the department gave was that the conventional round-nosed bullet lacked "stopping effectiveness", the ability to incapacitate the person shot so that they cannot fire back. The chief proposed replacing it with a hollow-point bullet, the kind that flattens on impact, penetrating less and ricocheting less. The proposal was opposed by the American Civil Liberties Union, minority groups and many others, who argued that the new bullet was no different from the banned "dum-dum" and caused far more severe injuries. Both sides held their ground, public hearings followed, and both went looking for ballistics experts to back them. According to Kenneth Hammond and Leonard Adelman, who studied the decision and took part in it, the parties argued over specific bullets, mixing the physical effects of a bullet with the policy question: policymakers were unwittingly acting as ballistics experts, and ballistics experts as policymakers. [8] [4]

Hammond and Adelman pulled the two questions apart. From the policymakers they drew out the dimensions that mattered: stopping effectiveness (the probability that someone hit in the torso cannot fire back), the probability of serious injury, and the probability of harm to bystanders. Then they had the ballistics experts rate each bullet on those three dimensions. The last two dimensions largely moved together but did not quite coincide with the first: risk to bystanders goes with penetration, while stopping effectiveness goes with the width of the entry wound. Since the policymakers could not agree on weights, the two authors proposed weighting the three dimensions equally. Adding up the experts' ratings with equal weights, they found a bullet with greater stopping effectiveness, less likely to cause injury and less of a threat to bystanders than the one the department was using, and also less injurious than the one it had proposed. That bullet was accepted by the city council and all the parties involved, and, according to the 1976 paper, was in use by the department. Dawes adds a detail in a footnote: only eight bullets were not dominated by some other bullet on both stopping effectiveness and probability of harm, so any weighting would have picked one of those eight. [4]

This piece reads the case through Dawes's account and the passages he quotes verbatim from Hammond and Adelman's 1976 paper in Science; the source calls the method a linear model with equal weights, which is exactly the structure of a decision matrix: options, criteria, weights, scores, total. No comparison tells us how the new bullet fared in the field. The case is worth reading for three moves: the question "what should matter" went to the people with authority over values, the question "which bullet does what" went to the people with the expertise, and the sum only joined the two. The footnote about the eight bullets shows that most of the choosing was already done in the experts' ratings: the weights only decided among eight. And when nobody agreed on weights, equal weights were enough to reach a choice every side accepted.

Some Research on the Decision Matrix

The decision matrix appears in the quality toolkits of many fields, from AHRQ's healthcare workflow toolkit [1] to engineering design textbooks, but the evidence for its own value is far thinner than that popularity suggests. Within this piece's search, no study has turned up that compares the outcomes of organisations choosing with a decision matrix against those that do not. The Pugh variant has one model-based evaluation: Daniel Frey and colleagues built computer models simulating a team going through repeated rounds of evaluation, idea generation and investigation, and found that Pugh's method, over a fairly wide range of assumptions, produced better design outcomes than the alternative processes compared (read through the abstract). [9] That is a result from simulation, not from real design teams. The thickest experimental evidence sits one level up, at the underlying question the matrix stands on: does combining separately scored items with a fixed sum beat the overall judgement of the very people who scored them? The meta-analysis by William Grove and colleagues mentioned above answers that for prediction in health and behaviour. [5] The study below asks a further question that matrix users care about more: how precise do the weights have to be?

The study: In "The Robust Beauty of Improper Linear Models in Decision Making", published in American Psychologist in 1979, Robyn Dawes set out again five studies that he and Robert Corrigan had pulled together in 1974. [4] In each study, a group of judges predicted a measurable outcome from the same set of variables: 29 clinical psychologists classified 861 patients as psychotic or neurotic from their MMPI profiles (a personality inventory that yields 11 scores); 80 graduate students at the University of Illinois and 41 at the University of Oregon predicted the first-year grade point average of 90 Illinois psychology graduate students from 10 variables; the admissions committee predicted later faculty ratings of 111 Oregon graduate students admitted from 1964 to 1967, from GRE scores (the standardised graduate admissions test in the US), undergraduate grade point average and the selectivity of their undergraduate institution; and an experiment had people estimate the value of ellipses from their size, eccentricity and greyness. For each study, Dawes set the accuracy (the correlation between prediction and actual outcome) of the judges' average judgement beside the accuracy of four ways of adding: a model derived from the judges' own judgements, a model with random weights (ten thousand randomly chosen sets of weights, keeping only the right sign for each variable), an equal-weight model, and a regression model with optimal weights, including a version cross-validated on new data. Equal weights only make sense once the variables have been put on the same standardised scale.

Findings: The judges' average judgement reached 0.28, 0.33, 0.37, 0.19 and 0.84 in the five studies in the order above; the equal-weight model reached 0.34, 0.60, 0.60, 0.48 and 0.97, higher than the judges in all five. The random-weight model, on average, reached 0.30, 0.51, 0.51, 0.39 and 0.84, level with or above the judges. In the Oregon admissions case, the equal-weight sum of the three variables correlated 0.48 with later faculty ratings, against 0.19 for the admissions committee (only 23 of the 111 students could be compared, because the committee's rating scale changed over the years) and 0.38 for the cross-validated regression model. In the four studies with a cross-validated version, equal weights beat the cross-validated regression in three; the exception was the psychosis case, where cross-validated regression reached 0.46. Optimal weights, a ceiling computed on the same data used to estimate them, reached 0.46, 0.69, 0.69, 0.54 and 0.97. [4]

What it shows: When there is a measurable outcome to predict and each variable points in the right direction, a fixed sum of the very variables the judges use predicts better than the judges' overall judgement, even when the weights are equal or chosen at random. Dawes explains this by a property of linear models: the result is insensitive to departures from the optimal weights, so roughly right weights give nearly the same ranking. He cites the closing line of Dawes and Corrigan, to the effect that the whole trick lies in knowing which variables to look at, and then knowing how to add. [4] This study is about predicting a measurable quantity. It does not measure choosing between options where "better" depends on the chooser's values, which is what decision matrices are most often used for, and it assumes the variables have been chosen correctly, which is exactly the part of the job the matrix leaves to people.

The counter-case: Defenders of the matrix would reply with two points. First: the matrix does not promise to hit a correct answer; it promises to bring the trade-offs into view, to put every option through the same rule, and to leave a trail others can check. That value does not need a measurable outcome to stand, and Dawes himself proposed using additive models where there is no measurable criterion, such as admitting graduate students towards a long-term goal that has not been precisely defined. [4] Second: the experiments showing that analysis spoils choices, such as those by Timothy Wilson and Jonathan Schooler, looked at people with no criteria fixed in advance; the matrix is exactly what prevents that. This piece accepts the first point, on one condition: when the measurable answer is dropped, so is the promise of "choosing better" borrowed from Dawes's figures; what remains is consistency and checkability, worth having but smaller. The Denver case shows what that value looks like: a choice every side accepted, not a choice proven to be the best. The second point this piece accepts only by half. In Wilson and Schooler's second study, 243 students chose courses; the group asked to evaluate every attribute of every course, the condition closest to a matrix in the experiment, made choices that agreed less with expert opinion than the control group's, and so did the group asked to write down reasons; the authors suggest that evaluating many attributes may blur the differences between options, so that choosers tell them apart less well (read through the abstract). [10] That group had no weights fixed in advance, so this is not yet a test of the matrix. But the failure the authors name, scoring many attributes making options look more alike, does not vanish once weights are added; in a matrix it shows up as totals so close together that they cannot be told apart. On balance: the evidence strongly supports combining by a fixed rule in place of weighing in the head, weakly supports fine-tuning the weights, and there is as yet no controlled measurement showing the matrix picking a better option when "better" is a matter of values.

Why this is useful: It changes where you spend time in your next meeting to choose between options. Before arguing over weights, run the matrix twice: once with the weights the group proposed, once with all weights equal. If the ranking does not change, the argument over weights decides nothing; spend that time checking whether the criteria are complete and whether each score has grounds. If the ranking does change, the decision turns on exactly the weight that changes it; name that weight and decide it in the open. The question to ask yourself before signing off: what made this option win, the well-grounded cells or the weight that was just adjusted?

How to Apply It

A decision matrix works by separating the choice of what to look at from the combining of what is seen, then handing the combining to a sum that runs identically for every option, so using it well means keeping those two jobs genuinely apart: fix the criteria and weights before knowing which option wins, keep the requirements that cannot be traded off outside the sum, and read the result to find what the decision really turns on. The tool repays the effort most when there are a few mutually exclusive options and many criteria pulling in different directions, and least when a single criterion is enough to decide.

  • Fix the criteria and must-have thresholds before seeing the options, and the weights before seeing the totals: A sum only guards against lopsided weighing in the head when the rule for adding comes before the result; a matrix built after the presentation, as in the warehouse software case, only records a choice already made. Something you can do straight away: the person compiling the scores keeps the option names, and the people setting the weights see only the range on each criterion ("cost from 92 to 118 million dong a month"), without knowing which option each end belongs to. Every later proposal to change the criteria goes into the minutes with its reason
  • Filter by must-have requirements and remove dominated options before scoring: Because the sum compensates, any requirement that cannot be missing has to be a pass-or-fail threshold run first, not a weighted row. Then drop any option that another option equals or beats on every criterion: in Denver, only eight bullets were not dominated by another, so every weighting would choose among those eight [4]. Before discarding the rejected group for good, ask once more: which of those thresholds is actually negotiable?
  • Set weights by the range between options, and merge overlapping criteria: Ask in terms of jumps: how much is it worth to go from the most expensive quote to the cheapest among those on the table, compared with going from the worst option to the best on the other criterion? The question "how important is price" cannot be answered without knowing how far apart the quotes are. People adjust weights too little when the range changes [7], so the question has to be put directly in terms of the range; a criterion on which every option is the same gets dropped, because it only dilutes the rest. A criterion described in more detail grows heavier on its own [6], so rows that measure the same thing (rent, service charge, fit-out cost) merge into one figure, and the number of criteria stays at the level where the options can still be told apart
  • Rerun with equal weights and with each weight changed, and treat any gap smaller than that change as a tie: Dawes's results show that roughly right weights usually give nearly the same ranking [4], so the obvious approach, spending a whole meeting haggling over each percentage point, rarely changes the result. What to look for is the tipping point: which weight has to change, and by how much, for the second option to pull ahead. This fails when two options are so close that every small change reverses the order; at that point the matrix has finished its job, which is to expose exactly one trade-off that the person with authority has to decide in words, and no sum can decide it for them

Applying It to a Scenario

An accounting services firm in Hanoi has 42 staff, 40 of whom come to work by motorbike. Its current office lease ends on March 31, 2027; the landlord has already let the space to someone else from April 1 and will not renew. In December 2026 the admin team finds five premises, called A to E. What the firm needs before January 15, 2027: to choose one to sign a lease for, in such a way that every score in the choice traces back to a measurement or a document, and the three partners know how far their weights would have to shift before the choice changes. The lowest row is A, at 4.06: being 20 million a month cheaper than C does not make up for the longest commute and only one meeting room. The choice rests on four conditions. Commute time is a figure from a map app for one Tuesday morning, and the average hides the 3 people at C who still travel over 45 minutes. The scale runs between the worst and the best of the three options, so C's 0 on cost means the most expensive of the three, not too expensive; if a fourth place widened the range, the anchors and weights would have to be set again, not just a column added. The weights are the judgement of the three partners, not of the staff, who pay most of the price of commuting time. And C's lease hands over on the last day the threshold allows, so a week's delay eats the fit-out buffer. The sum does not prove C is the best place; it shows that C wins under weights fixed before anyone knew the totals, and that the real decision sits in exactly one comparison: 26 million dong a month in exchange for commuting time, a meeting room and 20 parking spaces.

Summary

Key Point: A decision matrix works by separating the choice of what to look at from the combining of what is seen, giving the first job to people and the second to a sum that runs identically for every option. The most counter-intuitive part is that, at least in prediction, weights matter less than people think: in the five studies Dawes and Corrigan pulled together, an equal-weight sum predicted better than the judges' own average judgement in all five, as with 0.48 against 0.19 in the Oregon admissions case. That evidence concerns predicting a measurable outcome; within this piece's search, no controlled measurement has turned up showing that the matrix picks a better option when "better" depends on the chooser's values. The principles: fix the criteria and weights before knowing who wins, keep must-have requirements outside the sum, set weights by the range between options, and read the result by finding the tipping point instead of trusting a few tenths of a point.
