Impact/Effort Matrix

Both axes are estimates, and effort is usually underestimated; finishing many easy tasks looks like progress while the work that creates most of the impact sits untouched in the major-projects quadrant

Published: November 19, 2026

Definition

What Is an Impact/Effort Matrix

After a month with 140 complaints, suppose the six staff of an online cosmetics shop sit down and collect eight ideas: fix the lipstick swatches on the product page because the photos show the wrong colour, send an automatic message when an order runs late, switch courier, use crush-proof packaging, build a dedicated mobile app, add an auto-reply chatbot, film how-to videos, and redesign the logo. Six people cannot do all eight next month. An impact/effort matrix places each idea on a plane with two axes: the vertical axis is estimated impact, here the number of complaints removed per month, and the horizontal axis is estimated effort, here the number of person-days (one person working a full day) until the idea is running. Two lines through the midpoint of each axis divide the plane into four quadrants, and the top-left one, high impact for low effort, is where you start.

The tool page of NHS Education for Scotland, the education body of Scotland's public health service, calls it a prioritisation matrix and describes the four quadrants in turn: high impact, low effort is "do all of these", the highest priority; high impact, high effort is "do these with support", because they need more planning and resources; low impact, low effort is "do some of these"; low impact, high effort is "do less or none of these". The team writes each idea on its own note, places the notes on the matrix together according to where each sits relative to the others, then chooses what to test first and revisits the order regularly. The same page says the axes can be adapted to suit the decision being made, that the matrix does not replace professional judgement, and advises against letting every idea pile into the top-priority quadrant. [1] The Alberta Medical Association in Canada calls the same tool an impact-effort chart, also known as a PICK chart, and says that high-impact, low-effort changes should be implemented first. [2]

The tool is not tied to an original text or an author; within this piece's search, no document that introduced it has turned up. One variant was published with data: in 1997, Joachim Karlsson and Kevin Ryan described a cost-value diagram for prioritising software requirements, with each requirement's percentage of value to customers on the vertical axis and its percentage of implementation cost on the horizontal, both estimated by comparing requirements in pairs. Instead of four quadrants, the two authors split the diagram into three areas by the ratio of value to cost: above 2, between 0.5 and 2, and below 0.5. [3] This piece treats that diagram as the value-and-cost variant of the same tool, which is this piece's reading: the same two questions, what does it gain and what does it cost, asked with different measures and divided into different areas. The examples and checking questions here use the four-quadrant version as NHS Education for Scotland describes it; Karlsson and Ryan's diagram comes in where splitting by ratio shows something the four quadrants hide.

The tool it is most easily confused with is the decision matrix, where a few options are scored on several weighted criteria and the scores added up. The difference lies in how things are combined. According to AHRQ, the US government's agency for healthcare quality research, a decision matrix ranks and evaluates a set of options against a weighted list of criteria, and is most often used when one of several options has to be chosen; all the criteria are folded into a single total. [4] An impact/effort matrix lays a long list out along two dimensions, impact and effort by default, adds nothing up, and keeps the two dimensions apart so that the trade-off stays visible. What it produces is a set of priority groups, not a detailed order among the tasks inside each group. Placing the cosmetics shop's eight ideas on the matrix gives Figure 1.

Why We Need It

A team with twenty things it wants to do and capacity for five can easily choose without making any comparison at all. What gets picked may be whatever is easiest to start, whatever the boss mentioned last, or whatever came up at the most recent meeting; under heavy workload, the pull towards the easy option grows stronger. Diwas KC and colleagues saw this in a hospital emergency department in the United States: each time a physician took on one more patient, the odds of picking up an easy case next rose by about 8%, odds here meaning the ratio between the chance of choosing an easy case and the chance of choosing a hard one; in the same team's experiment, 76.0% of people under high workload chose the easy version of a task, against 63.7% under low workload. [5] Easy work is not the problem. The problem starts when effort becomes the only question anyone answers.

The tool's mechanism is to make every task answer two questions separately, what does it gain and what does it cost, and then to lay all the tasks side by side on one plane, so that each is seen by both its gain and its cost. With effort always standing next to impact, an easy task has to show it is worth doing, while a large project is seen for what it would deliver instead of being dismissed for being heavy. Because the output is the relative position of the notes, the tool suits a discussion around a wall of sticky notes, where people argue about whether this note belongs above or below that one, and that argument is the moment when the estimates get tested. [1]

Two figures, from the Examples and the Research sections, show the cost of choosing without a comparison. In the Ericsson project that Karlsson and Ryan describe, the value and the cost of individual requirements differed by a factor of about 20: the four most valuable of the 14 requirements carried 61% of the total value, and the four least valuable carried 8%. [3] When the gains are packed into a few tasks like that, getting the order wrong costs a great deal. And in the emergency department, picking up more easy cases on busy shifts went with more patients seen per shift, but also with a lower volume of work once complexity was taken into account. [5] On this piece's reading, at the moment of choosing, the impact axis is what stops that count from running the decision.

What Are Its Limits

Both axes are estimates, and the matrix checks neither of them; this limit is built in. A wrong estimate sits just as neatly in its quadrant, looking exactly as certain as a right one. Effort is the axis whose bias has been measured. Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin and Michael Ross asked 37 final-year psychology students at the University of Waterloo, Canada, when they would hand in their honours thesis: on average they predicted 33.9 days, it actually took 55.5, and fewer than a third (29.7%) finished within their own prediction. Even their predictions for the case where "everything went as poorly as it possibly could", 48.6 days on average, were still shorter than the real time, although that difference was not statistically significant. [6] The pattern is known as the planning fallacy. The impact axis is an estimate too: at Ericsson, each requirement's share of value was estimated in advance by a group of project members standing in for customers, and nobody measured it again once the software was in use. [3]

The second limit is also built in, on this piece's reading: the four quadrants throw away ratio and size. Two tasks in the same quadrant can differ tenfold in impact per person-day, and a task sitting close to a dividing line only needs a slightly different estimate to change its label. The midpoint lines measure each axis separately, so they do not answer the question people choosing tasks usually need answered most, how much is gained for each unit spent; Karlsson and Ryan's diagram splits its areas by the ratio of value to cost, and so keeps that question in view. [3] Figure 1 shows two places where the quadrants and the ratio line disagree. But reading by ratio is still only a way of ordering, not a way of choosing a bundle: by this piece's inference, when there is a hard budget and a minimum level of impact that must be reached, a better bundle can include a task with a low ratio, because that task fills exactly the budget that is left; the Scenario section has a case like that.

Third, and also built in: the matrix looks at each task as though it stood alone. Karlsson and Ryan note that their method takes no account of dependencies between requirements: to implement a low-cost, high-value requirement, you may have to implement a high-cost, low-value one as well. [3] Overlap works the same way: when two ideas target the same cause, their impacts do not add up, because whichever is done second only has what the first left behind. The default four-quadrant template also has no place for deadlines, money or risk, and the effort axis usually counts only one-off implementation effort. A task that takes one person-day to start but adds a phone call to every order looks cheap on the axis and turns out expensive to run; recurring running costs, discounts or software fees have to be written next to the note and counted across the whole period under consideration.

The rest are faults in how the tool is used: leaving "impact" without a unit, so that everyone sees their own idea as high-impact and the "do all of these" quadrant fills up, which NHS Education for Scotland advises against [1]; estimating effort from the plan in one's head; and reporting progress as the number of tasks finished. The How to Apply It section deals with each.

Examples

An invented tour operator and two real software projects at Ericsson ask the same pair of questions, what does it gain and what does it cost, but read the answers in two ways: the tour operator reads its matrix as a to-do list and counts what has been finished, while at Ericsson value and cost were estimated by comparing requirements in pairs and read by ratio.

Eight Tasks Done, One Parked for Two Quarters

An illustrative scenario: a tour operator in Hue with 30 staff gets back its first-quarter customer survey, with an average satisfaction score of 7.1 out of 10. The directors meet and collect 18 ideas on sticky notes. Everyone places their own idea on the matrix; "impact" is taken to mean how many customers complain about the thing, and effort is estimated by whoever proposed the idea. Nine notes end up in "do all of these". The idea "enough guides for the peak season", meaning recruiting and training six more freelance guides, lands in the quadrant that needs support and is pushed to the following quarter. At the end of the second quarter, the report says eight of the nine priority tasks are done, including the switch to new tour-booking software, estimated at three days, which took five weeks. Satisfaction in the second quarter is 7.0. Customer comments still circle the same complaint: one guide for 40 people, and every question means waiting. The third quarter repeats the pattern, with a fresh "do all of these" list.

The four quadrants are all there, but three things the mechanism needs are missing. Impact has no unit, so each person places their own note by feel and "do all of these" swells. Effort is estimated by the person proposing the idea, from the plan in their head rather than from previous jobs, exactly the bias Buehler and colleagues measured. [6] And the report counts tasks finished, the very count the emergency department data show to be misleading. [5] The one task aimed at the most repeated complaint is the one that keeps being postponed, because it sits in the heavy quadrant.

Those three faults match three principles in the How to Apply It section. For this company alone, measuring impact as the share of negative comments about guides, then adding up the estimated impact of the whole "do all of these" quadrant before fixing the quarter's list, would be enough to show that the guide-recruitment project cannot wait. A check for your last quarterly report: is the number at the top a count of tasks done, or an outcome a customer could see?

Fourteen Requirements, Ninety-One Pairs: Ericsson in 1994

In January 1994, under a joint research programme between Ericsson Radio Systems AB and the Department of Computer and Information Science at Linköping University, Karlsson and Ryan were invited into the RAN project, which was building a system to give managers information on the operation of a mobile telephony network, such as capacity, coverage and quality. The project had 14 high-level requirements. The prioritisation method in use at the time was to rank each requirement from 1 to 3; category 1 requirements were implemented and the rest dropped or postponed to later releases, and the two authors had already found this far from optimal. A group of experienced project members, standing in for customers, compared 91 pairs of requirements by value, then in a separate session by estimated cost; the whole process took about an hour. The consistency ratio of the comparisons, a measure of how far the judgements contradict each other, was 0.18 for both value and cost, above the 0.10 that the general rule the authors cite treats as acceptable. [3]

The results showed value and cost differing by a factor of about 20. The four most valuable requirements carried 61% of the total value and the four least valuable 8%; requirement 13 was worth about 20 times as much as requirement 11. The four most expensive requirements made up 56% of the total cost, and requirement 13 also cost about 20 times as much as requirement 1. Split by the ratio of value to cost, three requirements fell into the high-ratio area, five, including number 13, into the medium area, and six into the low area. The authors worked through an example: dropping requirements 10, 11 and 12 would keep 94% of the maximum value at 78% of the cost. In the second project, PMR, a system for recording and analysing mobile traffic that was developing its fourth release, each member made 55 comparisons per criterion in slightly over 30 minutes; of the 11 new functional requirements, two fell into the high-ratio area, six into the medium and three into the low, and choosing the first two areas would deliver 95% of the value at 75% of the cost. [3]

The source calls the tool a cost-value diagram; it belongs here because it places each requirement on exactly the two axes of gain and cost, with only the measuring and the dividing done differently. Three things carry over to anyone using the matrix. Comparing in pairs forces the estimator to set each requirement against every other one; on this piece's reading, that is what a sticky-note matrix does more loosely. Requirement 13, the most valuable and also the most expensive, did not end up among those dropped, because its ratio still sat in the medium area; a line drawn on cost alone would have pushed it to the heavy end. And reading by ratio gives a manager a figure to weigh, such as 94% of the value for 78% of the cost, rather than just a label. The limits of the case sit in the same place: the 94% and 95% figures are calculated from the very percentages that were estimated, not measured after the software was in use; a consistency ratio of 0.18 shows the judgements still contradicted each other; the article is a report by the people who built the method, with no comparison against the old 1-to-3 ranking; and the authors themselves note that the method ignores dependencies, that comparing in pairs is tedious, and that the number of comparisons grows with the square of the number of requirements. [3]

Some Research on the Impact/Effort Matrix

Three healthcare organisations in Scotland and Canada include this tool in their quality improvement material: NHS Education for Scotland [1], the Alberta Medical Association [2], and Healthcare Improvement Scotland, where a project summary describes a general practice on the west coast of Scotland, aiming to cut missed appointments by 10% by September 2025, using the matrix to pick a "low effort, high impact" idea to test first. [7] None of the three reports a comparison of outcomes. Within this piece's search, no study has turned up that compares the outcomes of teams choosing work with an impact/effort matrix against teams that do not. The evidence closest to the tool is the Ericsson case above: a report by the people who built the method, with figures calculated from estimates. [3] The thicker evidence sits one level down, on each axis. On the effort axis, beyond the Waterloo result, Buehler and colleagues found that the optimism in predictions disappeared when people were asked to connect relevant past experiences with the task they were predicting, and that observers predicting someone else's completion time overestimated it. [6] The study below asks a question closer to the "do all of these" quadrant: when people choose their own next task under heavy load, what do they pick, and which way of counting hides the price of that choice?

The study: Diwas KC, Bradley Staats, Maryam Kouchaki and Francesca Gino took six years of data, more than 230,000 patient encounters, from the medium-sized emergency department of a large metropolitan hospital on the US East Coast; the results appeared in Management Science in 2020. [5] In this department, physicians pick their next patient from a computer queue after a triage nurse has rated urgency on the ESI scale from 1 (most severe) to 5. The authors treated levels 1 and 2 as difficult cases and levels 3 to 5 as easy ones, which made up 50.3% of patients. They reconstructed the queue and each physician's current load at every moment a patient was picked up, so they saw the same information the physician saw when choosing. Productivity per shift was measured two ways: the number of patients seen, and the total of RVUs (relative value units), units used to set physician pay that reflect how complex the work is. Over the longer term, the team followed each physician to see how the number of hard and easy cases they had handled related to their later speed. They then ran an experiment with 365 adults recruited on Amazon MTurk, an online platform for hiring people for small tasks, randomly assigned to two groups: the high-workload group copied a page of text while counting how often five words occurred in a song, the low-workload group only copied; each person then chose an easy or a hard version of a word-building task and answered questions on their sense of progress, fatigue and stress.

Findings: Each additional patient in a physician's current load raised the odds of picking up an easy case next by about 8%; each patient the physician had just discharged during the shift, a measure of fatigue, went with a rise of about 5%. On busy shifts, a higher share of easy cases went with more patients seen, but also with a lower total of RVUs for the shift. More hard cases handled went with faster service and more RVUs per patient later on. In the experiment, 76.0% of people in the high-workload group (133 of 175) chose the easy version, against 63.7% in the low-workload group (121 of 190), a statistically significant difference (p = 0.011); a weaker sense of progress and greater fatigue explained part of that difference, while stress did not. [5]

What it shows: Under heavy load, people lean towards easy tasks, partly because finishing something brings a sense of progress; and if productivity is counted as tasks completed, that lean looks like rising productivity, while the volume of work adjusted for complexity is in fact lower. RVUs are units for paying physicians according to how complex a visit is; they do not measure quality of care or patient outcomes, so "fewer RVUs" means less complex work, not necessarily less value. The emergency department data are observational and come from a single hospital; the experiment replicated only the choice, not the productivity result. The study did not test the impact/effort matrix. On this piece's reading, it measures exactly the kind of choice the effort axis invites when the impact axis is left empty.

The counter-case: Defenders of the matrix have two arguments worth hearing. The first: the quick tasks in "do all of these" are chosen deliberately to build momentum and learn fast; NHS Education for Scotland calls them useful starting points that may offer quick learning opportunities [1], and KC and colleagues themselves measured that finishing tasks creates a sense of progress, then suggested breaking hard tasks into smaller pieces with clear completion points so that people get to finish things more often. [5] The second: a physician picking a patient in the middle of a busy shift is not a team planning a quarter, and the matrix has an impact axis precisely so that nobody chooses by ease alone. This piece accepts most of the second point: the study is a warning about a mechanism, not a test of the tool, and the impact axis really is the counterweight. But on this piece's reading, the counterweight only works when impact has a unit; when "high impact" is just a feeling, effort becomes the only axis with a number on it, and the counterweight stops working. On the first point, this piece accepts that the sense of progress is real, since the study measured it, but that is also why counting finished tasks misleads: in the emergency department data, the feeling of getting a lot done and the volume of work adjusted for complexity moved in opposite directions. Building momentum with quick tasks is fine, as long as the report counts outcomes and not notes. And the advice to break hard tasks into smaller pieces is a way to keep the big project moving; it is no licence to replace that project with other small tasks. Weighing the two sides, the evidence strongly supports that people lean towards easy tasks under heavy load and that counting finished tasks hides complexity, strongly supports that people's estimates of effort for their own work tend to be optimistic, and offers no controlled measurement showing whether the matrix helps or harms results.

Why this is useful: The number at the top of a progress report should change. Before fixing a task list from the matrix, add up the estimated impact of every note in "do all of these" and compare the total with the target; if it falls short, either a task from the major-projects quadrant has to start now, or the target has to be renegotiated at the start of the period, not at reporting time. And in the end-of-period report, put the outcome measure on the first line and the number of tasks finished below it. One question worth asking before every task-picking session: if everything in "do all of these" gets done, does the target number get there?

How to Apply It

An impact/effort matrix works by making each task answer two questions separately, what does it gain and what does it cost, then laying all the tasks side by side on one plane so that each is seen by both its gain and its cost, so the way to use it well is to make the two answers comparable: measure both axes in agreed units, correct effort estimates with the past, read by ratio and size rather than by quadrant alone, and check the estimates against measured results. The tool fits best when there are many independent tasks and limited resources, and worst when the tasks depend tightly on each other or a deadline has already fixed the order.

  • Agree the units of both axes before the first note goes up: Impact is the change in one measure tied to the goal, such as complaints removed per month, estimated from data where data exist; effort is person-days until the task is running. Without units, effort easily becomes the only axis with a concrete shape, and the choice can lean towards easy tasks in exactly the direction KC and colleagues measured under heavy load [5]. Money, deadlines and risk go next to the note, because the default template has no axis for them
  • Estimate effort from the last similar job, not from the plan in your head: People estimating time for their own work tend to be optimistic because they look at the plan rather than the past, and that optimism disappears when they are asked to connect relevant past experiences with what they are estimating [6]. Changing who estimates does not fix this on its own: observers predicting for others draw more on the past but overestimate [6]. The obvious fix, telling everyone to "be pessimistic", does not work either: at Waterloo, worst-case predictions were still shorter than reality, and their absolute error was no smaller than that of ordinary predictions [6]. The right question is "how long did the last job like this take", with that figure written right next to the note. For work nobody has done before, use a comparable job from another team or another company as the anchor; if you give a range, tie the upper end to how far previous jobs overran, not to an imagined worst case
  • Read by ratio and size, and net out overlap before adding: For a note close to a dividing line, ask for its ratio of impact to effort, the way Karlsson and Ryan split their areas by ratio [3]. When two tasks target the same cause, count the second one's impact only on what remains after the first. Then add up the estimated impact of "do all of these" and compare it with the target; if it falls short, choose the major project with the best ratio and start it alongside the quick tasks rather than after them, and if no project can close the gap, renegotiate the target at the start of the period. When the budget is tight, try a few bundles instead of simply following the ratio order, because a low-ratio task may be the one that exactly fills the gap
  • Report with an outcome measure taken before and after, then move the notes to match reality: The number of tasks finished can rise while the volume of work adjusted for complexity falls, as in the emergency department [5]. For each task done, set the estimated impact and effort next to the real figures, then move the remaining notes in light of what was learned; the matrix is a hypothesis, and hypotheses get revised against the numbers. This breaks down when the outcome measure shifts for other reasons, such as seasons or promotions; check one more measure that no task targeted, which should stay flat. To separate the share of each task, switch tasks on at different times by route, team or week, so there is always a group that has not changed to compare against

Applying It to a Scenario

Every week, a distributor in Can Tho sends 12 vehicles out with about 2,000 orders of 20-litre bottled water to homes and offices. In the four weeks from 8 March to 4 April 2027 there were 8,000 orders and 640 failed deliveries, meaning the vehicle arrived but could not deliver and had to come back or cancel: a rate of 8.0%. Each failure costs a return trip and often a customer. The goal: in the first full four-week measurement period after the changes, ending before 30 June 2027, a failed-delivery rate below 4.0%, calculated with the same definition as before on that period's own orders. The dispatch lead, two call-centre staff and the warehouse manager have about 25 person-days for this in April. All figures are illustrative.

Four conditions come with it. The margin is very thin: just two failures under the line, eight fewer failures removed than estimated, and it took both a task from the quadrant that needs support and a low-ratio task, because "do all of these" alone was only estimated to reach 4.8%. It is a before-and-after comparison with no control group; cancellations staying flat and similar order numbers suggest the measurement period was not off overall, but do not prove that each reduction came from the task aimed at it, and separating those shares would need the tasks switched on at different times by route, whereas here only the evening slot ran first on two routes, too briefly to compare. It depends on the evening slot continuing: if the allowance agreement with drivers does not hold, most of the 63 failures estimated for C will come back. And effort overran the roughly 25 person-days available by four, mostly on the evening slot, a task nobody had done before and so had no previous job to sit its estimate beside. The five-task bundle is not necessarily the best one, and the matrix does not claim it is. What the matrix showed is that the bundle was enough to reach the goal on estimates with overlap netted out; that the cheapest task on the effort axis, the call ten minutes ahead, was the most expensive to run; and that the thing that looked biggest on paper, the routing software, had mostly been done already by three cheaper tasks.

Summary

Key Point: Two questions asked separately, what does it gain and what does it cost, then every task laid side by side so that each is seen by both its gain and its cost: that is the whole mechanism of an impact/effort matrix, and both answers are estimates. The most counter-intuitive point is that finishing many easy tasks can look like rising productivity: in the emergency department KC and colleagues studied, busy shifts with more easy cases went with more patients seen, but also with a lower volume of work once complexity was taken into account. The other axis is biased in a predictable direction too: in Buehler and colleagues' study, students predicted handing in their thesis after 33.9 days and actually took 55.5. Within this piece's search, no controlled measurement has turned up showing whether the tool helps or harms results. What follows for use: agree the units of both axes and record running costs separately, estimate effort from the last similar job rather than from the plan, read by ratio and net out overlap rather than reading by quadrant alone, and report with an outcome measure rather than the number of tasks finished.
