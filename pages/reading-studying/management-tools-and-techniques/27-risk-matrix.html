Risk Matrix

A matrix squeezes each risk into one cell with one scenario; a rare but catastrophic risk can score lower than a common, minor one, and simply flipping the direction of the scale is enough to move the last-ranked risk to the top

Published: November 22, 2026

Definition

What Is a Risk Matrix

Suppose the HR department of a 300-person company is about to switch to new time-and-attendance software and can list five things that might go wrong: staff forget to clock in during the first week, the fingerprint clock breaks down for a day, the first month's payroll comes out wrong for some people, the vendor stops supporting the software, and the personal data of all 300 employees, including ID numbers and the bank accounts their salaries go to, leaks out. A risk matrix places each of these on a two-dimensional grid: one axis is the likelihood of it happening, the other is the consequence if it does, and each axis is split into a few ordered categories. Each cell of the grid carries a risk level, usually shown by colour, and that level says how a risk in the cell is handled: accepted, monitored, or acted on now.

The model matrix that the National Patient Safety Agency (NPSA) of the NHS, Britain's public health service, proposed for healthcare organisations shows the tool in a common form: a five-by-five grid and multiplied scores. Consequence is scored from 1 to 5, from negligible to catastrophic, with a descriptor table for each domain: harm to patients, complaints, staffing, finance, reputation. Likelihood is also scored from 1 to 5, from rare to almost certain, preferably by expected frequency, then by probability over a time period, with verbal descriptions used only when no number can be estimated. The risk score is consequence times likelihood, from 1 to 25, split into four levels: 1 to 3 is low, 4 to 6 moderate, 8 to 12 high, 15 to 25 extreme. The model states that the tables can be adapted to suit each organisation. [1] In Britain the matrix is also used at national level: the National Risk Register, the public version of the government's classified risk assessment, presents risks on two likelihood-and-impact matrices and explains that a matrix shows at a glance the relative likelihood and impact of different risks. [2]

The tool has no founding text and no single author; within the scope of this piece's search, no document that introduced it has been found. The earliest marker the piece could check lies in US military system safety. According to a 2005 presentation on the history of the US Department of Defense system safety standard MIL-STD-882, the original 1969 version and 882A of 1977 had no matrix, while 882B of March 1984 put a qualitative matrix in an appendix, with four hazard categories from catastrophic to negligible and five frequency categories from frequent to improbable. [3] The piece has not read the standard itself. It uses the NPSA model for Figure 1, for the checking questions and for multiplying consequence by likelihood; where another matrix divides its categories or colours its cells differently, the piece says so.

The two tools most easily confused with it are both other ways of scoring. FMEA generates its own list by asking "where can this fail" at each step of a process, and adds a third axis, detectability; a risk matrix takes risks that have already been named, from any source, and scores them on two axes. An impact/effort matrix is also a two-axis grid, but its two axes pull against each other, how much you gain and how much it costs, so the best cell sits in one particular corner; in a risk matrix both axes point the same way, worse as you go up and to the right, and it only looks at the downside. Putting the HR department's five issues on the NPSA model gives Figure 1.

Why We Need It

A meeting that lists thirty things that could go wrong, with no frame at all, easily ends with a list ordered by the worry of whoever speaks loudest. What just happened at the company next door gets discussed at length, what nobody has ever seen gets skimmed, and two different questions, "does it happen often" and "how bad is it if it does", get blended into one general feeling of "scary". A very common but minor risk and a very rare but deadly one can earn the same level of worry, or a level of worry that runs opposite to what each risk actually calls for.

The tool's mechanism is to split the question "how big is this risk" into two separate judgements, likelihood and consequence, reduce each judgement to a few ordered categories, and let the position on the grid decide how the risk is treated. The split is the part that does the work: every risk has to answer both questions, and its answers sit next to every other risk's answers on the same page. Reducing to categories is the part that makes the matrix quick and easy to read, and also the part that loses information: a wide range of probabilities becomes a single number from 1 to 5, and every consequence above a threshold lands in the top category.

The gain, as far as it has been measured, is that people can understand risk information more accurately when it sits on a matrix rather than in text, but only with some designs. In the experiment by Sutherland and colleagues in the Research section, with 1,273 adults in Britain, two matrix formats gave higher overall comprehension scores than text; and on the questions comparing two risks, changing the axis labels from 1 to 5 into 1, 5, 25, 125, 625 raised the average score from 7.61 to 10.39 out of 18. [4] On this piece's reading, the tool helps at the stage of looking and discussing, and helps more when the axis labels show the real magnitudes.

What Are Its Limits

The first limit is inherent: reducing to categories is compression. Louis Anthony Cox, in a mathematical analysis published in Risk Analysis in 2008, calls this poor resolution: the matrix assigns the same rating to risks that differ enormously in quantity, and a typical matrix can correctly and unambiguously compare only a small fraction of randomly chosen pairs of risks, less than 10% in his example. [5] The compression shows up straight away in the category tables. In the 2000 standard MIL-STD-882D, the "remote" category runs from one in a million to one in a thousand, a thousandfold spread from one end of the same category to the other. [3] Philip Thomas, Reidar Bratvold and J. Eric Bickel show that a drilling matrix the oil and gas industry calls typical puts a loss of 20 million dollars and a loss of around 50 billion dollars, the scale of BP's losses after the Macondo blowout, in the same "catastrophic" category. [6]

Second, also inherent: scores and colours cannot track expected loss, that is, probability times loss. Cox proves that a matrix can give a higher rating to a quantitatively smaller risk, and that when likelihood and consequence run in opposite directions, so that the worse a risk is the rarer it is, a matrix can lead to decisions worse than random. [5] Figure 1 is one such case on the NPSA model. Thomas and colleagues reviewed 30 papers from the Society of Petroleum Engineers (SPE) that present the matrix as best practice and found that every one violated at least one of the consistency conditions Cox set out; in 8 of them, the structure of the matrix allowed a risk in a yellow cell to have a larger expected loss than every risk in the red cells. [6] How bad this error gets depends on how the categories are set and the cells coloured, but as long as likelihood and consequence are reduced to a few categories, it never fully goes away.

Third, inherent: the ranking depends on design choices that have no scientific basis. The same authors show that just switching the scoring from ascending to descending changes the priority order of three risks outright, moving the last-ranked risk to the top, and that shifting the boundaries between categories slightly also changes the ranking; the Examples section retells this case. [6] The top of the scale is compressed in another way too: in their example, a blowout can be many orders of magnitude worse than a loss of well control, yet the consequence categories differ only by 6 against 5, and on the original matrix the two risks share the same yellow, simply because the "catastrophic" category takes every loss above 20 million dollars. And the default template has room for only one point per risk: a risk with both a mild scenario and a catastrophic one has to have one scenario chosen to place in the cell, unless the user adds a second point.

The rest are errors of use, and they have been counted. Categories with words but no numbers are read differently by everyone: Thomas and colleagues cite a 2009 study by Budescu and colleagues in which, even though the guidelines said "very likely" meant above 90%, participants still read the phrase as anywhere from 43% to 99%; in the SPE papers themselves, "frequent" means more than once a year in one paper and more than once in ten years in another. [6]

Scorers also avoid the ends of the scale: in six SPE papers with more than 15 risks, 83% of likelihood scores fell in the middle three categories. [6] And different scorers give the same hazard very different ratings: David Ball and John Watt, in a study of hazards in leisure and tourism activities, note that the spread stayed high even after scorers had thought and learned about it at length, and that its source lies in worldviews and beliefs that are rarely voiced. [7] The How to Apply It section covers how to reduce each error.

Examples

An invented software company, a drilling matrix that three researchers rescored on scales running in two directions, and the 2017 UK National Risk Register matrix show three places where reducing to categories loses information: words without numbers, rankings that depend on design, and a cell that can hold only one scenario.

One "Server Incident" Line, Two Different Scenarios

An illustrative case: an accounting software company in Hai Phong with 80 staff, serving about 600 business clients, updates its risk register every quarter. Six department heads score their own departments' risks on a five-by-five matrix whose likelihood categories are words only, from "rare" to "almost certain". The fourth-quarter register has 31 lines; 27 of them sit in the middle three columns. The "server incident" line, scored by the infrastructure lead as "possible" times "moderate", gets 9 points, high, the same level as eight other lines; the board sees nine high lines, none extreme, and keeps its plans unchanged. When scoring, the infrastructure lead had a failed hard drive in mind, replaced in two hours. In March, ransomware encrypts the main server; the backups have never been test-restored, and the first restore fails. The service is down for four days, in exactly the week clients must file their quarterly tax returns. Only in the review does the company find that the infrastructure lead read "possible" as about once a year, while the head of sales, who scored the sales lines with the same word, read it as about once in ten years.

The matrix still has five columns, five rows and four colours, but three things its mechanism needs are gone. The likelihood categories have no numeric ranges, so the same word carries two meanings ten times apart, exactly the kind of gap that research on how people read probability words has measured. [6] One line holds two scenarios, and the mild one decides the cell; Cox calls this ambiguous input: when consequences are uncertain, there is no objective way to put them in one category. [5] And 27 of 31 lines crowd into the middle three columns, so nine high lines look alike although they differ greatly. A check for your own risk register: among the lines at moderate or high, which ones merge a familiar scenario with one that, if it happened, would belong in the top row?

Three Risks of One Well, Two Priority Orders

In 2010, an article in a drilling special issue of World Oil presented a matrix its authors called a typical industry risk assessment matrix, applied to one incident: lost drilling fluid in a section of a well. It listed three bad outcomes that could follow: severe losses, probability 40%, costing 1 to 5 million dollars; loss of well control, probability 10%, costing 5 to 20 million; and blowout, probability 5%, costing above 20 million. Thomas, Bratvold and Bickel took exactly that example, adjusted it so the category ranges no longer overlapped, then scored it the way many SPE papers do: multiplying the likelihood score by the consequence score on a six-by-six matrix. With an ascending scale, severe losses gets 5 times 4 equals 20, loss of well control 3 times 5 equals 15, blowout 2 times 6 equals 12; severe losses sits in a red cell and is handled first, and blowout comes last. The original article, however, used a descending scale, where a lower score means more serious. Rescored on that scale, severe losses gets 2 times 3 equals 6, loss of well control 4 times 2 equals 8, blowout 5 times 1 equals 5, and blowout, with the lowest score, goes to the top of the list. [6]

Nothing about the well changed between the two scorings; only the direction of the scale did. Of the 30 SPE papers the authors reviewed, 5 used descending scales and 25 ascending, and there is no scientific basis for either. They also show that shifting the boundaries between the probability categories slightly changes the ranking of the three risks as well. [6] To compare with an ordering that does not pass through categories, this piece takes the midpoints of the loss ranges the authors use in their sensitivity analysis, 3, 12.5 and 50 million dollars, and multiplies them by the probabilities: the expected loss of severe losses is about 1.2 million, of loss of well control about 1.25 million, of blowout about 2.5 million, twice the other two; this is the piece's own calculation, and the 50 million figure is an assumption, since the top category has no ceiling. The case does not show which scale is right. It shows that a priority order that can change with the direction of the scale or with the category boundaries is not solid enough to decide anything for anyone, and that for the risks with the largest consequences, you need to go back to the numbers.

Flu in the Top Row, a New Disease in the Middle: Britain in 2017

The 2017 UK National Risk Register has two matrices; matrix A covers natural hazards, diseases, accidents and societal risks, with likelihood of occurring in the next five years on the horizontal axis and severity of impact on the vertical, each axis in five categories. Each step up the likelihood axis corresponds to a roughly tenfold rise in probability; impact is scored on five dimensions, from fatalities to psychological impact, then combined into one score; and each risk is described by a reasonable worst case scenario. Pandemic influenza is the only symbol in impact row 5, likelihood column 4, with a scenario of up to 50% of the population showing symptoms and between 20,000 and 750,000 deaths. Emerging infectious disease sits in impact row 3, in the same likelihood column 4, sharing a cell with heatwave, space weather and poor air quality, with a scenario of several thousand people showing symptoms and up to 100 deaths. The Register's text says the likelihood of an emerging infectious disease spreading within the UK is lower than that of a flu pandemic, yet on the grid the two risks stand in the same column: one tenfold step is wide enough to hold both. [2]

The Module 1 report of the UK Covid-19 Inquiry, published in July 2024, names as the first of five major flaws in pre-pandemic risk assessment an over-reliance on a single scenario, pandemic influenza, and on that scenario's likelihood. The assessments of 2014, 2016 and 2019 put the reasonable worst case for an emerging disease at 200 deaths, then 101 to 1,000, then 200 again, based on data from the 2002-2003 SARS outbreak; the figure of 100 in the 2017 public register is lower still than the 2016 classified assessment, and this piece has found no explanation for the gap. Meanwhile, the Advisory Committee on Dangerous Pathogens had noted that beyond calling such an event likely, no estimate of its likelihood or impact could be given. The report calls taking SARS as the sole basis "a significant error", because it sent the message that no specific preparation was needed for an emerging disease other than flu reaching pandemic scale. The report also agrees with the Royal Academy of Engineering's 2021 recommendation that likelihood should not be the main driver of prioritisation, and notes that the 2022 assessment introduced more generic pandemic and emerging disease scenarios. [8]

The report does not discuss the matrix as a tool; what it criticises is the risk assessment behind the matrix, and a different presentation would not by itself have fixed the choice of the wrong scenario. But on this piece's reading, the matrix in the Register is where that choice became visible to readers, and its shape demands exactly that choice: one symbol per risk, one scenario per symbol. A scenario of a new respiratory disease spreading to pandemic scale has no symbol on the grid: the uncertainty the Advisory Committee said could not be estimated was replaced by a scenario of up to 100 deaths, and only that scenario has a place in the "impact 3" cell. The question a matrix user can take from this case: for each risk whose consequences span a wide range, is the scenario placed in the cell the one we would most regret having ignored?

Some Research on the Risk Matrix

The tool's reach is wide and well documented. According to Thomas and colleagues, the matrix is recommended in standards from ISO, the American Petroleum Institute (API) and NORSOK in the Norwegian oil and gas industry; ISO 31000 calls it a probability/consequence matrix, and ISO/IEC 31010 rates it "strongly applicable" for risk identification and analysis. Searching for "risk matrix" and "risk matrices" in the industry's OnePetro database returned 527 papers; of the 407 from 2000 onwards, 68 both promoted the matrix as best practice and demonstrated its use; removing papers that presented the same example left 30 for review, and the authors found no SPE paper documenting its known pitfalls. [6] British healthcare has the NPSA model [1], and the British government uses the matrix to present national risks. [2] The evidence of its value is much thinner. Within the scope of this piece's search, no study has been found that compares the risk management outcomes of organisations that use the matrix with those that do not; Thomas and colleagues write that they know of no published scientific study showing that the matrix improves risk management decisions [6], and Cox notes that little research rigorously validates its performance. [5] The evidence available falls into two strands. The first points out errors: Cox's mathematical analysis, Thomas and colleagues' review of 30 SPE papers with the figures given in the Limits section, and Ball and Watt's study of the spread between scorers, read here via its abstract. [7] The second asks a narrower question but measures the tool directly: do people understand risk information better when it is on a matrix, and which designs help. The study below belongs to the second strand.

The study: Holly Sutherland, Gabriel Recchia, Sarah Dryhurst and Alexandra Freeman, at the Winton Centre for Risk and Evidence Communication at the University of Cambridge, ran two randomised online experiments with 2,699 adults in Britain in total, published in Risk Analysis in 2022. [4] The matrices in the experiments were modelled on the 2017 UK National Risk Register and showed hypothetical flood risks; the authors describe each likelihood category of the Register as five times the one before, while the methodology section of the 2017 edition itself says each step rises about tenfold. Experiment 1, with 1,426 people, randomly assigned participants to four formats: a standard matrix, a square-cell matrix, a matrix with cells growing towards the top right corner, and text only. Experiment 2, with 1,273 different people, assigned them to 12 groups by three factors: format (standard matrix, growing-cell matrix, text), axis labels (linear 1 to 5, or exponential 1, 5, 25, 125, 625), and a separate key or labels written directly on the axes. Participants answered basic reading questions, 18 questions comparing two risks, such as "how many times more likely is risk A than risk B", and a few questions choosing between reducing likelihood and reducing consequence.

Findings: In experiment 1, on the risk comparison questions, only the growing-cell matrix scored significantly higher than text (p = 0.038); the standard and square-cell matrices showed no difference from text. In experiment 2, both matrix formats gave higher overall comprehension scores than text (p = 0.03 and p = 0.003). The factor with the largest effect was the axis labels: on the 18 risk comparison questions, the group with exponential labels averaged 10.39 correct, the linear group 7.61; the advantage remained when the "how many times" questions were removed. Putting the key on the axes made no main difference. Even so, when asked, more people preferred linear labels (42%) than exponential ones (35%). In both experiments, participants leaned towards reducing consequence rather than likelihood, and this tendency did not change with format. [4]

What it shows: A matrix is not automatically easier to understand than text; it helps people understand more accurately in some formats, and in this experiment, how the axes were labelled made a bigger difference than choosing a matrix over text. On an axis where each step is five times the last, labels of 1 to 5 left people far worse at comparing the ratio between two risks than labels showing the real magnitudes. The study measures understanding, not organisational decisions or outcomes; it tests only semi-quantitative matrices with one or two risks at a time, on the general adult population, where only 359 of the 1,260 people analysed in experiment 2 had used a matrix before; and the authors themselves note that most effect sizes were small, and that exponential labels can make a rough estimate look more precise than it is.

The counter-case: The defenders have two arguments worth hearing. The first: the matrix was never meant to be used as a calculation; it is a place for a group to look at risk together and argue about it, and Thomas and colleagues themselves, the strongest critics, note in a footnote that using the matrix may be better than doing nothing, since any method that prompts discussion of risk is useful. [6] The second: the mathematical errors only appear when someone multiplies scores or ranks mechanically by colour; experienced users know that a rare but catastrophic risk needs looking at separately, and in experiment 2 of Sutherland and colleagues, the matrix still gave better understanding than text. This piece accepts the first argument: splitting likelihood from consequence is the valuable part, and prompting discussion is a real benefit, even though nobody has measured it in outcomes. The second it accepts only in half. The experiment measures reading one or two risks that someone else has already scored, not the scoring; and the places where the errors arise, such as words without numbers, scores crowding into the middle, rankings that change with the direction of the scale, all sit in the scoring step and have all been counted in papers written by people in the industry itself [6]. If good users always look separately at rare but catastrophic risks, that rule needs writing down as a step, because on Figure 1, built on the NPSA model, colour does not do it for them. Weighing the two sides, the evidence strongly supports that the matrix compresses information and that its ranking depends on design, moderately supports that a well-designed matrix helps people understand risk information better than text, and no controlled measurement yet shows whether it helps or harms risk management outcomes.

Why this is useful: Axis labels cost almost nothing to fix, and they are the factor the experiment found to have the largest effect. On the matrix you use, write the probability or frequency range directly on each column, at its real magnitude, instead of the word "possible" or a bare number 3; if the steps are five or ten times apart, let the labels show it. And before the scoring session, ask the whole group one question: how many times more likely is a risk in column 4 than a risk in column 2? If the answer is "twice" and your scale rises tenfold per step, the group is misreading its own matrix.

How to Apply It

A risk matrix works by splitting the question "how big is this risk" into two separate judgements, likelihood and consequence, reducing each to a few categories, and letting the cell decide how the risk is treated, so the effective way to use it is to keep the information that reducing to categories throws away: tie categories to numbers, separate the scenarios, do not let colour decide in the highest consequence rows, and switch to numbers when money has to be spent. The tool fits best for screening a long list of named risks and for letting a whole group discuss them on one page; it fits poorly when a budget must be divided among a few large risks, or when the consequences of one risk range from mild to catastrophic.

  • Tie each category to a numeric range and write that range on the axis: Write likelihood as a frequency or a probability over a stated period, as the NPSA model prefers [1]; write consequence in people, money or days of lost work. The word "possible" is not enough, because even with guidelines, people read the same probability phrase very differently [6]; and when the steps rise exponentially, the labels should too, so readers compare ratios correctly [4]. If you have past data, put the number of past occurrences right next to the score
  • Write each line as one scenario, and add a line for the bad scenario when consequences range widely: A cell holds only one point, so a line that merges a mild scenario with a catastrophic one gets scored on whichever scenario the scorer thinks of first, usually the familiar one. Split "heat illness" into "mild exhaustion" and "heatstroke needing hospital transfer", and split "server incident" into "failed hard drive" and "unrecoverable data loss". For each risk, also ask: is there another, less likely scenario that would sit in the top row if it happened? This is where the British case shows that a single scenario can hide most of the impact [8]
  • Filter by consequence row before sorting by colour: Every line in the two highest consequence rows is looked at separately and has a measure or a reason for acceptance written down, whatever its score. Multiplication can give a rare but catastrophic risk the same score as a common, minor one, as in Figure 1, and the matrix's colours can misorder risks by expected loss [5]; the Covid-19 Inquiry also agrees that likelihood should not be the main driver of prioritisation [8]. Expected loss is a better yardstick than colour, but it is not the only criterion either: a consequence that is fatal, unlawful or irreversible may deserve treatment before a risk with a larger expected loss, and this filter keeps room for that judgement. For lines close to the boundary between two colours, rescore with the adjacent category; if the order changes, do not treat that order as a conclusion, just as the boundaries and the direction of the scale changed the ranking in the drilling case [6]
  • Switch to numbers when money has to be spent, and measure with real incidents rather than the number of red cells: The matrix screens, but its categories are not enough to divide resources between measures [5]; for the lines that pass the filter, write down the cost of each measure and what it changes, then compare within the budget. Counting "red cells reduced" fails in two directions: rescoring to look better changes a cell's colour while nothing has changed, and a measure that reduces the harm within the same category, such as a defibrillator for a cardiac arrest, moves no cell at all. After each period, set incidents and near misses next to the cells as scored, then adjust the scores against the category definitions you wrote down

Applying It to a Scenario

The organisers of a running race in Quy Nhon are preparing its second edition, on Sunday 7 March 2027: two distances, 21 km and 10 km, 2,500 registered runners, starting at 5 am on a seafront road. On 5 February, the main sponsor asks for a risk assessment with a treatment plan by 20 February, and a post-race report. The goal: submit the post-race report before 21 March 2027, showing that every risk line with a consequence in category 4 or 5 had a measure, an owner and a deadline in the plan submitted before 20 February, that total spending on measures stayed within 120 million dong, the safety contingency in the race budget, and that each line had been set against real incidents to adjust the scores for the next race. Five people leading the medical, logistics, course, timing and communications teams do this over two weeks. All figures are illustrative.

  1. Fix a numeric scale before scoring: At the first meeting, while still using words, the logistics lead scores cardiac arrest as "rare", the medical lead as "unlikely", and the two argue for ten minutes before realising they mean the same number. The group switches to a numeric scale. Likelihood is the probability that the event happens at least once during the race: category 1 below 1%, category 2 from 1% to under 5%, category 3 from 5% to under 25%, category 4 from 25% to under 60%, category 5 at 60% or above; these thresholds are written directly on the horizontal axis, and each likelihood score carries a confidence rating: high when there are counts from several periods, low when there are only one or two observations or an expert's estimate. Consequence takes the most severe of three things, people, money and the race: category 1 nobody needs medical care, loss below 5 million; category 2 first aid on site, or 5 to 20 million; category 3 one person transferred to hospital and recovering within a few days, or 20 to 100 million; category 4 an injury that may leave lasting damage, or one distance cancelled, or 100 to 500 million; category 5 a death, or the whole race cancelled, or above 500 million. Scores and the four colour levels follow the NPSA model: multiplication is the model's convention, and the group keeps it for colouring but does not let it override the filter in step 4
  2. Write each risk as a specific consequence, one scenario per line: The draft list has a line for "heat illness". The medical lead splits it in two: A, heat exhaustion, resting at a medical station and then going home; and B, severe heatstroke needing hospital transfer. The final list has eight lines: A and B; C, cardiac arrest on the course; D, the inflatable start arch collapsing in strong wind; E, a motorbike getting onto the course and hitting a runner; F, the water station at km 15 running dry for more than 20 minutes; G, a timing chip fault producing wrong results for a group of runners; H, runners taking the wrong turn at the km 8 junction

Score consequence, then likelihood, recording the basis for each score: The main basis is the organisers' own race last year, 2,100 runners, the same course, the same month: 9 cases of mild exhaustion, 1 case of heatstroke transferred to hospital, one occasion when the km 15 station ran dry because the supply truck took the wrong road, no cardiac arrests. The goal is about preparation and record-keeping, not about outcomes, and it was met under four conditions. The consequence-row filter is what brought the start arch into the plan; by the colour of the multiplied score, that line got only 5 points, the same moderate level as runners taking a wrong turn, and the lowest-scoring line, H, was treated only because budget was left over. The combined score has not shown that the order among A, F, G and H reflects expected loss; here that order decided nothing, because all four fit the budget. One race is one trial: 11 cases of exhaustion and 1 of heatstroke fit the categories scored but cannot test a probability, and the absence of cardiac arrests says nothing about whether the defibrillators work, since there is no comparison group. And this year's heatstroke case was milder than the consequence category scored for line B, but one case is not enough to lower that category; the group keeps category 4 and records the case in the data for next year, along with the motorbike near miss at km 6.

Summary

Key Point: A risk matrix splits each risk into two judgements, likelihood and consequence, reduces each to a few categories, then lets the cell decide how the risk is treated; the split is the valuable part, the reduction to categories is the part that loses information. The most counter-intuitive point is that the matrix's ranking depends on design choices that seem harmless: in the drilling case reanalysed by Thomas, Bratvold and Bickel, simply switching the scoring from ascending to descending moved the blowout from the bottom of the priority list to the top. A matrix can also give a rare but catastrophic risk a lower rating than a common, minor one, as Cox proved. In Sutherland and colleagues' experiment, exponential axis labels helped people compare risks more accurately than labels of 1 to 5, but within the scope of this piece's search, no controlled measurement has been found showing that the tool helps or harms risk management outcomes. The practice that follows: tie categories to numbers and write them on the axes, write each line as one scenario and add a line for the bad scenario, filter by consequence row before sorting by colour, and switch to numbers when money has to be spent.
