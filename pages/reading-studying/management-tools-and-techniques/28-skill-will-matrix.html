Skill/Will Matrix

The matrix separates "can they do it" from "do they want to" for one person on one specific task; a "low will" box may hold causes that sit outside the person being scored, such as tools, workload or pay

Published: November 22, 2026

Definition

What Is a Skill/Will Matrix

The same person can be both the most trusted member of a team and the one who needs the closest supervision, depending on the task. Suppose the content lead at a 20-person media company has four direct reports: Thu and Minh have written articles for several years and write well; Minh has just been given short-video editing as well, work he very much wants to do but has never done; Lan is the best on the team at handling clients but has avoided every client meeting for the past few months; Quân, the newcomer, has to produce the monthly figures report, which he neither knows how to do nor wants to do. The skill/will matrix places each such pairing of person and task on a two-axis grid: skill, meaning how far the person already knows how to do that task and can do it, and will, meaning how much the person wants to do that task. Each quadrant calls for a different way of managing.

According to Mind Tools' introduction to the tool, the matrix was set out by Max Landsberg in The Tao of Coaching; Mind Tools cites the 2003 Profile Books edition and describes the four quadrants as follows. Low skill and low will is the direct quadrant: it takes the most time and effort, with clear deadlines and close monitoring. Low skill and high will is the guide quadrant: give time for learning and encourage managed risks. High skill and low will is the excite quadrant: find out what motivates the person and reduce what demotivates them. High on both is the delegate quadrant: the manager steps back into the role of mentor, a sounding board for the person's ideas. [1] The book was first published in 1996 in London by HarperCollins [2]; this article has not read the book, so it has not checked whether the matrix was already in the 1996 edition or arrived in a later one. According to the biography on his own website, Landsberg was a partner at McKinsey & Company, then a senior partner at two executive search firms, and has worked as an executive coach. [3] This article uses Mind Tools' description of the four quadrants for Figure 1 and for every test question.

The matrix's two axes coincide with the two components that Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard's situational leadership theory uses to determine a follower's development level: competence and commitment. [4] Within the scope of this article's search, no primary source was found saying the matrix was built from that theory; placing the two side by side is this article's reading, and a useful one, because situational leadership is the closest relative that has actually been researched. The matrix also differs from the RACI matrix, even though both work task by task: RACI asks who does the work, who is accountable, who is consulted and who is informed; the skill/will matrix asks how the person already given the task should be managed. Placing the content lead's five pairings of person and task on the matrix gives Figure 1.

Why We Need It

"He's good but lazy" and "she works hard but isn't there yet" are lines you hear in any office, and both are usually said about the same thing: work that doesn't get done. Without a framework, a manager can slide into one of two habits. One is to manage the whole team the same way, watching everyone closely or leaving everyone alone. The other is to misread the cause: someone who doesn't yet know how to do the work is treated as lacking commitment and gets a talk about attitude, while someone who can do it but doesn't want to is sent on yet another course.

The tool's mechanism is to split the question "why is this person not doing this task well" into two separate diagnoses, can they do it and do they want to, because the two answers call for different remedies: a skill gap is fixed with teaching, coaching and time; a will gap is fixed by finding out why and removing what gets in the way. The quadrant a person-task pairing lands in then sets how much direction and how much monitoring to give. The value lies in the split. Dividing it into four quadrants makes the tool quick, and also loses nuance, because each axis is left with only two levels.

The case for asking two questions instead of one rests on a number one level up. In the meta-analysis by Van Iddekinge and colleagues, described in the research section, pooling 55 samples and 11,283 people, the correlation between cognitive ability and motivation, after correcting for measurement error, was only 0.07 (0 means no relationship, 1 a perfect one), while each was related to performance, 0.44 for cognitive ability and 0.29 for motivation. [5] Knowing how well someone does on cognitive tests tells you almost nothing about how motivated they are, and the reverse. General cognitive ability is not skill on a specific task, and within the scope of this article's search, no study was found measuring the correlation between the matrix's own two axes. On this article's reading, the number is a reason not to infer one axis from the other; it is not yet direct evidence.

What Are Its Limits

The matrix is only as good as the two scores that go into it. The inherent part: the grid has no step that checks those scores. The part that comes from use: Mind Tools' version has the manager score both axes for the people who report to them. [1] Research on situational leadership has run into exactly this. Geir Thompson and Jarle Glasø, with 80 supervisors and 357 followers in financial organisations, found that the theory's predictions were more likely to hold when the supervisor's rating of a follower's competence and commitment matched the follower's own self-rating, compared with using the supervisor's rating alone, as earlier studies had done. [4] A later study by the same two authors, using survey data from supervisors and employees in Norwegian businesses, found the theory supported when the two ratings agreed; when they disagreed, falling back on the employee's self-rating did no better, and the supervisor's assessment remained the better basis for choosing how much direction and support to give. [6] This article read both studies through their abstracts. What they suggest is not to trust the person being rated over the person doing the rating; it is that the gap between the two is already information.

Second, also inherent to the four-box template: the will axis has no place to record a cause. Someone may not want to do a task because they dislike it, but also because they are carrying three jobs at once, because the tools are slow, because two bosses are asking for two different priorities, or because the task pays less than other work. Mind Tools' advice for the excite quadrant does mention reducing demotivators [1], but on the grid the "low will" label still attaches to the person, and anyone reading the grid later sees only the label.

Third, inherent: the matrix is a snapshot of one moment, while the very management style it suggests changes both axes. On this article's reading, a label on the grid can become a manager's expectation, and expectations can change performance. The Pygmalion effect is a self-fulfilling prophecy of this kind; the d below is the average difference in performance produced by the intervention, measured in standard deviations. In Brian McNatt's meta-analysis of 17 studies, 58 effect sizes and 2,874 people in management settings, the average effect was d = 1.13, stronger in the military, among men, and among people for whom low expectations were initially held. [7] Nicole Kierein and Michael Gold, across 13 effect sizes in organisations, found d = 0.81, stronger when initial performance was low and when the experiment took place in the military rather than in business. [8] This article read both through their abstracts; McNatt himself notes the need for more research in ongoing work rather than training, and in for-profit organisations rather than the military. Both figures measure interventions that raise expectations; the article has no measurement for the reverse direction, where a low label pulls performance down. A low will score is not necessarily a low expectation either: a manager can see that motivation is low today and still believe the person will come up quickly. The risk lies elsewhere. A "low will" box read as a verdict on the person rather than on a task can become a low expectation written down, placed on exactly the group where changing expectations had the strongest effect in these meta-analyses; how much the label itself does, no one has measured.

The rest are errors of use. Mind Tools' version talks about assessing the skills and motivation of the people who report to you without saying it should be done task by task [1], so the matrix is easily filled in with one dot per person, as if skill at writing and skill at video editing were the same thing. And the delegate quadrant is often read as leaving people alone, when that same version describes the manager stepping back into the role of mentor and remaining present. How to reduce each error is covered under How to Apply It.

Examples

Two constructed scenarios, each showing a common failure: a low will label pinned on a person when the cause lies in the task and the working conditions, and a delegate quadrant borrowed from one task for another. Within the scope of this article's search, no real case was found whose source records the matrix being used and with what result.

An Architect With Four Years' Experience in the Excite Quadrant

An illustrative scenario: the head of design at a 25-person architecture firm in Vũng Tàu fills in the matrix for the six people in the department at the start of January. Hà, an architect with four years' experience, has always submitted drawings on time with few errors, but since October has been late on two of every three submissions and says almost nothing in meetings. The head puts Hà in the excite quadrant: high skill, low will. He does exactly what that quadrant suggests, talks with Hà about a path to lead designer, and praises Hà in front of the whole department when a former client comes back. In March, Hà is still late. The head moves Hà into the direct quadrant: every drawing must go through him before it is sent, and there is a fifteen-minute progress meeting every morning. In May, Hà resigns. In the conversation before leaving, Hà explains that since October the firm has switched to new design software, which Hà was never properly trained on and has been learning alone in the evenings; that Hà's computer takes about 40 minutes to render a single perspective view; and that since November Hà has been running three projects at once.

The matrix still has both axes and all four quadrants, but three things its mechanism needs were missing. Hà was scored once for everything, when for drawing in the new software Hà is low on skill; whether the right quadrant for that task is guide or direct depends on Hà's will for that particular task. The will axis absorbed every cause that lay outside Hà, from the slow computer to the three parallel projects, while the label attached to Hà. And only the head did the scoring; nobody ever asked Hà for a self-assessment, the kind of one-sided diagnosis that, in research on situational leadership, predicted worse than a diagnosis on which both sides agreed. [4] A test question for your own team: for each person sitting in a low will quadrant, have you asked them, and have you looked at their workload and their tools?

Six Weeks in the Delegate Quadrant

An illustrative scenario: a chain of seven optical shops in Hà Nội opens an eighth and hires three new staff. Tùng, whose rate of turning eye-test customers into glasses sales has been the highest in the chain two years running, is asked to train the three newcomers over their first six weeks. The regional manager puts Tùng in the delegate quadrant on the strength of his sales record and leaves him to it: no meetings are scheduled in six weeks. Two of the three newcomers quit before week five. When the regional manager asks what happened, Tùng says he had never taught anyone; he let the newcomers watch him sell for a few days, then left them to get on with it. He sensed things were going wrong from week two but didn't ask, because he took being given the job to mean his manager trusted him to do it.

Selling glasses and teaching someone else to sell glasses are two different skills, and for the training task, Tùng is in the guide quadrant: high will, no skill yet. The score for the old task has been borrowed for the new one. The delegate quadrant in Mind Tools' version doesn't mean absence either: the manager moves into the role of mentor, a sounding board for the other person's ideas [1], so even if Tùng really belonged in that quadrant, six weeks without a single meeting would still depart from how that version describes it. A test question: when you give your best person a new task, are you scoring their skill on the new task, or borrowing the score from the old one?

Some Research on the Skill/Will Matrix

Within the scope of this article's search, no study was found that measures the matrix itself, whether a comparison of managers who use it and managers who don't, a test of the advice for each quadrant, or a survey of how widely it is used. The only sign of its reach is its presence in management training material such as Mind Tools. [1] The closest evidence sits one level up and falls into three strands. The first is research on situational leadership, the theory with the same two axes, where the two studies by Thompson and Glasø in the limits section show that the weak point is who does the scoring. [4] [6] The second is the meta-analyses of the Pygmalion effect, which speak to the weight of the expectations a manager carries, something a label can create. [7] [8] The third tests, at the level of general cognitive ability, the premise the whole matrix rests on: are ability and motivation separate, and how do they combine to produce performance? The study below belongs to the third strand, and it is the only study in this article read in full.

The study: Chad Van Iddekinge, Herman Aguinis, Jeremy Mackey and Philip DeOrtentiis tested a long-standing belief in management: that performance is a function of ability multiplied by motivation, so that with little of one, people perform poorly however high the other is. The authors trace the belief to Maier (1955) and Vroom (1964), note that it appears in undergraduate, graduate and executive textbooks, and cite anecdotal evidence that it shapes the advice consultants give. From over 3,000 studies screened, they kept 56 independent samples that measured all three: 39 journal articles, 16 dissertations and theses, and one conference paper. Because no study reported enough statistics to test the multiplication, they contacted the authors of 56 studies; 48 replied, and 33 sent raw data or ran the analyses themselves using syntax the team provided. Ability here is cognitive ability, measured with objective tests (verbal, quantitative, spatial); in the findings below, "ability" carries that meaning. Motivation was measured as a trait, such as achievement motivation, or as a state, such as effort or time devoted to a specific task and the goals set; conscientiousness was excluded as too broad. Performance meant job performance (rated by supervisors or peers, or measured objectively, as with sales), training outcomes, and scores on simulated work tasks in the laboratory. The analyses pooled 40 to 55 samples and 8,507 to 11,283 people. [5]

Findings: The corrected correlation between ability and motivation was 0.07; between ability and performance, 0.44; between motivation and performance, 0.29. Adding the product of ability and motivation to the model raised the multiple correlation, a measure of how well the model predicts performance, by only 0.02. When the explained variance, meaning the part of the differences in performance between people that the model accounts for, was divided up, ability took 60.1%, motivation 30.5% and the interaction 9.4%; the additive effects of the two therefore held about 91%. This is a division of what the statistical model explains; it does not say how much of performance each one causes. For job performance alone, with 19 samples and 2,964 people, the interaction's share was larger, 17.0%, but still the minor part. The rise in performance with ability, the slope, was only very slightly steeper when motivation was higher: 0.22 at low motivation, 0.24 at average, 0.25 at high; and across 67 individual analyses, the slope rose by 0.10 or more in 23 (34.3%), barely changed in 27 (40.3%), and fell by 0.10 or more in 17 (25.4%). In the additive model, ability and motivation contributed equally to job performance, 50% each; ability dominated in training (83.0% against 17.0%) and in the laboratory (70.8% against 29.2%). Motivation measured as a state was more strongly related to performance than motivation measured as a trait, 0.37 against 0.23. [5]

What it shows: Cognitive ability and motivation are almost independent of each other, each is related to performance, and they combine mainly by adding rather than multiplying: at the level of the sample, someone low on one axis can partly make up for it with the other. That does not mean a person lacking a required skill will get the job done through will. Where an interaction appeared, its direction was inconsistent. The study does not test the matrix. Ability here is general cognitive ability measured by tests, not skill on a specific task like the matrix's horizontal axis; motivation includes personality-type measures; job performance was mostly rated by supervisors; and the data are correlational, not experiments with an intervention. On this article's reading, it tests two premises behind the matrix, that the two axes are separate and that both matter. The advice for each quadrant it does not test.

The counter-case: Defenders of the tool could answer with two arguments. The first: the matrix has never claimed to be a theory that predicts performance; it is a frame for a conversation between a manager and the people who report to them, and its value lies in forcing the manager to ask two questions instead of one. The meta-analysis above partly supports this: cognitive ability and motivation are almost independent, so at least for those two, asking each question separately has a basis. The second: the advice for each quadrant has evidence behind it through the tool's close relative; Thompson and Glasø write that the principles of situational leadership are supported when the two sides' ratings agree, and treat this as a step towards making it an evidence-based theory rather than one that merely sounds right. [6] The first argument stands, and the article has nothing to set against it. The second the article accepts in part. It read those two studies through their abstracts; they were carried out in financial organisations and businesses, the later one in Norway, and they concern situational leadership rather than the matrix; the support appears only in the group where the two ratings agree, which means its condition is precisely the step that use of the matrix tends to skip: asking the person being scored. Taken together, there is strong evidence that cognitive ability and motivation are separate and both contribute to performance, which the article extends to the matrix's two axes by inference but which no one has been seen to measure directly; there is moderate, indirect evidence that choosing a management style according to a follower's development level helps when both sides agree on the diagnosis; and within the scope of this article's search, there is no measurement showing that the matrix itself makes outcomes better or worse.

Why this is useful: If ability and motivation add up, as the meta-analysis suggests, there is no need to wait for one axis to be high enough before investing in the other; the data are correlational, so this is the article's inference, not the result of an intervention. Addition is also only a tendency across thousands of people, and it promises nothing for any one person: when a task requires a particular skill, high will cannot stand in for it. For someone in the direct quadrant, pick one axis to raise first this month, whichever is cheaper to raise, then write down one concrete action and how you will know whether it worked. And because motivation measured on the task at hand predicts performance better than motivation measured as personality, go by what the person has done and said about this particular task in the last few weeks, then ask why, rather than by "that's just how he is".

How to Apply It

The skill/will matrix works by splitting the question "why is this person not doing this task well" into two separate diagnoses, can they do it and do they want to, then choosing the amount of direction and monitoring that fits each quadrant, so using it well means keeping those two diagnoses accurate: score task by task, score with the person being scored, separate will from working conditions, and score again after each cycle. The tool fits best when assigning a specific task and deciding how closely to support it; it fits poorly when used to rank people, for year-end appraisals, or to decide rewards and penalties.

  • Put a pairing of person and task on the matrix, not a person: Write the task next to the name on every dot, and give a person as many dots as they have distinct kinds of task, as with Minh in Figure 1. Score skill by what the person has achieved on this particular task, such as how often it came out right or which errors recur; seniority and performance on other tasks do not count towards this score. For a new task with no results yet, write "not enough data" rather than borrowing a high score from the old task, even for the best person on the team; a lack of evidence for high skill is not evidence of low skill, but in terms of how to manage, support the person as you would someone with low skill when the cost of one botched job is higher than the cost of the extra support
  • Have the person being scored rate themselves separately, then discuss the gaps: Both sides write their scores on separate sheets first, then meet to compare. The predictions of situational leadership hold up better when the two ratings agree [4]; but when they still differ after discussion, the self-rating is not the default answer, because in the same authors' later study the supervisor's assessment remained the better basis [6]. Record the gap, each side's grounds, and a date to check it against actual results
  • Before writing "low will", check the conditions of the task: Refusals or missed deadlines are signals to ask why, not a will score: people may turn down a task because they think it unsafe, or impossible to do under current conditions. Look at the workload the person is carrying, the tools, competing priorities, and how this task is paid compared with other work. A test question: if someone equally skilled were put in exactly those conditions, would they be any keener? If not, what needs fixing is the conditions, and a talk about motivation is unlikely to work, because it treats the person when the cause lies in the task. The excite quadrant is only for what remains once those causes have been ruled out
  • Set the rescoring date in advance, and measure by results on the task, not by how many people have moved into the delegate quadrant: The chosen management style may change both axes, and a label left in place may harden into an expectation, the kind that meta-analyses of the Pygmalion effect show can change performance, especially for people of whom little was expected at the start [7]. Counting the people who reach the delegate quadrant fails the way many count-based targets fail: score more leniently and the number looks better while the work stays the same. Measure the outcome of the assigned task itself, before and after, using one definition

Applying It to a Scenario

From April 2027, an eight-person technical team at an internet provider in Biên Hoà, Đồng Nai, takes on home security camera installation alongside its existing internet installations. In the four weeks from Monday 10 May to Sunday 6 June 2027, the team carries out 120 camera installations, and only 84, or 70%, are first-time-right, meaning no return visit within 7 days to fix an installation fault; for internet installations the rate is 95%, 380 of 400 jobs. The team leader sets a goal: in the four weeks from Monday 21 June to Sunday 18 July, weeks 3 to 6 from the start, the first-time-right rate for camera jobs should reach at least 90%, the rate for internet jobs should not fall below 95%, and a results report should go to the branch director before 30 July. The eight technicians are Dũng, Hải, Khoa, Long, Phát, Thịnh, Sơn and Vũ. All figures are illustrative.

  1. Choose the task to score: The team leader scores camera installation only. All eight install internet well, and a single dot per person would put the whole team in the delegate quadrant while the rate on the new task is only 70%
  2. Fix the definitions of both axes in observable terms before scoring: Skill is high when at least 85% of camera jobs are first-time-right, over at least 15 jobs in the four baseline weeks; the team leader chose 85% because it sits close to the team's 90% goal, and it is the team leader's choice. Anyone with fewer than 15 jobs is recorded as "not enough data", not "low", because a lack of evidence for high skill is not evidence of low skill; in terms of management, they are supported for now as if their skill were low, because the team leader judges that one faulty job reaching a customer's home costs more than a few accompanied jobs. Will rests on two things: the number of assigned camera jobs the person asked to swap or turned down in the four baseline weeks, used as a signal to ask why, not as a score; and the person's own answer. The definition of "first-time-right" stays the same for the baseline and the measurement period
  3. Score from both sides, then discuss the gaps: The team leader scores separately, and all eight score themselves separately on paper. The team leader marks four people as low on will: Hải, Phát, Sơn and Thịnh. Two gaps are discussed in one-to-one meetings. Phát's self-score for skill is high, the team leader records not enough data; Phát has done only 12 jobs, under the threshold of 15, but 4 of the 5 faulty jobs failed at the same step, setting up the remote-viewing app on the customer's phone, so the two agree that Phát's skill is low at that particular step, a gap that now has a name. Thịnh's self-score for will is high, the team leader's low because Thịnh has done only 10 jobs; the dispatch schedule shows that Thịnh's area generates few camera orders and that Thịnh has never turned one down, so Thịnh's will is rescored as high
  4. Before writing "low will", check the conditions: The three still marked low on will, Hải, Phát and Sơn, give the same reason: pay. The team leader checks the four baseline weeks. Every job pays 90,000 dong, but a camera job takes 150 minutes on average and an internet job 50, so an hour on camera work earns 36,000 dong and an hour on internet work 108,000 dong. Hải asked to swap 5 camera jobs, Phát 4 and Sơn 6, all for internet jobs. The branch director raises camera pay to 270,000 dong a job from 7 June, which equals 108,000 dong an hour. Asked again afterwards, Hải and Phát move to high will. Sơn still doesn't want the work: he doesn't want to work on a tall ladder on the front of a townhouse. That is a reason about the safety conditions of the task, so the team leader records it next to the score rather than just writing "low will". The team leader and Sơn agree that Sơn will take no camera jobs this period and do more internet jobs instead
  5. Place each person in a quadrant and choose a style for each: Delegate: Dũng and Hải; each coaches two or three others, and the team leader meets the two of them once a week, as a mentor. Guide: Khoa, Long, Phát, Thịnh and Vũ, with Thịnh and Vũ treated as low on skill for now because there is not enough data; for the first two weeks each accompanies Dũng or Hải on 4 jobs, then works alone with a 12-point checklist and posts photos of the installed equipment to the team chat before leaving the customer's home; Phát also gets one extra session on app setup. Nobody remains in the excite quadrant once pay has been fixed. Nobody in the direct quadrant takes camera jobs
  6. Measure by results, with the same definition and the same period length, then check whether the two periods are comparable: In the four measurement weeks, the team carries out 150 camera jobs, 138 of them first-time-right, 92%; internet jobs reach 361 of 380, 95.0%. Camera jobs at townhouses, which need outdoor cabling and are harder than apartment jobs, make up 60 of 120 jobs in the baseline and 78 of 150 in the measurement period, 50% and 52%, so the rise did not come from the team taking fewer townhouse jobs. Because Sơn has left camera work, the team leader recalculates the baseline without him: 79 of 110 jobs, 71.8%
  7. Rescore skill against the agreed threshold, then send the report: On 19 July, Khoa (18 of 20), Long (16 of 18), Thịnh (20 of 22) and Vũ (18 of 20) all clear the 85% threshold over at least 15 jobs and move to the delegate quadrant. Phát reaches 12 of 16, 75%, and stays in the guide quadrant; 3 of Phát's 4 faulty jobs are still at the app setup step, so the next coaching session focuses on that step alone. Sơn's will for camera work will be asked about again next period. The report, including the table below, goes to the branch director on 23 July

Goal met: in the four measurement weeks, the first-time-right rate for camera jobs was 92%, above 90%; the rate for internet jobs held at 95.0%; the report went out on 23 July, before the 30 July deadline. The result came with two conditions: two technicians good enough to coach five others, and a branch director willing to spend an extra 27 million dong in the measurement period. It also comes with three limitations, and the contribution of the matrix alone cannot be separated out. Three changes happened at once, new pay, coaching by the strongest technicians and a photo checklist, so the scenario cannot say which change raised the rate by how much; the matrix's job here was to make the team leader ask why will was low before choosing a style, and that question is what put pay on the table. Sơn left camera work, but the baseline without him was still only 71.8%, so the rise did not come from removing a weak performer. Each person did only 16 to 28 jobs in a single four-week period, so four people clearing the threshold is one measurement and does not yet show that their skill is stable. As for the 27 million dong, which tripled the pay per camera job, it was the branch director's decision, and the scenario has not set it against the cost of the return visits avoided or the margin per job: it shows that the quality goal was met, not that the move paid for itself. The matrix only helped show that three people's "low will" boxes contained a pay gap; whether to close it is a separate question.

Summary

Key Point: The skill/will matrix splits the question of why someone is not doing a task well into two separate diagnoses, can they do it and do they want to, then chooses the amount of direction and monitoring by quadrant; what goes on the matrix is one person with one task, not one person. The most counter-intuitive point is that the familiar belief that ability and motivation multiply, so that when one is low a high level of the other cannot rescue performance, is not supported by the evidence: in Van Iddekinge and colleagues' meta-analysis of correlational data, cognitive ability and motivation are almost independent and combine mainly by adding, with the interaction accounting for only 9.4% of the differences in performance the model explains. The weak point is the scoring: for situational leadership, the matrix's closest relative, Thompson and Glasø found that predictions held up better when the supervisor's rating matched the follower's self-rating. Within the scope of this article's search, no study was found that measures the matrix itself. The practical rules that follow: score task by task, have the person being scored rate themselves and then discuss the gaps, check the conditions of the task before writing "low will", and set a rescoring date in advance, measuring by results on the task.
