RACI Matrix

Each row of a RACI needs exactly one A, and that rule alone exposes work nobody owns and decisions two people both think are theirs; but the chart records who, not what authority that person holds or what counts as done

Published: November 22, 2026

Definition

What Is a RACI Matrix

Suppose a 15-person children's book publisher is getting a new title ready for print, and in the meeting everyone nods at "you'll take care of that part, right?". Two weeks later the book comes off the press with a spelling mistake on the back cover: the editor and the illustrator both read the proof, and each assumed the other was the one who signed it off for print. A RACI matrix is a way of writing those nods down. Tasks go in the rows, roles go in the columns, and each cell takes a letter saying where that role stands on that task. R is responsible: the people who do the work, and a task can have several. A is accountable: the one who signs off and where the buck stops, exactly one person per task. C is consulted: people who give input before the work is signed off. I is informed: people who are kept up to date but do not contribute to the task. Two row rules come with it: every row has exactly one A and at least one R; C and I are there or not depending on the task. [1]

In Vietnamese, "responsible" and "accountable" are usually translated by the same phrase, and that is precisely the blur the matrix is built to separate: the person who does a task and the person who answers for its outcome can be two different people. This article writes A/R in a cell where one role both does the work and is accountable for it. Within the scope of this article's search, no primary source was found saying who devised the matrix or when. What can be checked is that it has been in the professional literature for a long time. Mind Tools' introduction carries a credit to the Project Management Institute's PMBOK Guide, fifth edition (2013). [1] ISACA, the body behind the IT governance framework COBIT, writes that IT practitioners have used RACI charts for decades and that COBIT has had RACI charts across several versions. [2] [3] This article uses Mind Tools' definition of the four letters and its two row rules for Figure 1 and for every test question.

The matrix sits close to two other tools in the series. A work breakdown structure answers "what does the project consist of", and its work packages can serve as the rows of a RACI; the RACI adds "who stands where on each package". The skill/will matrix also works one task at a time, but asks a different question: how should the person already given the task be managed. Put the publisher's five tasks for getting the title ready for print on the grid and you get Figure 1.

Why We Need It

A task that needs several pairs of hands tends to fail in one of two ways. The first is a gap: everyone assumes someone else has it, like the back-cover mistake at the publisher. The second is an overlap: two people each think the decision is theirs, like the director and the distribution team both setting the print run, while the person doing the work gets two different instructions. Neither usually shows in the meeting where work is handed out, because "you'll take care of that part" sounds clear to the person saying it, and clear in a different way to each person hearing it.

The tool's mechanism is to force every cell of the task-by-role grid to take a letter, or to be left blank on purpose. Once it has to be written down, an assumption sitting in one person's head becomes something other people can read and argue with. The one-A-per-row rule turns gaps and overlaps into two visible errors: a row with no A, a row with two. Reading down the columns shows a third kind: one person holding too many As to keep an eye on them all, or one person turning up in every row. [1] Separating C from I has a reason too: C is a two-way exchange and the work waits for that person; I is one-way and the work waits for nobody.

The case for clarifying roles has a figure behind it one level up. In the meta-analysis by Gilboa and colleagues in the Research section, pooling 114 samples and 22,258 people, role ambiguity as employees perceive it had a correlation of −0.24 with job performance after correcting for measurement error (0 is no relationship, −1 a perfect inverse one), and was one of the two stressors most strongly related to performance out of the seven examined. [4] Another line of research, on social loafing, shows that people tend to put in less effort when their contribution is pooled into a group product, and that whether individual contributions can be evaluated is one of the strongest influences on how much they loaf. [5] On this article's reading, a grid that names who does the work and one person accountable for each task touches both mechanisms, with one exception: a row with several Rs pools those people's contributions back into one cell, which is exactly the condition the social loafing research describes. Neither figure measures the matrix itself.

What Are Its Limits

By definition the person holding the A is the one who signs off, but an A on paper does not give them the power to spend money, direct people or say "not yet". This limit is inherent: the matrix records names, and there is no step that checks whether the person named holds the authority the letter assumes. When the A lands on someone without that authority, the chart does not catch it, and the A becomes a place to pin the blame later rather than a place where decisions get made.

The default template also has no column for two other things. The first is time: the chart does not say which task comes first or when anything is due; order and deadlines belong to a schedule, such as a Gantt chart. The second is completion criteria: the R says who does the work, not how far it has to go before it counts as done, or who judges that. Users can add columns for both, but those are additions, not part of the four letters. The second gap matters more than it looks: the role ambiguity measured in Gilboa and colleagues' meta-analysis is not only not knowing who does what, but also not knowing what is expected of you, and the authors recommend reducing it with clearer goals, expectations and evaluation criteria. [4] A RACI with every letter in place can answer "who" and still leave "what counts as good enough" untouched.

Third, also inherent: the chart is a snapshot of the day it was drawn up. People change jobs, the structure changes, the project moves to another phase, and the chart still says what it said at the start. Mind Tools treats communicating and updating the chart as the last step of building it [1], so updating is part of the standard method, but nothing in the chart itself reminds anyone to do it.

The rest are errors of use. It is easy to scatter Cs across everyone with a stake, because one more C looks like diligence, when each C is one more person the work has to wait for; Mind Tools puts it bluntly as too many cooks spoiling the broth, even when the extra people only carry a C or an I. [1] A C can also be read as a veto, when the definition only says that person is asked before the work is signed off. And the chart may be filled in by just one person, in which case it records that one person's assumptions and misses exactly what it is most useful for: the places where assumptions differ. How to reduce each of these errors is in How to Apply It.

Examples

Both examples below are constructed scenarios, and both start from a chart that looks rule-abiding: Cs scattered across every department and then read as vetoes, and an A placed on someone without authority over the task. Within the scope of this article's search, no real case was found in which a source records that a RACI matrix was used and states a measured outcome.

Nine Cs for One Fleet Management System

An illustrative scenario: a container haulage company in Hải Phòng, with about 200 trucks, has four weeks to choose fleet management software so it can sign a contract before peak season. The deputy director of operations draws up a RACI for the selection: the IT team is R, he is A, and so that nobody feels left out, the nine other department heads are all C, from dispatch and maintenance to accounting, HR and legal. Every comparison of the three vendors has to go through nine people. Three rounds of comments take seven weeks, many of the comments contradict each other, and the IT team spends most of its time collating opinions instead of testing software. In week eleven, the deputy director picks the vendor that dispatch and maintenance preferred. The head of accounting objects in writing, saying she was "on the chart" but her view on licence costs was ignored, and signing slips another two weeks.

The chart had every letter and one A per row, but the C had been used as a roll call. Nine people consulted means the work waits for nine people, and nobody on the chart had written down that C means being asked before sign-off; the head of accounting read her C as a vote. Dispatch and maintenance, who use the software every day, did belong under C; accounting belonged under C for exactly one part, cost; legal belonged under C if company rules require every contract to go through legal; the other departments needed only an I. COBIT itself, a framework with a RACI chart for every practice, suggests only R and A and leaves C and I for each organisation to set [2]; this article reads that as a sign that those two letters depend more on local circumstances. A test for your own chart: for each C, if that person were dropped, where could the decision go wrong? If you cannot point to anywhere, and no rule, contract or law requires that person to be asked, they are an I.

The Store Manager's A

An illustrative scenario: a chain of 20 bubble tea shops in Ho Chi Minh City draws up a RACI for store operations, and the store manager is A for the row "food safety in the store". In June, the fridge holding fresh milk in one shop in District 3 stops keeping cold enough. The store manager emails the maintenance team four times in three weeks; under the chain's rules, any equipment repair over 2 million dong must be approved by maintenance, with purchasing booking the technician. In week four, district inspectors write up a report on storage temperature. The company disciplines the store manager, on the grounds that she holds the A.

The A was placed where the incident happened, not where the authority to decide sits. The outcome of that row depended on the fridge being fixed in time, and the store manager had no authority to pay for the repair, no authority to book a technician, and no option except to report upwards. Four reports show the escalation route existed; what was missing was someone on the receiving end with a deadline to act. The chart has no cell for recording that mismatch: it only records names. One way to fill it in so that it matches authority is to split the row: the store manager holds the A for checking temperatures and reporting faults the same day, the maintenance team holds the A for repairing equipment within a stated deadline. The test: what decisions does the outcome of this row require, and can the A holder make them, or only ask someone else to?

Some Research on the RACI Matrix

The RACI matrix is used far more widely than the evidence about it would suggest: within the scope of this article's search, no study was found that measures the matrix itself, whether by comparing outcomes between groups that use it and groups that do not, or by surveying how widespread it is. The sign of its reach is its presence in professional frameworks: RACI charts have appeared in COBIT across several versions; in COBIT 2019 the accompanying toolkit has a RACI spreadsheet for each of 231 practices across 40 governance and management objectives, and ISACA describes those charts as an initial view for each organisation to adjust. [2] [3] The closest evidence sits one level up and runs in two strands. The first is the meta-analyses of social loafing, which show effort falling when individual contributions are not seen separately; this article read Karau and Williams' meta-analysis via the abstract. [5] The second is research on role ambiguity, the thing the matrix is built to reduce. The study below belongs to the second strand, and is the only study in this article read in full.

The study: Simona Gilboa, Arie Shirom, Yitzhak Fried and Cary Cooper pooled studies on the relationship between job performance and seven work stressors: role ambiguity, role conflict, role overload, job insecurity, work-family conflict, environmental uncertainty and situational constraints. They searched databases covering 1975 to 2002 and obtained 169 samples with 35,265 employees, 374 effect sizes from 104 published studies and 33 unpublished doctoral dissertations. The mean sample size was 209 people, and 88% of the samples came from English-speaking countries. Role ambiguity, in the original definition by Kahn and colleagues (1964), is not being able to predict what one's actions will lead to; the most widely used measure, by Rizzo, House and Lirtzman (1970), also counts a lack of guidance from outside. Performance was split by source: self-rated, supervisor-rated and objective. Correlations were corrected for sampling error and measurement reliability using the Hunter and Schmidt method. [4]

Findings: With general performance, role ambiguity had an observed correlation of −0.20 and a corrected one of −0.24, across 114 samples and 22,258 people. The corrected correlation was −0.30 with self-rated performance (55 samples, 10,948 people), −0.19 with supervisor-rated performance (51 samples, 9,001 people), and −0.17 with objective assessments (11 samples, 2,458 people); with a further correction for the fact that different supervisors rating the same person often disagree, the figure was −0.24. Role conflict, meaning facing incompatible demands, was much more weakly related: between −0.08 and −0.12 with general, self-rated and supervisor-rated performance. Role overload was weakly related and not statistically significant for those three types of performance. Role ambiguity and situational constraints were more strongly related than every other stressor, for each type of performance. When measured with the Rizzo scale, role ambiguity's relationship with general performance was weaker than with other scales, −0.18 against −0.23 (observed correlations). For most of the correlations, tests showed the results were not consistent across studies, and the range estimated for the true correlation across different settings (the credibility interval) was wide and included zero, meaning there are still unmeasured factors that make the relationship stronger or weaker. [4]

What it shows: The more ambiguous employees find their role, the lower their performance tends to be, to a moderate degree, and that tendency shows up in every source of performance data, objective assessments included. The authors themselves note that most of the pooled studies were cross-sectional, measuring everything at a single point in time, so the direction of causality cannot be known: perceiving one's role as ambiguous may lower performance, and doing badly may also make people see their role as more ambiguous. Among the three main sources, the strongest figure, −0.30, comes from self-rated performance, where the same person answers both questions. The study does not test the RACI matrix. It measures employees' perception of their own role, including knowing what is expected of them and being able to predict what their work will lead to, not whether a chart saying who does what exists.

The counter-case: The defence does not need a direct measurement to have two arguments worth hearing. The first: RACI is cheap. Filling in a chart takes a few hours, and when a factor is related to performance at −0.24 across twenty-two thousand people, the chart only has to reduce ambiguity a little to be worth it. The second: a tool that practitioners have used for decades and that frameworks such as COBIT have built in has already passed a kind of real-world filter. This article accepts part of the first: the cost of drawing up a chart is low, and the premise that clear roles go with better performance has solid correlational evidence. But the cost is not just the session spent filling it in; it is also every extra C the work has to wait for, as the nine-C example above shows. And the −0.24 is about perceived ambiguity in general, including what one is expected to deliver, while the chart only touches "who" directly; within the scope of this article's search, no measurement was found of how much a chart reduces that perception. The second argument this article does not accept: wide use is evidence of popularity, not of effectiveness, and ISACA itself calls COBIT's RACI charts an initial view, leaving each organisation to set the C and I. [3] One point in the data is worth weighing on both sides: role conflict is much more weakly related to performance than role ambiguity. One could hypothesise that a missing A is close to role ambiguity and a double A close to role conflict, but these data cannot say which error costs more: the two constructs in the research are far broader than a single row on a chart, two As also leave the doer not knowing who decides, and a correlation with performance does not measure the damage done by each type of error. What the data do support is a narrower point: ambiguity, one of the two stressors most clearly related to performance out of the seven examined, includes what one is expected to deliver, so making sure every doer knows what counts as done deserves as much care as the one-A rule.

Why this is useful: When you hand out the chart, ask everyone holding an R one question: how will you know this task is done, and who is the person who says it is good enough? If their answer differs from what the A holder thinks, write the completion criterion next to that row. And because, among the three main sources, the strongest figure comes from self-ratings, do not measure whether the chart worked with a survey asking people whether their roles feel clearer; count real things: work held up waiting for someone consulted, work that fell between two people, work redone because people understood "done" differently.

How to Apply It

The RACI matrix works by forcing every cell of the task-by-role grid to take a letter, so the gaps, overlaps and overloads sitting in each person's head appear on paper; the effective way to use it is therefore to keep those letters matched to reality: have several people fill it in so that differing assumptions surface, put the A on someone with the authority the task needs, keep the Cs few, and add what the four letters cannot record, namely what counts as done. The tool fits best with work that several departments contribute to and hand between them; it fits poorly with work one person does from start to finish, where the chart only records what everyone already knows.

  • Have each person fill it in separately, then read along the rows and down the columns: Everyone on the chart puts a letter against every row on their own sheet before the meeting, then the sheets are laid side by side; any row where two people each give themselves the A, or nobody does, is a row to discuss. Once agreed, check that each row has exactly one A and at least one R, and check each column for anyone holding too many As to keep track of or appearing in every row [1]. A chart filled in by one person is always clean, and that is exactly the problem: it has no assumptions to disagree with each other
  • Put the A on someone with the authority the row's outcome requires: For each A, ask: what decisions does this row need to produce its result, and can this person make them? The proof-check row needs the authority to approve for print, not the authority to spend; the row for keeping the fridge cold needs the authority to call out a technician. If the A holder can only report upwards, move the A or split the row by authority, as in separating checking temperatures from repairing the fridge. When two people both claim the A, one way out is to split the row at a threshold already set in the delegation-of-authority rules, such as an amount of money, rather than leaving two As or picking one person at random
  • Keep the C for people without whom the decision could go wrong, or whom the rules require to be asked: For each C, write down where the decision could go wrong without that person, or which rule, contract or law requires them to be asked; if you can write neither, make them an I. Someone whom the rules give the power to approve is not a C: they are the A of a separate approval row. State on the chart that a C is asked before sign-off and has no veto. COBIT likewise suggests only R and A for each practice and leaves C and I for the organisation to set [2]
  • Write the completion criterion next to each row, and judge the chart by real work rather than by how many cells are filled: Add a "done when" column and a deadline column, because role ambiguity in the research includes not knowing what is expected of you, and the authors recommend reducing it with clearer evaluation criteria [4]. Counting the share of rows with exactly one A fails in the way many counting targets fail: every chart hits 100% after one meeting while the work is still late. Measure the outcome indicator of the process itself, before and after, using the same definition, and set a date to review the chart whenever people or structure change

Applying It to a Scenario

A pangasius processing plant in Cần Thơ that exports its products receives quality complaints from importers, such as short net weight or bones in fillets. Under its contracts with major customers, the plant has to send a written resolution within 10 working days of receiving a complaint. In the first quarter of 2027, from 1 January to 31 March, the plant received 30 complaints and sent a resolution within 10 working days for only 12 of them, or 40%. The plant director gives the head of quality assurance (QA) a target: in the third quarter, from 1 July to 30 September, at least 80% of complaints get a resolution within 10 working days, with a report on the results before 15 October. Six roles take part in handling complaints: the sales executive who manages the customer, the head of export sales, the head of QA, the production manager, the head of the cold store, and the plant director. All figures and dates are illustrative.

  1. Find where complaints get held up before drawing the chart: The head of QA goes back over the 18 complaints that missed the deadline in the first quarter and notes the task where each one waited longest. 11 waited longest at tracing the batch, 5 at deciding on compensation, and 2 at waiting for retest results on retained samples, which an outside laboratory runs under a service contract committing to results within 4 working days. The first two are hand-offs between departments, exactly the kind of problem a RACI handles; the third is a supplier's turnaround time, which the chart cannot shorten
  2. List the tasks in the actual order of handling, then have the six people fill in the chart separately: The draft chart has six rows, from receiving the complaint to sending the resolution to the customer. Each person fills in their own sheet before the meeting on 12 April. With the six sheets side by side, two rows clearly disagree. In the batch-tracing row, the head of the cold store gives the A to the head of QA and the head of QA gives it to the head of the cold store: nobody gives the A to themselves, at exactly the point where 11 complaints had waited. In the compensation row, both the head of export sales and the director give the A to themselves. Combining the sheets, the draft chart has 11 Cs
  3. Assign each A by authority, splitting the row where authority divides at a threshold: The A for batch tracing goes to the head of QA, who holds the batch records and can require the cold store to open its temperature logs; the head of the cold store is R. The compensation row is split in two under the plant's existing delegation-of-authority rules: rejecting a complaint or compensating up to USD 5,000 is decided by the head of export sales, anything above USD 5,000 is signed by the director. In the retest row, the head of QA holds A/R for sending the sample, following up and receiving the result; the test itself is done by the outside laboratory, sits outside the chart, and is governed by the service contract. The chart has seven rows, each with one A
  4. Ask of each C where the resolution would go wrong without that person: The production manager is a C on compensation but cannot point to where a compensation decision would go wrong without him, so he leaves that row; he is already in the corrective action row, where the cause gets decided. The head of the cold store keeps the C in the corrective action row, because half the short-weight causes in the first quarter were linked to glazing and storage. After the questions, the chart has 3 Cs
  5. Write a completion criterion and a deadline for each row: Each row gets a "done when" column and a deadline in working days, as in the table below. The rows run in sequence, each starting the same day the previous one finishes, so the deadlines add up to 6 days when no retest is needed and 10 days when one is; a complaint needing a retest has no spare day at all, and the laboratory missing its committed deadline is enough to make the complaint late. The definition of "resolution sent within 10 working days" stays the same for the baseline and the measured period: counted from the day the complaint email arrives to the day the resolution email goes out, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and public holidays
  6. Use the chart from 1 July, measure the third quarter with the same definition, then check whether the two periods are comparable: In the third quarter, the plant received 28 complaints and sent a resolution within 10 working days for 24, or 85.7%. The share of complaints needing a retest of retained samples was 9 out of 30 in the first quarter and 9 out of 28 in the third, 30% and 32.1%; the share involving compensation above USD 5,000 was 6 out of 30 and 5 out of 28, 20% and 17.9%. These two variables are not changed by the chart and are close between the two quarters, so they give no sign that the third quarter brought easier complaints; they do not rule out other changes between the two quarters, such as who handled the complaints, which customers raised them, or the type of defect
  7. Read the four late complaints, then send the report: Three of the late complaints needed a retest, and the outside laboratory returned results after more than 4 working days, that is, later than the deadline committed to in its contract; the fourth was in the above-USD 5,000 compensation row and waited 3 days because the director was travelling abroad. The only A on that row was away and the chart named no deputy, so the head of QA proposes adding a delegate for when the director is absent. Target met: in the third quarter, 85.7% of complaints received a resolution within 10 working days, above 80%, and the report went out on 8 October, ahead of the 15 October deadline. The result depended on two conditions: delegation-of-authority rules by amount already existed, which made it possible to split the compensation row, and the head of QA really could require the cold store to open its temperature logs, so the new A on the batch-tracing row came with real authority. The chart's own contribution cannot be separated out: the A on the tracing row, the split compensation row and the deadlines written next to each row all came in at once, so the scenario cannot say which change raised the rate by how much. There are only about thirty complaints a quarter, so one quarter on target is one measurement. Nor does the chart touch the third bottleneck: three of the four late complaints in the third quarter sat with the outside laboratory, the same kind of wait seen in the first step; what needs doing there belongs to the supplier contract or a backup laboratory, not to any letter on the chart. And the complaint delayed by the director's absence shows the one-A rule at work: a single accountable person is also a single person the work has to wait for.

Summary

Key Point: A RACI matrix forces every cell of the task-by-role grid to take a letter, responsible, accountable, consulted or informed, and the one-A-per-row rule turns gaps and overlaps into visible errors. The most counter-intuitive point is that the closest evidence behind the tool supports only part of it: in Gilboa and colleagues' meta-analysis of correlational data, role ambiguity as employees perceive it goes with moderately lower performance, but that ambiguity includes not knowing what is expected of you, which the four letters do not record. Within the scope of this article's search, no study was found that measures the matrix itself. The practical upshot: have each person fill it in and then compare, put the A on someone with the authority the row's outcome requires, keep the C for people without whom the decision could go wrong or whom the rules require to be asked, and write the completion criterion next to each row, judging the chart by late work and dropped work rather than by how many cells are filled.
