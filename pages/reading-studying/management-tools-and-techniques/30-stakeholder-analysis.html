Stakeholder Analysis

The power/interest grid allocates engagement effort by a deliberate choice; it does not show who is opposed, and people heavily affected but holding little power tend to land in the quadrant that is only kept informed

Published: November 24, 2026

Definition

What Is Stakeholder Analysis

Suppose the leadership of a state primary school with 1,100 pupils plans to move afternoon dismissal from 4:30 to 5:00 p.m., starting next semester. The meeting that discusses it has the principal, two deputy principals and the heads of the subject departments. Not in the room are the people whose whole day those thirty minutes rearrange: parents who will have to ask to leave work earlier to collect their children, the guard who will have to stay later, the bus operator who will have to redo its timetable. Stakeholder analysis is a way of bringing those absent people to the table before the decision is settled. A stakeholder, in R. Edward Freeman's 1984 definition, is any group or individual who can affect, or is affected by, the achievement of the organisation's objectives. [1] Mind Tools' introduction splits the analysis into three steps: identify your stakeholders; prioritise them by placing each on a two-axis grid, their power over the work and their interest in it; then understand the key stakeholders, what they think of the work and how best to engage them. [2]

The grid has four quadrants, and each comes with a level of engagement. High power and high interest is Manage Closely: engage these people fully and make the greatest effort to satisfy them. High power and low interest is Keep Satisfied: put in enough effort to keep them satisfied, but not so much that they tire of the message. Low power and high interest is Keep Informed: inform them adequately and talk to them to make sure no major issues are arising. The last is Monitor, where it is enough to keep an eye on people without burdening them with too much communication. In step three, Mind Tools suggests colour-coding each stakeholder by stance: green for advocates and supporters, red for blockers and critics, orange for the neutral. [2]

The grid's origins are messier than its tidy shape suggests. Mind Tools credits it as adapted from a paper Aubrey Mendelow gave at the International Conference on Information Systems in 1981 [2], but the abstract of that paper describes a model linking environmental scanning to two other variables: the dynamism of the environment, and the power of the stakeholder relative to the organisation. [3] Interest does not appear in the abstract; this article has read only the abstract, so it does not claim the full paper never mentions it. In his 2004 review of stakeholder identification and analysis techniques, John Bryson presents the power versus interest grid as described in detail in Colin Eden and Fran Ackermann's 1998 book, under four different names: players, who have both an interest and significant power; subjects, who have an interest but little power; context setters, who have power but little direct interest; and the crowd, with little of either. Bryson spells out that "interest" here is meant in a political sense, a stake in the issue; mere inquisitiveness does not count. [1] This article uses Mind Tools' three steps and quadrant names for Figure 1 and for every test question.

The nearest tool in the series is the RACI matrix, and the two answer different questions. A RACI records who is responsible, accountable, consulted and informed for each task inside a plan. Stakeholder analysis asks who, inside or outside the plan, can make it succeed or fail, who will bear its consequences, and how far each party needs to be engaged. It also stops short of asking what each group will have to change in its day-to-day work; that question belongs to assessing the impact of the change. Placing the nine parties to the dismissal-time decision on the grid gives Figure 1.

Why We Need It

A decision discussed carefully among the people present can still fail among the people absent. Parents learn the new dismissal time from a notice, the guard learns it from the duty roster, the bus operator learns it when the old route no longer fits the clock; by then the decision is settled, and every adjustment costs more than it would have if it had been raised at the start. The failure is usually not a lack of goodwill. Planners see whoever is nearby, whoever is speaking up, whoever is sitting in the room, and no one else.

The mechanism is two passes of forcing. The first forces a wide list before any choosing: Freeman's definition counts those affected, not only those who can affect, so the starting list is longer than the meeting's guest list. The second forces each party onto two axes, relative to the others, so who gets how much effort becomes a choice that can be written down, argued over and revisited, instead of a habit nobody names. Step three adds what the two axes lack, each party's stance, obtained by asking them. [2]

The reason to do this before deciding has a figure one level up, drawn from observational data with no control group. In Paul Nutt's study of 356 decisions, described under Research, decisions implemented by edict, without consulting the people who had a stake in the change, were in full use two years later in only 35% of cases; when the manager formed a task force of the people involved to work on it, that rate was 73%, and when managers went to each key person to show why change was needed, it was 92%. [4] Bryson cites a later analysis by Nutt of 400 strategic decisions, which concludes that about half failed, in large part because decision makers did not attend to the interests and information held by key stakeholders. [1] These figures measure how managers treated the people with a stake; drawing a grid lies outside what was measured.

What Are Its Limits

The first thing the grid cannot answer is who is for and who is against. The two axes of the default template are power and interest. A parents' committee with high interest may want the new dismissal time more than anyone, or may be drafting a letter of objection, and both cases sit on the same dot. Mind Tools adds stance through colour in step three [2], which makes stance an addition, present only when the person drawing the grid bothers to ask.

The second limit is inherent, built into how the quadrants divide. The grid allocates engagement by power, so people heavily affected but with little power land in Keep Informed, where the advice is to inform them adequately and talk to them so that no major issues arise; asking them before the decision is not part of that advice, even though Mind Tools notes that this group can often be very helpful with the detail of the work. [2]

Who counts as a stakeholder at all is itself a choice. Bryson points out that definitions of stakeholders differ in how inclusive they are: for Eden and Ackermann, anyone without the power to directly affect the organisation's future is not a stakeholder; Bryson advises starting with an inclusive definition that counts even the nominally powerless, and writes that choosing which stakeholders are key is inherently political and has ethical consequences. He also writes plainly that stakeholder analysis can be used to advance causes many people would not see as serving the common good, and that it is never a substitute for ethical practice. The grid has no axis for the legitimacy of a claim; when ranking stakeholders, Bryson advises also weighing each party's power, legitimacy and attention-getting capacity, following Mitchell and colleagues (1997). [1]

Third, also inherent: the grid is a relative snapshot taken when it is drawn. In the construction Bryson describes, labels are moved around until the whole group is satisfied with each stakeholder's relative position [1], which makes every position the judgement of a group of people at one moment. Once the plan runs, power and interest shift with it, sometimes because of the plan itself: a group with little interest becomes very interested the moment its working hours are changed. For the participation planning matrix, another technique in his article used to assign each party to an intended level of participation, Bryson writes in an explicit step to revise the matrix as the change unfolds [1]; nothing on the grid prompts that step.

The rest are errors of use. Power is easily read straight off the organisation chart, while the people who carry a decision out hold powers written down nowhere: going slow, working to rule, quitting, or complaining to someone who does have authority. Parties are easily lumped under names that are too broad, such as "parents" or "the state"; Bryson, citing Huxham, calls a name like "the Government" a phantom stakeholder, because what holds a real stake and real power is a specific part inside it [1], while Mind Tools reminds readers that stakeholders can be organisations, but ultimately you must communicate with people. [2] The grid may be drawn by one person, in which case it records how one person sees other people's power. And a grid listing people by name with the label "monitor" is a sensitive document: Bryson notes that the preliminary analysis is typically back-room work, and that the process may stop because the analyses are too sensitive. [1] How to reduce each error is covered under How to Apply It.

Examples

Both examples below are constructed scenarios, and both start from a grid drawn by the book: in the first, power read off the organisation chart pushes a group that can sink the decision down into Keep Informed; in the second, high interest is read as support. Bryson mentions a state department of natural resources in the United States that used the basic analysis technique to bring about major change [1], but only in one sentence, with no figures; within the scope of this article's search, no real case was found with a readable source stating that a power/interest grid was used and produced a measured result.

A Hundred and Eighty Nurses in Keep Informed

An illustrative scenario: a 300-bed private general hospital in Nha Trang plans to move nurses on six inpatient wards from three 8-hour shifts to two 12-hour shifts, so that each patient goes through two handovers a day instead of three. The project team draws the grid: the director, the heads of department and the finance office in Manage Closely; the provincial health authority in Keep Satisfied; the 180 nurses in Keep Informed, because they care a great deal but none of them can approve a roster; patients in Monitor. The plan for engaging nurses is one briefing session and the new roster sent out two weeks ahead. In the first two months of the new roster, 23 nurses resign, against 7 in the same two months of the previous year, most of them parents of young children who cannot manage a 12-hour shift. The hospital has to hire agency nurses, and in the third month returns two wards to the old roster.

The grid is not wrong about approval rights: no nurse can sign off a roster. It is wrong about the kind of power it measures. No single nurse has a veto, but the group as a whole can leave, and a roster only runs when someone is there to work the shift. A briefing plus a roster sent down is, in Nutt's classification, implementation by edict, the group with the lowest rate of decisions still in full use after two years in his study [4]; placing this case in that group is this article's reading. A test for your own grid: for each party in the two low-power quadrants, if they disagree, what can they do, leave, go slow, work to rule, or take it to whom? If the answer is enough to make the decision fail, their power has been set lower than it really is.

Three Green Dots After the Kick-Off

An illustrative scenario: a confectionery company in Long An plans to switch the plastic wrapper of its wafer line to paper with a biodegradable film, in time to reach a supermarket chain's shelves in September. After the kick-off meeting, the marketing project lead draws the grid and colours it in: the head of quality assurance, the head of purchasing and the plant director are all in Manage Closely, and all three are coloured green, because all three attended the whole meeting and asked the most questions. The head of quality assurance had asked how long the new wrapper keeps the wafers crisp in southern Vietnam's humidity; nobody could answer, and the question went into the minutes as a comment. In August, quality assurance refuses to sign the release of the first batch because the shelf-life test has not run long enough. The product misses its shelf slot and is two months late.

The colours on the grid were inferred from interest, and high interest was read as support. The person who asked most at the kick-off turned out to be the person with the most reasons to block, and the question about crispness was a release condition, not a comment. Mind Tools places the question about each party's current opinion in step three, and suggests asking stakeholders directly [2]; this grid skipped that step and took its colours from step two. The test: for each colour on the grid, who asked that person, what did they answer, and what would make them change colour?

Some Research on Stakeholder Analysis

Within the scope of this article's search, no study was found that measures the grid itself, whether how widely it is used or what it achieves. The sign of its influence is that it has become a family of techniques: Bryson's 2004 review presents fifteen stakeholder identification and analysis techniques, grouped by four functions of public-sector strategic management, with the power versus interest grid one of five techniques for organising participation [1]; Mind Tools calls the analysis the first step in stakeholder management. [2] That reputation does not necessarily come with skilled use: Bryson himself notes that, at the time he was writing in 2004, stakeholder analysis was either not taught, or taught in a very limited way, in schools of public policy, administration and planning, and that practitioners knew less about these techniques than they should. The evidence on effectiveness is thinner still, and Bryson, an advocate of the tool, says so too: very little research has been published on which techniques work best under which circumstances and why, and critics could argue with considerable justification that there is at present no overwhelming body of evidence that stakeholder analyses help produce desirable outcomes. He states his own belief as a hypothesis: strategic management processes that employ a reasonable number of competently done stakeholder analyses are more likely to be successful. [1] The nearest evidence sits one level up, in Paul Nutt's research programme on how managers make and implement decisions, the programme Bryson cites as the main reason to take stakeholders seriously. The 1999 article below was read in full.

The study: Nutt collected 356 decisions made by senior managers in medium to large organisations in the United States and Canada, including public agencies, businesses and non-profits, on matters such as new products, equipment purchases, staffing, pricing, marketing, control systems, construction and customer service. The cases were not randomly selected: a top executive of each participating organisation suggested a decision, then the decision maker and two others who knew it well and were responsible for carrying it out were interviewed separately; only cases where the accounts agreed and were detailed enough were kept. The steps managers took were sorted into tactics, and a colleague who repeated the sorting independently agreed 90% of the time. Implementation had four tactics. Edict is issuing a directive without consulting the people who have a stake in the change. Persuasion is having experts find an option and then using their arguments to sell it. Participation is forming a task force of the people involved, subdivided by how many of those affected are brought in and how much the group is asked to do. Intervention is the manager going to key people and showing that current performance falls short of a credible norm, so that they see the need for change themselves. Success was measured by whether the decision was put to use, at two levels: sustained use after two years, and full use after two years. [4]

Findings: Among decisions that used a single implementation tactic, edict had 132 cases, with 53% sustained after two years and 35% in full use; persuasion had 133 cases, 56% and 47%; participation had 63 cases, 80% and 73%; intervention had 26 cases, 96% and 92%. The two tactics with the lowest rates, edict and persuasion, each accounted for 38% of cases. Within participation, when only some of those affected were brought in and given little to do (9 cases), 67% of decisions were in full use; when the group still held only some of those affected but was asked to propose a solution (34 cases), 77%; when everyone affected was in the group and invited to comment on an aspect of the decision (20 cases), 95%; there was no case in which everyone affected both identified concerns and proposed solutions. Nutt writes that as the proportion of participants to all affected parties falls, the failure rate for participation rises. His table has an internal inconsistency: the four rows add up to 354 cases while the total row reads 344; this article keeps the figures for each row. The table reports no significance test for individual differences. [4]

What it shows: Decisions where managers took the trouble to go and see people, or brought those with a stake in to work on them, were more often still in use two years later than decisions imposed by edict or sold by experts. That is an observed association. Managers chose their own tactics, so a tactic may travel with other differences; Nutt himself records that when middle managers used intervention, 75% of decisions were in full use, against 100% when the user was a top manager. The data come from insiders' accounts of cases the organisations themselves suggested, and some cells are very small: complete participation has only 20 cases. The study does not measure drawing a grid. It measures how managers treated the people with a stake in a decision, which is what the grid is built to prepare for. [4]

The counter-case: The defence has two arms. The first: Nutt's data show exactly the cost of ignoring stakeholders, since edict, the tactic of consulting no one, has the lowest full-use rate, and a grid is the cheapest way to find out whom to consult. The second: the tool is cheap enough not to need strong evidence; Bryson writes that these techniques need only flip charts, marking pens and stick-on labels, plus some time, an expenditure that is minuscule against the cost of failing to attend to key stakeholders. [1] This article accepts nearly all of the second arm: a session drawing a grid costs little, and a decision nobody uses two years on costs a great deal. The first arm it accepts by half. The data support the premise that attending to people with a stake goes with more durable decisions, but they also contain something that pulls against the grid's natural use. The grid is built to concentrate effort in the high-power quadrants and economise in the low-power ones, while within Nutt's own participation category the success rate rises as more of those affected are brought in. A grid used to choose whom to spend less on can lead a manager straight back to token participation, the variety with the lowest rate among the participation types; that is this article's reading, built on cells of only 9 and 20 cases. The other direction has a limit Nutt names himself: decisions that affect very large numbers of people, such as siting prisons or airports, can never hope to involve all the interested parties. [4] As for how far to go, Bryson writes that there are no hard and fast rules, let alone good empirical evidence, on where to draw the line. [1]

Why this is useful: Before treating consultation as done, set the list of people who were asked next to the list of people directly affected, and count what share you reached. Five representatives for two hundred people, invited only to comment, is the exact shape of token participation. When you cannot bring everyone in, do not stop at representatives: in Nutt's table, one case of complete participation is a survey of people living near a proposed prison site to identify their concerns, while surveys that sample only some users' needs he classifies as token participation. [4] The question to ask of your own plan: who among those affected has nobody asked yet, and what is the cheapest way to ask them all?

How to Apply It

Stakeholder analysis works by forcing planners to list widely everyone who can affect or is affected, then place each party on the two axes of power and interest, so engagement effort is allocated by a choice that can be written down rather than by who happens to be nearby; so using it well means keeping the list wide and specific enough, making each position on the grid match the power that actually exists, getting stance by asking, and setting the promise to each party by what the decision does to them, not only by how much power they hold. The tool fits best with decisions that touch several groups with different interests, inside or outside the organisation; it fits poorly with internal work in a small team, where everyone with a stake is already in the room.

  • List specific people or units, and include those affected: Start from an inclusive definition, as Bryson advises, then break lumped names such as "parents", "workers" or "the authorities" into groups with different interests and specific people you can actually talk to [1] [2]. The test: if this decision goes ahead, who will have to change their hours, their work or their money, and are they on the list yet
  • Let the whole group set relative positions, note the basis of power beside each dot, and keep the draft within a small group: Each person writes stakeholder names on labels, the labels go onto the grid in turn, then the group moves them until it agrees on relative positions, as Bryson describes [1]; beside each dot, note what that party's power concretely is: signing off, spending money, leaving, slowing things down, or complaining to whom. Power read only from the organisation chart is where grids most often go wrong, because the people who carry out a decision hold powers that are not on the chart. A grid naming people with the label "monitor" can do harm if it is passed around, so the draft stays with the planning group, in line with what Bryson notes about the preliminary analysis
  • Ask about stance directly instead of inferring it from interest: For each party in the two top quadrants, ask the questions Mind Tools suggests in step three: what they gain or lose from the outcome, what information they want and through which channel, whether their current opinion rests on good information, and what would change that opinion [2]. Colour only after asking. The obvious approach, colouring right after the kick-off by who asked the most, fails because someone asking a lot may be stating a condition that has not been met
  • Set the promise by what the decision does to each party, revise the grid at each milestone, and measure by whether the decision is used: For each party, choose a level on the participation spectrum Bryson adapts from the International Association for Public Participation (IAP2): inform, consult, involve, collaborate, empower, each level a different promise [1]. Parties heavily affected but with little power are at least consulted before the decision, even if their quadrant only says Keep Informed. Revise the grid whenever the plan passes a milestone or a new party appears. Counting meetings held or notices sent fails in the familiar way of every counting target, the number met while the decision is still abandoned; measure as Nutt did, whether the decision is still in use, and in full use, after a set period [4]

Applying It to a Scenario

An export furniture factory in Tây Ninh has 800 workers. The finishing and packing shop, with 150 workers, sits on the east side of the site, about 30 metres from the perimeter wall that borders a residential neighbourhood, and the gate where container trucks load out is also on the east side. To meet its year-end orders, the factory needs to add a night shift, from 10 p.m. to 6 a.m., in this shop: 60 positions a night, six nights a week from Monday night to Saturday night, for eight weeks from Monday night, 7 June, to Saturday night, 31 July 2027, that is, 48 nights. In 2026 the factory tried a night shift in this same shop and had to stop after 18 nights. The director gives the head of HR a target: the night shift runs all 48 nights with no night stopped, on average at least 90% of positions are filled by someone working the full shift, and the results are reported before 10 August. The fill rate is defined as the total number of full shifts worked divided by the number of nights times 60. All figures and dates are illustrative.

  1. Write down the decision, then reread why the last attempt stopped: The 2026 records show that the 18 nights that ran had 756 full shifts worked out of 1,080 position-nights (one position for one night), a fill rate of 70% by the definition above. The night shift stopped after the commune People's Committee received a noise complaint signed by 14 households along the east wall and asked the factory to halt night work until an inspection was done. The stakeholder list in the 2026 plan had five lines: the director, the customer, the shop manager, team leaders and "workers"; no households, no bus operator, no security team
  2. List widely, and split lumped names into groups with different interests: On 17 May, a five-person planning group, made up of the head of HR, the manager of the finishing and packing shop, the chair of the factory's trade union, the head of administration and a team leader, lists 11 parties. "Workers" is split into two groups, workers with children under 6 and the rest, because the 2026 timesheets show the first group missed night shifts more often. "Households" is written as the 14 households along the east wall, represented by the head of the neighbourhood; "the authorities" is written as the commune People's Committee officer responsible for the environment. The list also adds the bus operator, the catering contractor and the security team
  3. Place each party on the grid and note the basis of its power: Each person places name cards on a grid drawn across four flip chart sheets, then the group moves the cards until it agrees, giving the table below. The longest discussion is about the 14 households: on paper they have no power over the factory, but in 2026 their complaint was enough for the commune to halt the night shift, so the group puts them in Manage Closely. The bus operator goes into Keep Satisfied: it does not care how many shifts the factory runs, but without a 6 a.m. run many workers cannot do the night shift. Individual workers have little power, but the 60 positions fill only if enough people are willing to come, so both worker groups are placed close to the midline of the power axis. The version of the grid with people's names stays within the five-person group
  4. Ask about stance and conditions directly, before colouring: On the afternoon of Saturday, 22 May, at the neighbourhood community hall, the 14 households name the two sources of last year's noise: two extraction fans on the east side of the spray booth roof, and container trucks entering the east gate after 11 p.m.; 9 households say they will object if those two sources remain, 5 have no view. HR asks all 150 workers in the shop: 41 have children under 6, and the most often mentioned reason for missing night shifts the previous year was having no bus home at 6 a.m., because the buses ran only on the morning and afternoon shift times. 74 people sign up for the night shift if there is a bus, 66 without young children and 8 with. The union chair proposes that workers with children under 6 not be rostered on nights unless they sign up themselves
  5. Set the promise by what the decision does to each party, and check each measure against what went wrong last time: For the 14 households, the promise is to consult and act: from 10 p.m., container trucks use the west gate; the two east-side extraction fans are fitted with silencers; the night shift supervisor's phone number goes to every household; and the group arranges to meet again after two weeks of night shifts. For workers, the two groups sit in Keep Informed but receive the promise to consult: the night shift takes only volunteers, and the bus operator signs an addendum adding a 9:15 p.m. run and a 6:10 a.m. run, at a cost the director has approved. The commune officer receives the plan and the noise measures before the shift starts; the customer receives a weekly shipping plan; the catering contractor and the security team receive the roster and the number of night meals. Each problem recorded in 2026 has a measure facing it: the noise complaint has the new gate and the silencers, the 70% fill rate has the morning bus and the volunteer-only rule
  6. Revise the grid after two weeks of running: The follow-up meeting with the neighbourhood on the afternoon of Saturday, 19 June, raises no new noise complaints; one household complains that a car park floodlight shines into its house, and the light is re-aimed that same week. The grid changes in one place, a change the plan itself caused: moving container trucks to the west gate means the west-gate guards have to open the gate and check trucks all night, so the security team moves from Monitor to Keep Informed, and the head of administration adds a guard to the west gate on the night shift
  7. Measure the 48 nights by exactly the definition used for 2026, then send the report: The night shift runs all 48 nights with no night stopped, with 2,640 full shifts worked out of 2,880 position-nights, a fill rate of 91.7%, as in the second table. The first week reaches only 88.3%, while the two new bus runs settle their times; from the second week on, every week is above 90%. To compare with 2026 over the same length, the first three weeks of 2027, the same 18 nights as the year before, reach 974 shifts out of 1,080 position-nights, about 90.2%, against 70%. The commune People's Committee receives no complaint about the factory in the eight weeks, against one complaint signed by 14 households in 2026. The number of night positions, the shop and the night allowance under the factory's rules are the same in both years, so the higher fill rate does not come from changing the number of positions or raising the allowance. The report goes to the director on 6 August

Target met: the night shift ran all 48 nights with no night stopped, at a fill rate of 91.7%, above 90%, and the report went out on 6 August, before the 10 August deadline. The result rested on three conditions: the bus operator accepted two extra runs at a price the director could approve; the west gate was wide enough for container trucks at night; and the number of volunteers, 74, exceeded the positions needed, 60. If an order needed 100 positions, the volunteer-only rule might not find enough people, and the promise to workers with young children would have to be renegotiated with them. The contribution of the analysis alone cannot be separated out: the bus, the volunteer-only rule, the new gate and the silencers all came in at once, and 2027 differed from 2026 in other ways too, such as the orders and the very people working nights. Eight weeks is one measurement; that the 14 households did not complain for eight weeks does not show they could live with a night shift all year. And the grid changed while the plan was running, at the security team, because of one of the plan's own measures: the drawing of 17 May was true for that day, not for good.

Summary

Key Point: Stakeholder analysis forces planners to list widely everyone who can affect or is affected, place each party on the two axes of power and interest, then ask the key parties where they stand, so engagement effort is allocated by a choice that can be written down instead of by who happens to be nearby. The most counter-intuitive point is that the nearest evidence behind the tool pulls against its natural use: across the 356 decisions Nutt studied, when managers formed participation task forces, decisions were more often in full use two years later when more of those affected were brought in, whereas the grid is built to economise on those with little power. As for evidence that stakeholder analysis in general helps produce good outcomes, Bryson, an advocate of the tool, says there is no overwhelming body of it, and within the scope of this article's search, no study was found that measures the grid itself. The way of using it that follows: list specific people and include those affected, note the basis for each position on the grid, including powers that are not on the organisation chart, ask about stance instead of guessing, consult those heavily affected but with little power before deciding, and measure engagement by whether the decision is still in use.
