Change Impact Assessment

The assessment turns a change into yesterday and tomorrow for each group across ten aspects of their work; a score from 0 to 5 says how much the work shifts, not whether the people in the group gain or lose

Published: November 24, 2026

Definition

What Is Change Impact Assessment

Suppose a taxi company in Vinh with 600 cars decides to scrap its radio dispatch desk and move all fare allocation to a ride-hailing app from the start of next quarter. In the meeting minutes, the change fits on one line: "roll out dispatch app". For drivers, that line means a screen on the dashboard, a fare that pops up with a few seconds to accept it, and fares that can now be paid by e-wallet. For the forty dispatchers, it means that answering the phone and reading addresses over the radio, most of their working day, is about to disappear. For regular customers who book by ringing a landline, it may mean a number nobody picks up. Change impact assessment is the work of translating that line in the minutes into what each group of people will have to do differently every day, and how differently, before the change begins. Tim Creasey of Prosci, a change management training and consulting company, calls the translation of a change from the organisational level down to each person's daily work a crucial part of a comprehensive impact assessment, and writes that the same change may barely touch one group while completely disrupting another. [1]

Prosci describes a person's work through ten aspects: processes, the steps taken to reach an outcome; systems, people and software organised to meet a set of objectives; tools, an object or piece of software used for a specific purpose; job roles, what the person does and the competencies it needs; critical behaviours, how an individual or group must respond to a situation; mindsets, attitudes and beliefs; reporting structure, who reports to whom; performance reviews, how results are measured and assessed; compensation, the monetary and non-monetary pay received for the work; and location. [1] Prosci's Change Impact Canvas turns those ten aspects into a worksheet. Each canvas covers one impacted group and has a box for that group's adoption challenge; each aspect has a "yesterday" box (before the change), a "tomorrow" box (after the change), and a degree-of-impact score from 0 to 5, where 0 is no impact, 3 is moderate impact and 5 is extremely high impact. [2] Andrew Horlick, also at Prosci, calls this the Yesterday-Tomorrow exercise, and points out that working through the whole list defines both what is changing and what is not. [3] At the taxi company, the dispatchers' canvas would score high on job roles, processes and tools; the drivers' canvas would score high on tools and critical behaviours, would carry a score on compensation if the way fares are paid changes, and could still show 0 for their reporting structure.

Within the scope of this article's search, no one was found who coined the name change impact assessment, nor a year in which it appeared. The article uses Prosci's ten aspects, canvas and 0 to 5 scale for every check question, and states plainly that these are the materials of a company that trains and consults on this very method.

The nearest tool in the series is stakeholder analysis. Stakeholder analysis asks who can make a decision succeed or fail, who bears its consequences, and how far to engage each party. Change impact assessment goes further with the groups who will have to live with the change: where their working day shifts, and by how much. The two answers can sit far apart. A group with little power, like the forty dispatchers, may only be promised Keep Informed on the power/interest grid, while on the impact canvas their job role scores 5.

Why We Need It

A change planned at the organisational level easily brings with it a training plan at the same level: everyone attends the same two-hour session on the new app. That session is just enough for drivers, who only need to learn one screen. For dispatchers, who need to know what they will still be doing next month, it answers almost nothing. What breaks is the allocation: training and conversation are spread evenly over groups that are changed unevenly. Creasey puts the gap in an image: we tend to live at 30,000 feet, but change happens at 300 feet. [1]

The mechanism of the tool is three forcing moves. The first forces the change apart by group, because each canvas covers one group only. The second forces yesterday and tomorrow into concrete sentences for every aspect, so "roll out the app" has to become "accept fares on a screen within seconds instead of listening to the radio", and any aspect that does not change is written down as unchanged. The third forces every group onto the same scale, so the question of which group is changed most, and where, gets an answer that can be written down and compared. Creasey lists what the output is for: defining concretely what employees must be able to do at the Ability step of Prosci's ADKAR model, the step that comes after Awareness, Desire and Knowledge; defining what sufficient adoption and proficient use look like, so that adoption can be measured; and answering "how much change management do we need?" with "how much does this change touch the way people do their jobs?". [1] Horlick adds that defining impacts helps identify where resistance will arise. [3]

The reason to look at change from each person's side has a number in the Research section, taken from a cross-sectional survey in a single organisation, with no control group. In Shaul Oreg's study of 177 employees of an organisation moving to a matrix structure, the more people believed the change would increase their influence and standing, the less negatively they evaluated its worth, with a path coefficient of −0.28 (a measure of the strength of a link in a statistical model; the minus sign means the two move in opposite directions); the more they believed their job would become more interesting and autonomous, the less negative their feelings and evaluations of the change. [4] What people think they are about to gain or lose is what this tool was built to see in advance. What the canvas actually records is narrower than that.

What Are Its Limits

The first limit sits in the scale itself, and it is inherent. The canvas's 0 to 5 score measures the size of a change, not its direction. [2] A 5 on compensation can be a new bonus or an allowance being cut, and the canvas records both with the same number. The abstract of a study by Donald Fedor, Steven Caldwell and David Herold of changes in 32 public and private organisations shows why this gap matters: employees' commitment, both to the change itself and to the organisation, is best understood through an interaction of three things at once, namely how favourable the change is for the members of the work unit, how much the work unit changes, and how much the change affects the individual's own job. [5] Size on its own is not enough to predict a reaction. The article has read only the abstract, so it does not state the direction or strength of that interaction.

The ten aspects describe how a person works, and the default template has no row of its own for the three things Oreg measured: the chance of having to look for another job, influence and standing in the organisation, and how challenging, autonomous and interesting the work is. [4] Part of them can be written under job roles or reporting structure, but only if the person filling in the canvas thinks of it.

The canvas also records only the view of whoever fills it in, and who fills it in is a choice. Creasey suggests a change practitioner can start alone by reviewing project documents, then work with the project team; he also suggests having stakeholder participants list yesterday and tomorrow themselves. [1] Taking the first route, the canvas records how the project team imagines the impact, whereas Oreg measured how employees themselves saw the change. [4] Views also diverge among people within the same group. In a study by Caldwell, Liu, Fedor and Herold of 820 employees who had been through a change, according to the abstract, the fairness perceived across the whole work unit was more closely linked to each individual's judgement of the change's fairness when that individual's own job was little affected. [6] Read the other way round, for the people changed most, the general mood of the group says the least about how they think; that is this article's reading.

Another inherent limit lies in the canvas's unit: each canvas covers one group in one change. When an organisation runs three projects in the same quarter, each project may score the same group as moderate, and no canvas shows that the group is taking all three. According to the abstract of a study by Herold, Fedor and Caldwell across 25 different changes, the positive relationship between self-efficacy and commitment to a specific change grew stronger as the amount of simultaneous, overlapping change in the surroundings increased. [7] Other changes under way alter how people react to the change in question, and alter it unevenly from one person to the next; the default template has no place for them.

And the canvas is a snapshot taken when it is filled in. When the design of the change shifts, say a step is automated or a group is moved to other work, the scores shift too, and nothing on the canvas reminds anyone to fill it in again. The rest are faults of use. Groups may be divided along the organisation chart instead of by how their work changes, so a single score for "staff" hides a small group changed far more than the rest. The yesterday and tomorrow boxes may be left blank in a rush to the score, which then has nothing behind it. A high score may be read as an order for more training or communication, when what the group needs may be a different design. How to reduce each of these faults is in How to Apply It.

Examples

Both examples below are constructed scenarios. In the first, a high score is read as a training need while part of the group is losing money; in the second, three projects each score the same group as moderate and nobody puts the three canvases side by side. Horlick describes using the Yesterday-Tomorrow exercise for the move to online teaching during the pandemic, with significant impacts on at least six of the ten aspects [3], but his article gives no figures on outcomes; within the scope of this article's search, no real case was found with a readable source stating that the tool was used and producing a measured result.

A 5 on Compensation, Two Opposite Meanings

An illustrative scenario: a property brokerage in Binh Duong with 120 staff changes how it pays commission. Instead of commission earned on each agent's own deals, there is a bonus pool shared by team, and enquiries are distributed evenly among agents by customer relationship management (CRM) software, instead of going to whoever picks up the phone first. The project team fills in one canvas for "agents" and scores compensation 5, processes 4 and tools 4. Reading those high scores as a learning need, the team runs three sessions on using the CRM and one session explaining the bonus formula. In the three months after the change, 9 of the 30 agents with five or more years of experience leave, a few of them taking regular clients to other firms.

The canvas is right about size: the agents' compensation really does change, and by a lot. It is silent on direction. For new agents, the shared pool and evenly distributed enquiries are a steadier income. For senior agents with a book of regular clients, the same change means losing most of their commission, and a CRM that distributes enquiries means losing their source of clients as well. One score of 5 lumps together two groups whose interests run in opposite directions. Three training sessions answer the question of whether they can use the software, while the senior agents' question is how much they lose. In Oreg's study, the more timely and useful the information about a change was judged to be, the higher, not lower, the behavioural and cognitive resistance, and the author explains that when employees have something to lose, more information may raise resistance [4]; placing this case under that explanation is this article's reading. A check question for your canvas: for every score of 4 or more, who in the group gains, who loses, and are those two groups sitting under a single name?

Three Projects, Each Scoring Store Managers a 3

An illustrative scenario: a fashion chain with 60 stores runs three projects in the second quarter: new tills and point-of-sale software, a new display layout for a fresh collection every two weeks, and moving staff rostering onto an app. Each project has its own canvas, and all three score store managers at 3, moderate impact, because for each project a store manager only has to learn one more thing and coach staff through it. Nobody puts the three canvases side by side. In May, each store manager attends two rounds of training, rebuilds the display four times and draws up the whole month's roster on the new app. Over May and June, 7 of the 60 store managers resign, and the point-of-sale switchover at their stores slips by three weeks.

Each canvas is right about its own project. What went wrong is the unit of assessment: the canvas takes one change as its unit, while the store manager lives with all three at once. Three scores of 3 do not add up to a meaningful number on Prosci's scale, but they are three rounds of learning, doing and coaching in the same month. According to the abstract of the study by Herold, Fedor and Caldwell, simultaneous changes in the surroundings alter how people react to a specific change [7]; that study did not measure resignations, and linking it to the seven store managers here is this article's reading. A check question: for each group on your canvas, which other projects are changing their work in the same period, and who is keeping that list?

Some Research on Change Impact Assessment

Within the scope of this article's search, no study was found that measures the tool itself, whether how widely it is used or how projects that ran an impact assessment compare with projects that did not. The most detailed material on how to use it is Prosci's three documents [1] [2] [3]; they show how a training and consulting company instructs people to use the tool, not how much value the tool creates. The nearest evidence sits one layer beneath the tool, in research on how a change touching each person's own job relates to the way that person reacts. Three studies by the group of Fedor, Caldwell and Herold belong to this line, but the article could read only their abstracts [5] [6] [7]. Shaul Oreg's 2006 study below was read in full.

The study: Oreg surveyed a defence-industry organisation of about 800 employees, mostly technicians and engineers, while it merged two core units. The main change was structural. Before, each person belonged to one project and reported to one project manager; afterwards, each person was assigned to several projects and had at least two bosses, a functional manager and the project managers. About ten months after the change began, while it was still under way, Oreg interviewed 17 people to design the questionnaire, then distributed it anonymously. 236 questionnaires came back, a response rate of 30%; dropping the 59 from people who joined after the change left 177. Respondents were asked whether, when the change was first introduced, they had believed three things would improve, stay the same or get worse, on a five-point scale: power and prestige, such as responsibility over others or the importance the organisation ascribes to one's unit; job security, a single question on the chance of having to look for a new job; and intrinsic rewards, meaning the challenge, autonomy and interest in the work. Resistance was measured in three components of five items each: feelings about the change, the intention to act against it, and the evaluation of whether it is worthwhile and beneficial. The questionnaire also measured trust in management, the stance of people around the respondent, information received about the change, and a dispositional tendency to resist change. [4]

Findings: The factors in the structural equation model together accounted for 43% of the differences between people in affective resistance, 30% in behavioural resistance and 47% in cognitive resistance. The more people believed their power and prestige would improve, the lower their cognitive resistance (coefficient −0.28); believing the job would become more secure went with lower affective resistance (−0.13); believing the work would become more interesting and autonomous went with lower affective resistance (−0.17) and lower cognitive resistance (−0.23). None of these three gain-or-loss factors was significantly related to behavioural resistance. The intention to act against the change went with two other things: trust in management (−0.27) and opposition among the people around the respondent (0.24). Trust in management was the only factor linked to all three components, most strongly to cognitive resistance (−0.42). Information about the change, measured by how timely and useful respondents found the information they had received, went with higher behavioural and cognitive resistance (0.15 for both), contrary to the author's own hypothesis. On the consequences side, behavioural resistance went with the intention to quit (0.20), and affective resistance with lower job satisfaction (−0.17). [4]

What it shows: In one organisation, at one point in time, what employees believed they would gain or lose went with how they felt about and evaluated the change; whether they intended to act against it went with trust in management and the stance of those around them. These are associations in a single survey, collected after the change had run for ten months, with retrospective questions about the start. Oreg himself writes that the design does not allow causal conclusions, and the response rate was only 30%. He did check for the bias that comes from measuring every variable in the same questionnaire, using a marker variable, and the significant correlations remained after removing that bias. The study does not measure the impact canvas. It measures what the canvas was built to anticipate, but measures it from the employees' side and with a direction of gain or loss, two things Prosci's canvas lacks. [4]

The counter-case: The defence has two arms. The first: Oreg's data show exactly why the tool exists; what a person is about to lose at work goes with how they react, and a canvas that walks through ten aspects for each group is a systematic way of knowing in advance who is about to have what changed, rather than waiting for resistance to surface. The second: the size score was never meant to replace asking; Prosci uses it to decide how much change management is needed according to how much the change touches people's work [1], and a group that is changed a great deal, whether it gains or loses, needs more support to be able to do the new work. This article accepts nearly all of the second arm: a large impact, even a favourable one such as a group being freed from manual data entry altogether, still takes time to learn. The first arm it accepts by half. Oreg supports the premise that what people think they will lose is linked to how they react; but what he measured was the direction of gain or loss, answered by employees themselves, whereas the canvas records a size scored by whoever fills it in. The same study also holds something that pulls against the natural use of the tool. If a high score leads to more communication, it is worth knowing that in Oreg's sample, information judged more timely and useful went with higher resistance, not lower, and he explains this by employees having something to lose. This is a coefficient of 0.15 in one organisation, and Oreg himself raises the possibility that the relationship between information and resistance is not a straight line, with both too little and too much information doing harm [4]; the article builds no rule on that number, and uses it only to say that a high score does not yet tell you what to do.

Why this is useful: Next to every row on your canvas with a score other than 0, including the low ones, add a column recording what that group gains or loses, written in their own words after you have asked them, not as the project team's guess. Then reread the plan for the rows marked as a loss: is the measure you intend to use to say more, or to change the design so they lose less, offset the loss, and say plainly what cannot be offset? A question to ask yourself: if the people in that row read the "gain or loss" column you wrote about them, would they agree?

How to Apply It

Change impact assessment works by forcing an organisation-level change to be translated into yesterday and tomorrow for each group on each aspect of their work, then scoring its size on a single scale, so the effective way to use it is to divide groups by how their work actually changes, keep a yesterday-and-tomorrow sentence behind every score, add what the scale lacks, the direction of gain or loss as the group itself states it, look at each group across every change in the same period, and use the result to change the design or the support rather than only to ration communication. The tool fits best with changes that touch many groups in many different ways, such as new software, a new structure or a new way of paying people; it fits poorly with a small change touching a single group, where asking that group directly is enough.

  • Divide groups by how their work changes, and write yesterday and tomorrow before scoring: Start from the list of impacted parties, then split any group that will work in two different ways after the change into two canvases, even if they share a job title on the organisation chart. For each aspect, write two concrete sentences, what they do yesterday and what they do tomorrow, and only then score; any aspect that does not change is written down as unchanged, as Horlick advises [3]. A score with no yesterday and tomorrow behind it is a score nobody can argue with, and nobody can check either
  • Ask the group itself about the direction of gain or loss, including things outside the ten aspects: A draft filled in from project documents, the starting point Creasey suggests [1], is only half the job. Take the draft to a short working session with a few people from each group, read each row aloud, ask whether that row is a gain or a loss for them, then ask three more questions the default template has no row for: is my job safe, how does my influence change, is the work still worth doing [4]. The obvious shortcut, letting the project team fill in the gain-or-loss column itself, fails because it repeats exactly the gap in the canvas: the size is scored by whoever fills it in, and the direction is guessed by them too
  • Lay the canvases of every project in the same period side by side, group by group: Keep a list arranged by group, not by project: in a given quarter, which changes is this group taking, and in which week does each one land? According to the abstract of the study by Herold, Fedor and Caldwell, simultaneous changes in the surroundings alter how people react to a specific change [7]. Do not add the projects' scores into a total: the 0 to 5 scale is ordinal, and two 3s do not make a 6 on any scale; read the list to postpone a change, or to keep two rounds of learning out of the same month
  • Use the result to change the design and the support, fill the canvas in again at each milestone, and measure by adoption: For each row marked as a loss, before thinking about communication, ask whether the design can change so they lose less, whether the loss can be offset, and say plainly what cannot. Fill the canvas in again when the design changes or a new group comes to light during the rollout. Counting training sessions or notices sent can hit the target while the work still fails; Creasey uses the ten aspects to define what sufficient adoption and proficient use mean [1], so measure exactly that behaviour in each group, such as the share of work done the new way, using a definition that stays the same from start to finish

Applying It to a Scenario

A building cleaning contractor in Ho Chi Minh City has 196 cleaners working across 15 office buildings, with one team leader per building, plus 4 payroll accountants at head office. At present, each team leader signs a paper attendance book every day and sends it to head office at the end of the week, the accountants key it into a spreadsheet by hand, and each building's management signs a paper inspection sheet after every shift. From Monday, 1 March 2027, the company moves to an app: cleaners scan a QR code posted in the building when they start and finish a shift, photograph the area once it is done, and pay is calculated from the scan data. Each cleaner works 6 shifts a week, so there are 1,176 scheduled shifts every week. The director gives the head of HR two targets for the first eight weeks, from 1 March to 24 April: from week four onwards, every week must have at least 95% of scheduled shifts fully clocked on the new system, meaning both a start and a finish scan; and the share of cleaners leaving in March and April must be no higher than in the same two months of 2026. The report goes to the director before 10 May. All figures and dates are illustrative.

  1. List the impacted groups and set anchors for the scale: On 11 January, a planning team of four, the head of HR, the chief accountant, a team leader and the person in charge of the app, writes down four groups from the organisation chart: cleaners, team leaders, payroll accountants, and the management of the 15 buildings. To score consistently, the team writes an anchor for all six points: 1 is one detail changing, familiar within a day; 2 is a few steps or a calculation changing, familiar within a week; 3 is part of the daily work changing how it is done, taking a few weeks to get used to; 4 is most of the way of working changing, requiring a new skill; 5 is the old way dropped entirely, with the new way needing a tool or skill the person does not yet have. The anchors for 1, 2 and 4, and the specific reading of 3 and 5, are choices made for this scenario. The scale measures size only; the direction of gain or loss goes in a separate column after asking
  2. Fill in yesterday and tomorrow from documents, then score a draft: From the current attendance procedure and the app's specification, the team fills in four canvases. The cleaners' canvas scores 3 on processes, because scanning at the start and end of a shift and photographing the area replace signing the book at the team leader's desk; 3 on tools, because a personal phone becomes a tool used every shift; and 2 on compensation, because pay is now calculated from scan data while the pay rate stays the same. The other seven aspects are marked unchanged or 1. The team leaders' canvas scores 3 on job roles, because confirming who came to work changes how it is done while supervising the cleaning stays the same; the accountants' canvas scores 4 on processes, because most of their working week moves from data entry to reconciliation in software; the building management canvas scores 2 on processes
  3. Take the draft to each group and ask about gain or loss: From 18 to 29 January, the team holds sessions at five buildings with 41 cleaners and all 15 team leaders, phones the management of all 15 buildings, then sends a short questionnaire to all 196 cleaners. Three things come out. First, 38 people have no smartphone, or a phone too old to install the app; for them, the old way is dropped and the new way needs a tool they do not have, which is a 5 on tools by the anchors set, so the cleaners' canvas is split in two, 158 people with a suitable phone and 38 without. Second, the compensation row, the lowest-scoring of the three rows scored, is the row both groups of cleaners mark as a loss: they fear that forgetting to scan once will cost them a shift's pay, something the paper book never did because the team leader could sign for a missed entry. Third, 11 of the 15 team leaders say signing the book is how they confirm who came to work and who swapped shifts with whom, and dropping it means losing almost the only authority they have over their team; less paperwork at the end of the week is the gain. The accountants mark it as a gain: two days of data entry a week shrink to a few hours of reconciliation. The team agrees not to add up the scores on a canvas into a total, and to read row by row
  4. Check for other changes in the same period, group by group: HR draws up a list by group and finds that in March, cleaners in all 15 buildings are also due for a switch of cleaning-chemical supplier, with new dilution rules and new equipment, which is one more change to the processes and tools of the very same group. The director approves moving the chemical switch to June
  5. Recheck the low-scoring rows before only informing: The team intends merely to inform building management, a group scoring 2 that describes itself as gaining, since it will receive photos instead of signing a sheet after every shift. Rereading the contracts shows that 2 of the 15 buildings require the inspection sheet to carry management's signature. At those two buildings, the paper sheet is kept alongside the photos until both sides sign a new contract annex
  6. Change the design around the rows marked as a loss, and only then plan the training: For the 38 people without a suitable phone, each building gets a shared clock-in tablet in the team's rest room, 15 tablets in all, and people using the shared tablet clock in with their staff card; the head of administration is responsible for buying and installing them by 22 February. For the fear of losing pay, the company writes into the notice sent to every cleaner: for the first eight weeks, team leaders keep signing the book, pay is calculated from the paper book whenever the book and the app disagree, and nobody loses pay for a forgotten scan. For team leaders, the app gains a permission: approving forgotten scans and shift swaps within their team, so confirming who came to work still goes through them, only moved from the book to a phone. After those three changes, each team gets a 30-minute session at its building at the start of a shift, led by the team leader together with the person in charge of the app
  7. Revise the canvas after the first week: Week one reaches only 88.0%, 141 shifts short. Filtering the shifts with a missing scan by building, the team finds that 104 of the 141 are evening shifts at 3 buildings where the rest room, the QR code and the tablet sit in the basement, out of signal; those three buildings have 19 people on evening shifts, or 114 scheduled shifts a week. During week two, the QR codes and tablets at those three buildings move up to the ground-floor lobby, and the canvas gains a row under location for evening staff at those three buildings, as in the table below
  8. Measure the eight weeks by exactly the definition set, then send the report: From week four onwards, every week is above 95%, the lowest being 95.6% in week four itself, as the second table shows. Across all eight weeks, the total is 8,888 of 9,408 scheduled shifts, about 94.5%, below 95% because of the first three weeks; the target applied only from week four. In March and April 2027, 7 of 196 cleaners left, about 3.6%, against 9 of 190 in the same two months of 2026, about 4.7%; both rates divide by the number of cleaners employed on 1 March of that year. The number of buildings, the schedule of 6 shifts a week and the contractual pay rates are the same in both years. The report goes to the director on 7 May

The targets are met: from week four onwards, the fully clocked rate is above 95% every week, the lowest being 95.6%; the share of cleaners leaving over the two months is 3.6%, no higher than 4.7% in 2026; and the report goes out on 7 May, ahead of the 10 May deadline. The result rests on three conditions: the 15 tablets were installed before launch; the company could carry eight weeks of running in parallel, meaning team leaders still signed the book and accountants still checked two sources; and the chemical switch could be moved to June. The no-deduction commitment grew out of the compensation row, the row scored 2; reading the canvas by score, that row would have been at the bottom of the list of things to deal with. From week nine, when pay is calculated from the app with no book to check against, the fear of losing a shift's pay may return, and both cleaners' canvases need filling in again at that point. The canvas's own contribution cannot be separated out: the tablets, the no-deduction commitment, the team leaders' approval permission and the postponed chemical switch all came in at the same time, and 2027 differs from 2026 in other ways too. The gap between 7 and 9 leavers is a small difference over a two-month period, not enough to say the company has reduced turnover; it shows only that the "no higher" target was met. And the canvas changed during the rollout itself, in the location row for evening shifts, because of a detail that no session in January asked about.

Summary

Key Point: Change impact assessment translates an organisation-level change into yesterday and tomorrow for each group across ten aspects of their work, then scores the size from 0 to 5, so the question of who is changed most, and where, gets an answer that can be written down and compared before the change begins. The most counterintuitive point is that the scale has no direction: a 5 can be a gain or a loss, while in Oreg's study of 177 employees, what people believed they would gain or lose went with how they felt about and evaluated the change, and information judged more timely and useful went with higher behavioural and cognitive resistance, so a high score does not yet show that more communication is needed. Within the scope of this article's search, no study was found that measures the tool itself, and the most detailed material on it comes from a company that trains and consults on this very method. How to use it: divide groups by how their work changes, write yesterday and tomorrow before scoring, ask the group itself what it gains or loses, including job security, standing and interest in the work, lay every change in the same period side by side group by group, and use the rows marked as a loss to change the design before thinking about saying more.
