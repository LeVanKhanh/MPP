5S

Everyone remembers it as a spring clean, and that is exactly why the fifth step is usually the first to die

Published: August 4, 2026

Definition

5S is a five-step method for organising a workplace, named after five Japanese words that all begin with S: seiri, seiton, seiso, seiketsu, shitsuke. In English they are usually rendered as sort, set in order, shine, standardise and sustain. Put plainly: throw out what you do not use, give everything that remains a fixed home, clean, lock that state in as the standard, then keep the standard from drifting. In a repair shop that has done 5S, the 17mm spanner is not "somewhere in the toolbox"; it sits in a cut-out shaped like itself, which means an empty outline on the board is an alarm all by itself.

That empty outline is the real mechanism of 5S. The aim of the method is not a clean workplace but a workplace where anything out of place announces itself. Cleanliness belongs to it as an instrument rather than a destination: when the floor under a machine is wiped every shift, a fresh oil stain becomes information instead of one smear among many, which makes the wipe-down an inspection. When every item has a home, a missing item is visible without anyone taking inventory. Hiroyuki Hirano, whose five-step presentation became the standard version in the West, called his book 5 Pillars of the Visual Workplace, and the title says the destination out loud: a workplace where looking is enough to know what is wrong. [1]

One detail in the method's history deserves more attention than it gets: in Toyota's own traditional presentation, there are only four S's. [2] The Lean Enterprise Institute records the same thing and gives the reason the fifth was left out, namely that shitsuke becomes redundant under Toyota's daily, weekly and monthly audits of standardised work. [3] In other words, at Toyota the sustaining was already built into how the place was managed and did not need to be a step of its own. Why the fifth S was added once the method travelled outside Toyota is better read as an interpretation than as settled fact: Art Smalley himself notes that the history of 4S and 5S is documented less cleanly than many other practices in the Toyota system. [2]

The two people who carried 5S to the rest of the world did not even translate the fifth word the same way. Takashi Osada called it discipline; [4] Hirano called it sustain. The gap between those two words is the gap between a quality a person is supposed to have and a job somebody has to keep doing. Treat it as conscientiousness and the organisation ends up exhorting people to try harder; treat it as sustaining work and the organisation has to design ownership and a cadence of checks. Most failed 5S programmes fit neatly inside that gap.

Examples

The method shows up at three different levels: a situation you can picture, a study that managed to measure it, and an accident that shows what a small object in the wrong place can cost.

Forty Minutes of Repair, Twenty-Five Minutes of Searching

Picture a small workshop and a machine that stops at nine in the morning. The fitter knows exactly what needs replacing and how; that part takes fifteen minutes. But the spare bearing is in one of four cabinets, the star-head screwdriver was borrowed yesterday and never came back, and somebody is fairly sure the strip-down instructions were printed out, just not where they ended up. The machine runs again at nine forty. In the month-end report the incident is logged as forty minutes of downtime from a bearing failure. No line records that twenty-five of those minutes were spent looking for things, because nobody measures that, and because looking for things has never counted as an incident.

One Laboratory, Non-Conformity Down By Nearly Seven Tenths

In a 2024 systematic review of 5S in healthcare, Kanabar and colleagues summarise a result from Dogan and colleagues at a clinical laboratory: after 5S was implemented, the laboratory's non-conformity score fell by 69.7%. [5] A non-conformity score is measured and audited by a third party rather than being staff opinion about whether the place feels tidier, which makes it considerably better than a before-and-after photograph.

It is still a before-and-after measurement at a single site. There is no comparison group, and this piece could not trace the sample size, the number of measurements or the confidence interval behind the original figure. A drop that large can still be shaped by a change in how the scoring was done, by the effect of being watched, by other things that changed in the laboratory at the same time, or simply by the fact that a metric sitting at an unusually bad level tends to look better at the next reading anyway. The number shows something happened and is worth noticing; it is not enough to credit the whole change to 5S.

A Strip of Metal on Runway 26 Right

On 25 July 2000, Air France Concorde flight AFR 4590 ran over a strip of metal on runway 26 right at Roissy Charles de Gaulle. The strip cut tyre number 2 on the left landing gear; debris from the tyre ruptured fuel tank 5, fuel leaked and caught fire. The aircraft got airborne but could neither climb nor accelerate, and came down on a hotel, killing nine crew, one hundred passengers and four people on the ground. The French air accident investigation bureau established that the strip had fallen from a Continental Airlines DC-10 as a result of maintenance work not carried out to the standard of the trade, and recommended the rapid implementation of debris prevention programmes at aerodromes, covering every organisation and every person working on the movement area. [6]

The limits of this example need stating, because it is easy to read too much into it. FOD management, foreign object debris management, is a dedicated aviation safety system built from four parts, prevention, detection, removal and evaluation, [7] not another version of 5S, and there is no basis for claiming a 5S programme would have prevented this accident. What it does show is one thing only: when an industry is forced to write down rules for controlling loose objects and tools across a working surface, what it writes overlaps almost entirely with the first three S's.

Some Research on 5S

The most striking thing about the review below is not what any individual study found, but how few studies there were to pool.

The study: In 2024, Kanabar and colleagues published a systematic review in Cureus on the impact and challenges of implementing 5S in healthcare settings. They searched the literature for every study meeting their inclusion criteria, across hospitals, health centres and clinical laboratories. [5]

Findings: Six studies qualified. Those six used different designs, cross-sectional observation, qualitative work, educational intervention and survey, across six different countries, and differed enough that the authors could not pool the numbers into a meta-analysis. Individual results were broadly positive, with the clearest gains in the first two steps. The authors record the limitations themselves: small samples, limited generalisability, almost no cost-effectiveness data, and publication bias, meaning studies that found something are likelier to appear in print than studies that found nothing. One finding recurs across several of them: sustaining is the hardest step to hold, and most studies did not run long enough to say whether it held at all.

What it shows: Six is this review's number, within healthcare, under its own criteria and its own set of databases. It is not an inventory of all research on 5S, and it says nothing about how much work exists in manufacturing, logistics or office administration. What it does show, kept strictly inside that boundary: evidence solid enough to pool and generalise in healthcare is still thin, and the review cannot answer how well 5S works against doing nothing, how long the effect lasts, or whether it is worth the money. For a method taught worldwide for more than thirty years, an application area that yields only six poolable studies is worth noticing.

The counter-case: The argument for 5S is a good deal stronger than the evidence table makes it look. Thin evidence here is partly a measurement problem rather than a verdict: 5S is almost never deployed on its own, so isolating its contribution demands study designs that run longer and cost far more than the way 5S actually gets rolled out. On top of that, the direct cost of 5S is far below most transformation programmes: no licence, no expensive equipment, no obligatory consultant. The evidence threshold for trying it across one small area can reasonably be lower as well. And the strongest strand: the real output of 5S may not be the metrics at all but a team's capacity to notice deviation, something every improvement method that comes afterwards quietly assumes is already there. Measuring it as an intervention and then complaining the gains are modest may be answering the wrong question.

All three strands need a caveat. Hard to measure does not mean impossible to measure: you can roll out area by area and compare the places that have been done against the ones still waiting, set matched units side by side, or track the same metric at several points before and after the change. None of those requires deliberately leaving somewhere in a mess. A low direct cost does not mean a failed attempt costs nothing either: a going-through-the-motions 5S programme still eats the team's time, still burns money on labels, shadow boards and re-layout sessions, and burns the most expensive thing of all, the feeling that improvement has already happened. Hardest to test is the argument about capacity, and it is precisely the sentence that lets a dying programme defend itself indefinitely: whenever the numbers refuse to move, one can always say the thing being built is capacity rather than results. What separates the two cases is not the audit score but whether that team has lately caught a problem it used to miss.

Why this is useful: It changes the question you should ask when somebody shows off 5S results. A before-and-after photograph only shows the area looked different on two occasions; it says nothing about what the change produced or how long it lasted, and every workplace looks tidier on precisely the day someone photographs it. Three questions are worth asking instead: apart from the 5S score, which metric moved, when was it measured again after rollout, and who actually threw anything away. The third is the hardest to answer, because a 5S programme that discarded nothing has almost certainly stopped at rearranging.

How to Apply It

5S works by making the abnormal visible, so the implementations that hold up are the ones that keep that looking inside the working day instead of splitting it off into an inspection process running alongside.

  • Do the first S for real, and measure it by what left the building: seiri is the only step that forces a decision, and the one most easily skipped, because throwing something out means somebody carries the blame if it turns out to be needed later; if nothing leaves the area after a 5S round, what happened was rearranging, not sorting
  • Build the check into the shift rather than staging a separate one: Toyota held the discipline while using only four S's because the checking already lived inside daily management, whereas a 5S audit with its own form, its own calendar and its own owner is the first thing postponed when an order gets urgent
  • Measure with numbers that existed before 5S did: downtime, time spent hunting for parts, errors caused by picking the wrong item, all of it was already recorded somewhere before the programme started; reuse those figures and the result means something, whereas a 5S score only measures compliance with the 5S scoresheet
  • Know in advance what it cannot fix: 5S rearranges the place where work happens, it does not touch the flow of the work itself; if the real constraint is a three-day approval step, or two departments that do not talk to each other, a tidy shop floor will not shave a single day off it, and worse, it manufactures the sense that improvement has happened and stops people looking for the actual constraint

Summary

Key Point: 5S does not stop at keeping a workplace clean. Its deeper value is establishing a standard state so that deviations, gaps and signs of trouble surface on their own, with cleanliness taking part as an inspection instrument rather than a destination. The most counter-intuitive part sits in its own history: Toyota's traditional presentation has only four S's, because sustaining already lived inside their daily audits of standardised work. The scientific evidence behind 5S is thinner than its reputation, at least in healthcare, which is where somebody has actually sat down and counted. The implementation worth trusting builds the check into the working rhythm instead of staging a parallel audit, and measures with numbers that existed before the programme began.
